How to Respond to a Negative Google Review as a Roofer

You come down off a roof at 4 p.m., check your phone, and there it is: one star on Google. The customer says your crew left nails in the yard and nobody called back. Your stomach drops, and your thumbs start typing.

Stop. What you do in the next 24 hours matters more than the review itself. Here is how to handle it without making it worse.

Why does one bad review matter so much?

Because people read them, and they read your answer too. According to Roofr's Homeowner Perspective Report (November 2025), 82% of homeowners read Google reviews before they call a roofer. BrightLocal's 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey (1,002 US adults) found that 97% of consumers read reviews for local businesses, and 41% say they always do.

Even homeowners who hear your name from a neighbor check you out. The 2024 Homeowner Roofing Survey from Roofing Contractor magazine (Clear Seas Research) found that 73% go by word of mouth or a recommendation, and 54% use search engine results. In the same survey, 95% of homeowners called online reviews important to some degree when hiring a roofer.

So the person you are really writing to is not the angry customer. It is the next homeowner, scrolling at the kitchen table, deciding whether to call you.

What should I do in the first hour?

Nothing public. Do this instead:

  1. Read the review twice, slowly.
  2. Pull the job file: contract, photos, texts, invoices, the dates of every call.
  3. Ask the crew lead what happened. Ask, don't accuse.
  4. Decide what is true, what is a misunderstanding, and what is simply wrong.

If you are angry, wait until tomorrow. A reply written at 9 p.m. in a truck usually reads like it was written at 9 p.m. in a truck.

What should my public reply say?

Keep it short, calm and written for the stranger reading it. A simple order works:

  1. Thank them. "Thanks for letting us know."
  2. Name the problem in a neutral way. "You mentioned debris left in the yard and a missed callback."
  3. Own what is true. If you dropped the ball, say so in one sentence.
  4. Take it offline. Give a direct phone number and a name.

Example:

"Thank you for the feedback, Mrs. Alvarez. You're right that you should have heard back from us sooner, and I'm sorry about that. I'd also like to make sure the yard was fully cleaned up. Please call me directly at 555-0142, or I will try you tomorrow morning. Dave, owner."

What to leave out:

  • Arguing the details. Even if you are right, a long rebuttal looks defensive.
  • Private information. No prices, addresses or contract terms in public.
  • Calling them a liar. Say "we'd like to understand what happened."
  • Offering money for a changed or deleted review. It turns a service problem into a bigger one.
  • Sarcasm. It always reads worse than you meant.

If you truly cannot find a job under that name, say so politely: "We can't find a project under this name. Please contact us so we can look into it." If the review breaks Google's rules, you can flag it. Check Google's current review policies, because you cannot assume it will be removed.

What do I do after I reply?

Call. A phone call from the owner settles more than three rounds of comments.

Listen first and don't interrupt. Then say what you can do, and do it. Send the crew back, fix the gutter, clean the yard, or explain the invoice.

If they are satisfied, you may say: "If you feel we made it right, we'd appreciate it if you'd update your review. No pressure either way." Then leave it alone. Never offer a discount or any payment in exchange for a review. Google's policy is clear that reviews should not be paid for.

And if they never update it, your calm public reply still does its job.

How do I keep one bad review from sitting on top?

You cannot delete your way out of it, and you must not write reviews yourself. Google's rules say the same: don't pay for reviews, don't write your own, and don't pick only happy customers to ask. Ask every customer the same way, every time.

The honest fix is a steady flow of real, recent reviews. BrightLocal's 2026 survey found that 85% of consumers say positive reviews make them more likely to use a business, and 74% look for reviews written in the last three months. A bad review from last spring gets pushed down by honest ones from this month.

Also put your reviews where homeowners can see them. According to the Roofing Contractor survey, 68% of roofing contractors feature online reviews on their website, and 21% do not. BrightLocal also names Google as the most used review platform, cited by 71% of consumers in 2026, so that is where your effort should go first.
