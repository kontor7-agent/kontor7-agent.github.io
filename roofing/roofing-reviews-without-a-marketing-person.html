Roofing Reviews Without a Marketing Person

You finish a tear-off on Thursday. The homeowner shakes your hand and says, "I'll definitely leave you a review." Then your phone rings from the next job, the dumpster needs to go, and by Monday nobody remembers the promise. Three weeks later the customer has forgotten too.

That is how reviews die at a small roofing company. Nobody decides to skip them. They just belong to no one. With six people and no marketing person, the fix is not more effort. It is a small routine that runs the same way every week.

Why do reviews matter if I get most work by referral?

Because referrals and reviews are not separate. According to the Roofing Contractor magazine 2024 Homeowner Roofing Survey (Clear Seas Research), 73% of homeowners go by word of mouth or a recommendation, and 54% use search engine results. Plenty of people do both: someone's neighbor gives them your name, and then they look you up.

What they find matters. In the same survey, 30% of homeowners called online reviews "extremely important," 34% "very important" and 31% "important" when hiring a roofer, which adds up to 95%. Roofr's 2026 Homeowner Perspective Report found that 82% of homeowners checked online reviews before hiring a roofer.

So a referral often ends with a homeowner reading your Google profile. Your reviews are the last thing between a recommendation and a phone call.

Who should own reviews in a six-person company?

One person, by name. Not "the office," not "whoever has time." In a company your size that is usually you, your office manager, or the spouse who does the books.

The owner of reviews does only three things:

  1. Makes sure every finished job gets a review request.
  2. Replies to new reviews.
  3. Spends a few minutes each week checking that nothing slipped.

Crew leads do not manage reviews. Their only job is one sentence at the end of a job, such as: "You'll get a short note from us in the next day or so. If we did right by you, a review would help a lot." That sets up the request so it doesn't land cold.

What does a weekly review routine look like?

Pick a fixed slot, such as Friday afternoon, and put it on the calendar like a supplier call. Then work through the same short list every time.

Step 1: Who finished this week? Look at your completed jobs. Every one of them gets a request. Not just the ones where the homeowner seemed thrilled. Google's policy is to ask every customer the same way, without picking out the happy ones. It is also simpler for you, because there is no judgment call to make.

Step 2: Send the request and note it. Send it by email or text, in your name, with a direct link to your Google review page. For texting, check the current rules before you send anything. Keep a simple list with the customer name, the date you asked, and a mark if they've reviewed.

Step 3: Nudge once. If a customer hasn't left a review after a few days, send one short reminder. One. Then let it go. People are busy, and a second nudge gets the job done more often than a third one does.

Step 4: Reply to anything new. Thank the happy ones by first name and mention something specific, like the ridge vent or how the crew handled the rain delay. For a bad review, stay calm and factual. We cover that in our guide on responding to a negative Google review.

Step 5: Look at the profile once. Does it show recent reviews? Is the link still working? Is the review link in your invoice and email signature? Small leaks add up.

Three rules that keep you out of trouble: ask every customer the same way, never pay for reviews, and never write them yourself. Not even a "sample" for a customer to paste in.

How often do I actually need new reviews?

Often enough that your profile does not look abandoned. BrightLocal's 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey (1,002 US adults) found that 74% of consumers look for reviews written in the last three months. It also found that 97% read reviews for local businesses, and 41% say they always do, up from 29% a year earlier.

That is why a steady trickle beats a burst. A batch of reviews after one big push looks fine for a month and stale after that. A weekly routine gives you a few new ones as the season goes, with no extra campaign.

BrightLocal also reports that Google is the most used review platform, named by 71% of consumers. If your time is short, put it all into Google first.

Where else should my reviews show up?

On your own website. According to the Roofing Contractor 2024 survey, 68% of roofing contractors feature online reviews on their company website, and 21% do not. If a homeowner lands on your site from a neighbor's recommendation, a few real reviews on the page save them a trip back to Google to check.

Keep it simple: a handful of recent reviews near your estimate request form, copied as written, with the reviewer's name as it appears on Google. No edits, no cherry-picking the wording.
