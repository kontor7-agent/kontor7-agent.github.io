Google Business Profile for Roofers: 5 Fields to Fix

A homeowner finds a wet spot on the bedroom ceiling after last night's storm. She doesn't know a roofer. She types "roof repair near me" into her phone and looks at what comes up. You're on a roof across town with your phone in the truck, and your Google Business Profile is the one you set up years ago and never touched.

You don't need to spend a weekend on it. Five fields do most of the work, and you can fix all of them in about 20 minutes with the profile open on your laptop.

Why does a roofer's Google profile matter?

People hear your name from a neighbor and then check you on Google. According to Roofing Contractor magazine's 2024 Homeowner Roofing Survey (Clear Seas Research), 73% of homeowners find a roofing contractor through word of mouth or a recommendation, and 54% use search engine results. Plenty of homeowners do both.

Then they read. Roofr's Homeowner Perspective Report (November 2025) found that 82% of homeowners read Google reviews before they call a roofer. BrightLocal's 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey named Google as the most used review platform, at 71% of consumers.

Your profile is the page they land on. The five fields below decide whether it looks like a working roofing company or an abandoned listing.

Which category and services should you pick?

Field 1: primary category. Choose the one that describes most of your revenue. For most of you that's "Roofing contractor." Add secondary categories only for work you really do and want more of, such as a gutter or siding category. Don't add categories for jobs you subcontract and don't want calls about.

Field 2: services. Most owners skip this list, and it's where you can be specific. Add each service in plain words, the way a homeowner would say it:

  • Roof repair
  • Roof replacement
  • Storm damage inspection
  • Leak repair
  • Flashing and chimney repair
  • Gutter installation

Add a one-line description to each. Example: "Shingle roof replacement, tear-off and install, with cleanup and magnet sweep." Write what you actually do, nothing more.

Keep your business name exactly as it appears on your truck and invoices. Stuffing keywords into the name can get a profile suspended. Check Google's current guidelines if you're unsure.

How should you set your service area and hours?

Field 3: service area. List the towns and counties where you'll really drive for an estimate. If you work from home, you can hide your street address and show only the area you serve. Don't list places two hours away just to look bigger. You'll get calls you won't want, or you'll turn people down, which looks worse than not being listed.

Field 4: hours and phone number. Roofing has a particular problem here. Your phone rings during business hours while you're on a roof, and during storm season it rings in the evening too. Set your regular hours honestly. If you take emergency tarping calls, say so in your description and make sure someone answers or the voicemail sounds like a real business. Use a number that actually reaches you or your office, and add your website link so a homeowner who doesn't want to call has somewhere to go.

Update holiday hours too. A profile that says "open" when nobody picks up costs you trust.

What photos does a roofing profile need?

Field 5: photos. Homeowners can't judge a roof from a logo. Upload real work from your own jobs:

  • A before and after of the same roof, shot from the same spot
  • Your crew in branded shirts and your trucks
  • Close-ups of finished details: flashing, ridge, valleys
  • A clean job site at the end of the day

Get permission from the homeowner before you post a recognizable house, and leave out house numbers. Phone photos are fine. Dated, sharp, honest pictures beat stock images. Add a few new ones after each good job; it takes a minute from the truck.

What should you do after the 20 minutes are up?

The fields above get you found and make you look real. After that, the profile mostly runs on reviews, and 85% of consumers say positive reviews make them more likely to use a business, according to BrightLocal's 2026 survey. Google's policies are simple: ask every customer the same way, never pay for reviews, and never write them yourself. We cover the asking itself in other guides in this section.

A quick check every few months keeps things from going stale: confirm the hours, add recent photos, and make sure the phone number still works.
