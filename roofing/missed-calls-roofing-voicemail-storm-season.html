Missed Calls in Roofing: Voicemail During Storm Season

It is the Tuesday after a hailstorm. You are on a steep pitch with a nail gun in your hand, and your phone is buzzing in the truck. By the time you climb down, there are six missed calls and two voicemails. One of them is mostly wind noise and a number you can barely make out.

Every roofer knows this day. The question is what the homeowner on the other end did while you were up there.

What does a homeowner do when they reach your voicemail?

Most of them do one of three things. They leave a message, they hang up and try the next name, or they give up for now and look for another way to reach you.

The second one is the one that costs you. A homeowner with water coming through a ceiling is not loyal to a company they have never used. They are looking for the first roofer who answers.

How did they find you in the first place? According to the 2024 Homeowner Roofing Survey (Clear Seas Research) reported by Roofing Contractor magazine, 54% of homeowners use search engine results to find a roofing contractor, and 73% go by word of mouth or a recommendation. Those two groups overlap. Someone gets your name from a neighbor, then searches for you and calls the number they find.

That call is a warm one, and voicemail is the worst possible reception for it.

Why is storm season so hard on a small crew?

Because everything happens at once. Calls bunch up in the first days after a storm. Your crew is busy doing tarps and repairs for people who already called, and you are the one who answers the phone, quotes the jobs and orders the material.

You cannot hire a receptionist for two weeks. What you can do is make sure that a missed call does not turn into a lost job. That comes down to three things: what the caller hears, how fast you call back, and whether there is a second way to reach you.

What should your voicemail greeting say?

Most greetings say "leave a message and we will get back to you." That tells the caller nothing, and in storm season it sounds like a long wait.

Try something more specific:

"This is Dan at Hartley Roofing. I'm on a roof right now and can't pick up. Please say your name, your address and what you're seeing, like a leak or missing shingles. I call everyone back between noon and 1 and again after 4. If water is coming in, say so first."

This does three things. It explains why you did not answer, it asks for the details you need to prioritize, and it gives a callback window the caller can plan around. Callers who know when to expect you are less likely to keep dialing other numbers.

Record it fresh at the start of storm season and update the times if they change. An old greeting that promises a same-day callback you cannot keep does more harm than a plain one.

In what order should you return the calls?

Pick an order before the phones start ringing, so you are not deciding while tired.

  1. Active leaks and open roofs first. Anyone who says water is getting in gets the first call. Tell them what you can do today, even if it is only a tarp.
  2. Callers with an address and a clear description second. You can look at the roof on a map before you dial, which makes the call short and useful.
  3. Garbled or incomplete messages third. Call back once. If they do not pick up, send one short text (check the current rules on texting customers) and move on.
  4. Insurance and inspection questions last, unless the caller has a deadline.

On each call, say your name and company first. Then repeat the address back, and give one next step with a time: "I can be at the house Thursday between 8 and 10 to look at it." A callback that ends with a time on the calendar is worth more than one that ends with "I'll let you know."

Is there a way to catch people who will not leave a voicemail?

Many people will not. Some do not like leaving messages, some are calling from work, and some just want to type a few lines when it is convenient. A written option on your website catches them.

The form should be short: name, address, phone, and one line about what is going on. Our guide on roofing estimate request forms covers what to ask and what to skip. Put the form link in your voicemail greeting or in a text reply, so the person you could not answer has somewhere to go.

Your Google profile matters here too. A homeowner who misses you will often check your reviews before trying again. Roofr's Homeowner Perspective Report (November 2025) found that 82% of homeowners read Google reviews before they call a roofer. If your reviews are thin or out of date, a missed call has a harder time turning into a second attempt.

The 2024 Roofing Contractor survey also found that 30% of homeowners call online reviews "extremely important," 34% "very important" and 31% "important" when hiring a roofer. That adds up to 95%. A good review history will not answer the phone for you, but it helps a caller decide to wait for your callback.

What is a simple routine you can keep?

Storm season is not the time for a complicated system. Keep it to four habits:

  • A greeting that names a callback time and asks for the address.
  • Two fixed callback windows a day, written on the dash of the truck.
  • One short text or email with your form link for anyone you could not reach.
  • A review request after each finished job, asked the same way every time, so your Google profile keeps growing.

The last habit is easy to drop when you are busy. Google's policies say you should ask every customer the same way, never pay for reviews, and never write them yourself.
