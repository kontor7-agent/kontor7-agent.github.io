Roofing Estimate Request Form: What to Ask and Skip

You are on a ridge at 2 p.m. and your phone buzzes. By the time you climb down, the caller has hung up and left no message. Or someone filled out the contact form on your site last Tuesday with "need a roof quote" and nothing else. No address, a phone number with a typo, and no hint whether it is a leak or a full tear-off. You spend the evening playing phone tag.

Both problems start with the form. Here is how to build an estimate request form that gets you a lead you can actually call, without scaring the homeowner off.

Why does the estimate form matter so much?

According to Roofing Contractor magazine's 2024 Homeowner Roofing Survey, 54% of homeowners use search engine results to find a roofing contractor, and 73% go by word of mouth or a recommendation. Either way, many of them land on your website to take the next step. The form is where a referral or a search click turns into a conversation, or quietly dies.

Roofr's 2026 Homeowner Perspective Report found that 82% of homeowners checked online reviews before hiring a roofer. So the person filling in your form has often already decided you look trustworthy. A long, nosy form can undo that in thirty seconds.

Want the four fields as a checklist? It is at the end of this guide.

What should you ask on a roofing estimate request form?

Ask only what you need to make the first call useful. Five fields do it:

  1. Name. First and last.
  2. Mobile phone number. This is the one you will actually use.
  3. Email. For the confirmation and a written estimate later.
  4. Property address. Or at least the ZIP code. You can check your service area and look at the roof on satellite imagery before you dial.
  5. What is going on? A dropdown: active leak, storm damage, repair, full replacement, inspection for a sale, not sure.

Two optional extras are worth adding: a box for "Anything we should know?" and a photo upload. Mark both clearly as optional. A photo of a stained ceiling tells you more than three paragraphs of typing.

What should you leave off the form?

A good rule: if you would not ask it at the front door, do not ask it on the form.

  • Budget. Most homeowners cannot know it yet, so you get a guess or a lowball. You will talk price after you have seen the roof.
  • Insurance company, claim number, adjuster name. Too early, and it can feel like a sales trap. Bring it up on the call if it applies.
  • Roof age, square footage, pitch, number of layers. Most people do not know these. You will measure anyway.
  • Shingle brand and color preferences. That is a conversation for the estimate visit.
  • Anything financial or personal. No date of birth, no Social Security number, no card details. Never.
  • Account creation or a password. Nobody wants a login to get a quote.
  • Hard-to-read CAPTCHA puzzles. Use a simple spam filter instead.

Every extra required field costs you some people. Keep the required ones to the five above.

What happens after the homeowner hits send?

The confirmation message is part of the form. Tell them who will call and when, and then do it. For example:

Thanks, Maria. We got your request for 214 Maple Street. Dan will call you today before 6 p.m. (or tomorrow morning if it is after hours). If water is coming in right now, call us at the number below instead.

If you plan to text people, put a plain sentence near the phone field saying so. Texting and consent rules change, so check the current rules for your state and for messaging before you set this up.

Then put a few real Google reviews right beside the form. Roofing Contractor's 2024 survey says 68% of roofing contractors feature online reviews on their website and 21% do not. The same survey found that 95% of homeowners call online reviews important, extremely, very or just important, when hiring a roofer. A homeowner who is about to type in their address wants one more reason to feel okay about it.

How do you know your form works?

Do a ten-minute test. Open your site on your own phone, with one thumb, standing in a parking lot. Can you finish the form without pinching or zooming? Does the confirmation arrive? Does the request land somewhere you will see it while you are on a job?

Then check who reads it. If the form goes to an office inbox nobody opens until Monday, a Thursday storm lead is cold by the time you call.

The same goes for the other end of the job. When the roof is done, ask every customer for a Google review the same way, never pay for reviews, and never write them yourself. That is Google's policy, and it also keeps your reviews believable. BrightLocal's 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey found that 97% of consumers read reviews for local businesses and 74% look for reviews written in the last three months. A steady trickle beats one big push.
