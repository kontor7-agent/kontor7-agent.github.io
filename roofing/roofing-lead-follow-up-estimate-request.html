Roofing Lead Follow-Up: How Fast to Reply, What to Write

You are on a ridge with a nail gun in your hand when your phone buzzes. It is an estimate request from a homeowner with a leak over the kitchen. You tell yourself you will answer at lunch. By lunch you are in the truck, then at the supplier, then it is dinner. The next morning the homeowner has already booked someone else.

This is not a marketing problem. It is a timing problem, and you can fix it with a few habits and some short messages written in advance.

How fast should I answer a roofing estimate request?

As fast as you can manage, and the same day at the latest. Someone who fills out a form is thinking about their roof right now. A day later, they are thinking about something else, or they are talking to your competitor.

I will not give you a magic number of minutes, because no number in our sources backs one up. Here is the logic instead. Homeowners rarely send a request to only one company. Whoever replies first with something useful usually gets the first real conversation, and that conversation is half the sale.

There is also a reason to be careful once they are comparing you. According to Roofing Contractor magazine's 2024 Homeowner Roofing Survey (Clear Seas Research), 73% of homeowners find a roofing contractor through word of mouth or a recommendation, and 54% use search engine results. Many of them use both. Even a referred homeowner will check you out, so your first reply is part of what they judge.

What should the first message say?

Keep it short and personal, and make it easy to answer. A first reply has four jobs:

  1. Say who you are and thank them by name.
  2. Show you read their request.
  3. Say what happens next.
  4. Ask one easy question.

Here is an example:

"Hi Maria, this is Dave from Ridgeline Roofing. Thanks for your request about the leak over your kitchen. I would like to look at it in person. Would Thursday morning or Friday afternoon work better for a quick inspection? If it is actively dripping, tell me and I will try to get there sooner."

Notice what is missing. There is no price, no sales pitch and no list of services. You are not trying to close the job in a text. You are trying to get a time on the calendar.

Avoid vague lines like "Let me know if you have questions." Offering two time options is much easier to answer than an open question.

What if I am on a roof and cannot answer?

You will miss the first window on many days. So split the job in two.

The first part is a quick acknowledgment that goes out automatically or is sent by someone in the office. It can be as simple as: "Got your request, Maria. I am on a job right now and will call you this evening between 5 and 6." That tells the homeowner a real person has seen it and when to expect a call.

The second part is the real conversation, and you must keep that promise. If you say you will call at 5, call at 5. A missed promise is worse than a slow reply.

If you have a spouse, an office person or a crew lead who can send the acknowledgment, give them the wording. If you plan to send texts, check the current rules on consent and business messaging first. Email and phone calls have rules too, so make sure you know which apply to you.

How do I follow up without nagging?

Most lost leads are not rejections. They are silence. The homeowner got busy, felt overwhelmed or forgot. A polite follow-up is normal and most people do not mind it.

A simple order that works:

  • Same day: the first reply with two time options.
  • Next day, if no answer: a short check-in. "Hi Maria, just checking that my message got through. I can still do Thursday or Friday. If you have already found someone, no problem, just let me know."
  • A few days later: one last message that leaves the door open. "I do not want to bug you, so this is my last note. If the roof is still on your mind, reply here and I will find a time."

Then stop. Giving people an easy way out makes them more likely to reply honestly, and it keeps you from becoming the contractor who will not go away.

After an inspection or an estimate, follow up again, this time about the estimate itself. Ask if they have questions about the scope or the materials. Do not discount just because they have gone quiet.

What happens after the job?

Your follow-up does not end when the last bundle of shingles is off the truck. Homeowners check you out before they call. Roofr's Homeowner Perspective Report (November 2025) found that 82% of homeowners read Google reviews before they call a roofer. BrightLocal's 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey found that 97% of consumers read reviews for local businesses, and 74% look for reviews written in the last three months.

So the same habit that wins the estimate, a quick and friendly message, also helps you get the review that wins the next one. Ask every customer the same way. Do not pick only the happy ones, never pay for reviews and never write them yourself. That is Google's policy, and it is also just the honest way to do it.
