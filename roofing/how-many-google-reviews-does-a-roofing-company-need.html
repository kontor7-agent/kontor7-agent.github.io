How Many Google Reviews Does a Roofing Company Need?

You are on a ridge when your phone buzzes. By the time you climb down, the homeowner has already moved on to the next roofer on the list. Later you look up your own Google listing and see a handful of reviews, the newest from last spring. Your crew does better work than that listing suggests.

The question every owner asks at that point is simple: how many reviews is enough?

Is there a magic number of Google reviews?

No. You will see articles claiming a specific count that "unlocks" more calls, but none of the research we cite here gives one, and we are not going to make one up.

What the research does show is how many homeowners look. According to Roofr's Homeowner Perspective Report (November 2025), 82% of homeowners read Google reviews before they call a roofer. So your reviews get read before most calls happen, whether you have 8 or 80.

The better question is not "how many do I need" but "how do I look next to the other two or three roofers this homeowner is comparing me with?"

Why do homeowners read reviews before they call?

Roofing is a big, stressful purchase, and people want proof before they let a stranger onto their house.

The 2024 Homeowner Roofing Survey in Roofing Contractor magazine (fielded by Clear Seas Research in fall 2023) found that 54% of homeowners use search engine results to find a roofing contractor. Word of mouth or a recommendation is still bigger at 73%. Those two often work together: a neighbor gives your name, and the homeowner then searches you to check.

The same survey asked how important online reviews are when hiring a roofer. 30% said extremely important, 34% very important and 31% important. That adds up to 95%. Almost nobody shrugs reviews off.

BrightLocal's 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey (1,002 US adults) points the same way. 97% of consumers read reviews for local businesses, and 41% say they always do, up from 29% a year earlier. Google was named as the most used review platform by 71%.

What matters more than the raw count?

A big number with nothing recent behind it can work against you. BrightLocal's 2026 survey found that 74% of consumers look for reviews written in the last three months. A listing whose newest review is a year old raises a quiet question: are they still in business, and still doing good work?

The same survey says 85% of consumers are more likely to use a business after reading positive reviews. So three things matter together:

  • Enough reviews that your rating is not based on a couple of people.
  • Recent reviews so it is clear you are working now.
  • Specific reviews that mention the job, such as a leak repair after a storm or a full tear-off, plus the crew and how you communicated.

You cannot script the third one. But you can make it more likely by asking in the right way, which we cover below.

How many reviews should you aim for?

Use a practical yardstick instead of a magic number.

  1. Search for the kind of job you want, in the towns you serve, the way a homeowner would. For example: "roof repair near me" or "roof replacement [your town]".
  2. Open the top few roofers and note their review counts, ratings and the date of the newest review.
  3. Your goal is to be at least in the same range as the best of them, with newer reviews than most.

Then keep going. Think of it as a habit, not a project with a finish line. If your competitors have far more reviews, closing the gap takes time, but each finished job is a chance to add one. If you are already ahead on count, the thing to protect is recency.

Two more places to use what you collect. The Roofing Contractor survey found that 68% of roofing contractors feature online reviews on their website and 21% do not. Putting yours on your site is an easy way to stand out from that 21%.

How do you get more reviews without a big project?

The owners who build up reviews are rarely the ones with a clever system. They are the ones who ask every time.

Ask at the right moment. The best time is right after the final walk-around, when the customer is relieved and the yard is clean. Say it in person first: "If you are happy with the roof, would you leave us a short Google review? I will text or email you the link so it takes a minute."

Send the link the same day. A promise to do it later is where most reviews die. Send the link while the job is still fresh.

Send one reminder, then stop. Many happy customers simply forget. One friendly nudge a few days later is fine. More than that feels like pressure.

Follow Google's rules. Ask every customer the same way, not just the ones you expect to be happy. Never pay for reviews, and never write them yourself. Rules about texting and review requests can change, so check the current rules for your state before you automate anything.

Reply to every review. A short thank-you shows future customers that someone is paying attention.
