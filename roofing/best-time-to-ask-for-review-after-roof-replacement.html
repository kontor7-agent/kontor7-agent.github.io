Best Time to Ask for a Roofing Review After the Job

You finish a tear-off on a Friday, the crew sweeps the yard, and the homeowner shakes your hand and says, "I'll definitely leave you a review." Three weeks later there is nothing on your Google profile. The customer wasn't lying. They just got busy, and you never gave them an easy next step.

Timing is most of the problem. Ask too early and the homeowner hasn't seen the finished work. Ask too late and the job is a fading memory. Here is how to find the window in between.

Why does timing matter for roofing reviews?

Reviews decide a lot of jobs. According to Roofr's Homeowner Perspective Report (November 2025), 82% of homeowners read Google reviews before they call a roofer. The 2024 Homeowner Roofing Survey from Roofing Contractor magazine (Clear Seas Research) found that 30% call online reviews "extremely important," 34% "very important" and 31% "important" when hiring a roofer. That adds up to 95%.

Recency counts too. BrightLocal's 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey found that 74% of consumers look for reviews written in the last three months. A steady trickle of reviews after each job helps you more than a pile you collected in one burst.

You can't get a steady trickle without a steady habit, and the habit starts with knowing when to ask.

When is the best moment to ask after a roof replacement?

The best moment is when the customer has seen the finished roof and feels relief. For a replacement, that usually means:

  1. The final walkthrough or cleanup is done. The roof looks good, the magnet sweep is finished, and the yard is clean. This is when the homeowner is happiest.
  2. The customer has had a short time to look at it. A day or two later, after they've seen it from the street in daylight and maybe after a rain, is a good time for the written request.
  3. Before the final invoice turns into a chore. If the last thing they remember is a payment problem, the review request will suffer. Settle the paperwork first, then ask.

Don't ask on the roof or while the crew is still loading the trailer. Do mention it in person, though. A short heads-up on-site makes the written request feel expected instead of random.

Is it different for a repair than for a full replacement?

Yes, a little. A replacement is a big, visible project, and homeowners often want to talk about it. A repair is smaller, and the proof comes later.

For a leak repair, the real test is the next rainstorm. If you ask the day you patch it, you're asking for a review before the customer knows whether it worked. Waiting until the first decent rain has passed, or until you've checked in and heard that the ceiling stayed dry, is usually the better call.

For small repairs like flashing, a few shingles or a vent boot, the customer's relief is immediate. Ask within a day or so, while they still remember who showed up and how clean you left the place.

If you do both kinds of jobs, write down your own rule for each so your office manager or crew lead doesn't have to guess.

What should you say, and in what order?

Keep it to two steps: a spoken heads-up, then a written request with a direct link.

On site, at the walkthrough:

"If you're happy with how it turned out, I'll send you a short note tomorrow with a link to leave a Google review. It helps a small company like ours more than you'd think."

The written request, a day or two later:

"Hi Mark, this is Dan from [Company]. Thanks again for trusting us with your roof. If you have a minute, a Google review would mean a lot to us: [link]. Whatever you write, honest feedback is welcome."

A few rules keep you on the right side of Google's policies:

  • Ask every customer the same way. Don't pick out only the ones you think are happy.
  • Never pay for reviews or offer a gift in exchange.
  • Never write reviews yourself, or ask someone else to write them for you.

Google is the most-used review platform, named by 71% of consumers in BrightLocal's 2026 survey, so make that link the main one. If you send requests by text, check the current rules for messaging customers first. E-mail is the simpler place to start.

When should you send a reminder, and when should you stop?

Most people who mean to leave a review simply forget. One gentle reminder a few days after the first request is fair. Something like:

"Hi Mark, just a quick reminder in case the link got buried. No pressure at all. If you'd rather tell us something privately, just reply to this message."

Then stop. Two messages is plenty. A third starts to feel like pressure, and a pushy request can cost you more goodwill than a missing review.

If a customer replies with a complaint, answer it by phone first. Fix what you can. Don't hide the request from unhappy customers or only send it to the happy ones, because that breaks the same-way-for-everyone rule.

How do you make this happen on every job?

The hard part isn't knowing the right moment. It's remembering it when you're juggling three crews, supplier calls and next week's estimates. Pick one trigger, such as "job marked complete," and one person who owns the request. If it depends on you remembering at the end of a long day, it will slip.
