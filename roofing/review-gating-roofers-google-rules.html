Review Gating for Roofers: Why Ask Every Customer

You finish a re-roof on a Friday. The homeowner shakes your hand, says it looks great, and you think: this one is a five-star review. Then you remember the job two weeks ago, where a gutter got dented and the customer was short with your crew. You skip that one. You only send the request to the people you are sure about.

It feels like common sense. It is also called review gating, and Google's rules are against it.

What is review gating, and why does it matter to a roofer?

Review gating is any system that filters who gets asked for a review based on how happy they are. Some versions are obvious: you only text the customers you think were pleased. Others are built into software, like a first question that asks "How was your experience?" and sends happy people to Google while unhappy ones go to a private feedback form.

Google's review policy points the other way. The short version for a small roofing company:

  • Ask every customer the same way. Do not pick out the happy ones.
  • Never pay for reviews.
  • Never write reviews yourself, or have your office staff write them.

Policies change, so read Google's current review rules for business profiles before you set up your process. The principle is simple, though: the reviews on your profile should reflect all of your customers, not a hand-picked group.

Why is it risky to only ask the happy ones?

The first risk is practical. Reviews that Google considers manipulated can be removed, and a profile that breaks the rules can get into trouble. After you have spent months collecting reviews, losing them hurts.

The second risk is trust. Homeowners are careful about this. According to the 2024 Homeowner Roofing Survey in Roofing Contractor magazine (fielded by Clear Seas Research), 30% of homeowners call online reviews "extremely important" when hiring a roofer, 34% say "very important" and 31% say "important." That adds up to 95%. A roof is a big purchase, and people read closely.

They also read on Google. Roofr's Homeowner Perspective Report from November 2025 found that 82% of homeowners read Google reviews before they call a roofer. And BrightLocal's 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey, covering 1,002 US adults, named Google the most used review platform, at 71% of consumers.

A page of nothing but flawless five-star ratings can look odd to a careful reader. A mix with a few honest, less-than-perfect reviews, and your calm replies to them, often looks more believable than a perfect record.

Will asking everyone bring in bad reviews?

Sometimes, yes. Here is the part that is easy to miss: you already have unhappy customers. Skipping them does not make them disappear. It only means you never hear from them in a way you can respond to, and they may post a review anyway, without being asked.

BrightLocal's 2026 survey found that 85% of consumers say positive reviews make them more likely to use a business. That is the reason to ask everyone. If most of your jobs go well, asking every customer lets that majority show up in your rating, instead of the few people who felt strongly enough to write on their own.

A less-than-perfect review is also a chance. A calm, specific reply that says what you will do about the problem shows the next homeowner how you act when something goes wrong. We have a separate guide on how to respond to a negative Google review as a roofer.

How do I ask every customer without it feeling awkward?

Make it a routine, not a decision. If every finished job gets the same request, you never have to judge who deserves one.

  1. Same trigger every time. For example: the final invoice is sent or the final payment arrives. Do not wait to see how the customer reacts.
  2. Same message every time. Keep it short and in your name. For example: "Hi Mark, thanks for trusting us with your roof. If you have a minute, a Google review helps other homeowners find us and tells us how we did. Here is the link."
  3. One reminder, then stop. One friendly nudge a few days later is enough. Do not chase anyone.
  4. Do not steer the answer. Skip lines like "let us know first if you are not 5-star." Do not offer discounts or gifts in return for a review. That counts as paying.
  5. Keep a private line open, openly. You can say "If anything was not right, call me directly at this number." That is fine as long as the Google link goes to everyone, not only to the people who say they were happy.

If you send requests by text, check the current rules on texting customers. If you use e-mail, keep it plain and sent from a real person at your company.

What if a customer is unhappy and I know it?

Call them first, as you would anyway. Fix what you can. Then send the same request you send everybody. You are not hiding the problem, and you are not pressuring anyone. If they leave a critical review after you made an honest effort, that is a fair record, and your reply will show how you handle it.
