Interviewed Last Week, No Reply? The Launderette Table

2026-10-24

The dryer drum turns behind a round glass door. Your jeans, one sock, a towel. Somewhere in there is the sweater you wore to the interview.

It is Thursday, 11:20 in the morning. You are sitting on the plastic chair next to the folding table, phone in your hand. You have refreshed your inbox four times since you sat down. Nothing.

The interview was last Wednesday. It went well, you think. The hiring manager laughed at your story about the warehouse project. She said, "We'll be in touch early next week."

It is not early next week anymore.

The email you keep writing in your head

You open a blank message. Hi Dana, I just wanted to check in on... You delete it. Too needy.

Hi Dana, I hope you're well. Following up on... Delete. Too stiff.

Hi Dana, just bumping this up in case it got buried. Delete. Buried? You make it sound like her fault.

The dryer hums. A woman across the room folds a fitted sheet with the calm of someone who has done it a thousand times. You envy her a little.

Here is what nobody says out loud about waiting after an interview. It is not the silence itself. It is that your whole week quietly arranges itself around one inbox. You say no to a Saturday plan "just in case." You flinch when your phone buzzes at the supermarket. You reread the job ad and wonder which sentence you got wrong.

And underneath, a smaller worry: If I write to her, will I look desperate? If I don't, will she think I lost interest?

The other thing you can't quite answer

Then a second thought arrives, and it is worse.

You also applied to a company last month. A different one. You remember a recruiter's name, maybe. Something about a logistics firm? Or was that the one with the green logo? You sent a version of your CV, but which version? Which cover letter?

If an email landed right now saying "Can we talk tomorrow at ten?", could you answer without bluffing?

You scroll through your sent folder. Twelve applications, scattered over six weeks, hiding behind subject lines like "Application" and "Re: Application." Two interviews. One rejection you only half remember. One company that never answered at all.

You realise the follow-up email isn't really what is bothering you. What bothers you is that you can't see the whole picture. You are holding it all in your head, and your head is tired.

What the folding table taught you

The woman with the fitted sheet is finished. She stacks her laundry in neat piles by type: towels, shirts, the rest. She doesn't think about it. It just sorts itself, because she sorted it when it was small.

You look at your own damp pile in the basket and almost laugh. There is a lesson hiding in there, and it is a small one.

Waiting is hard. But waiting without knowing where things stand is harder. A week of silence after an interview is ordinary. It often means nothing more than a busy team, a holiday, a decision waiting on someone's signature. You know that, in the reasonable part of your mind. The reasonable part just can't speak over the noise when you don't have the details in front of you.

So you stop typing. You do something different. You open a plain note and write down what you know: the company, the date of the interview, what she said about timing, the name of the person who asked about the warehouse project. Then the same for the logistics firm. Then the others.

It takes twelve minutes. The knot in your stomach is, to your surprise, a size smaller.

A week passed, no answer yet, and a polite nudge is perfectly normal. Seen next to a clear list, that email suddenly writes itself in three calm sentences. You thank her for her time, mention one thing you enjoyed from the conversation, ask if there is anything else she needs from you. It doesn't sound needy. It sounds like someone who knows what she's doing.

You send it. The dryer clicks off.

A different kind of Monday

Picture the Monday after that, a few weeks on.

You make coffee. You open one page, not twelve tabs. You see at a glance: these two are waiting for you to follow up today, this one has an interview on Thursday, this one is closed, and that's fine. You don't feel behind. You feel like someone with a plan.

Before Thursday's interview you don't scramble through old emails to remember what you promised. Your own stories about the warehouse project, the difficult colleague, the deadline that slipped are already sitting there, ready to be said out loud. You go in steadier. Whether you get the job or not, you leave knowing you were prepared.

And your phone buzzing at the supermarket is just your phone buzzing.

Nothing about the market changes. Hiring managers still take their time. But the part that was yours to carry, the remembering, the guessing, the dread of the blank message, gets lighter.
