Client Asking for a Discount? How to Reply Without Losing Them

2026-10-14

It is Monday, 9:12 am. You are standing at the kitchen counter with a coffee that is still too hot to drink.

Your laptop is open next to the bread bin. One new email. The subject line is friendly: Quick question about the quote.

You already know what it says before you open it.

"We really like the proposal. Is there any flexibility on the price? Our budget is a bit tighter than expected."

Your chest does a small, familiar drop.

The two answers in your head

There are always two of them, and they arrive at the same time.

The first one is quick and generous. Sure, no problem, I can do 15% off. You can almost feel the relief of the conversation ending. The client is happy. The tension is gone.

The second one is quieter and more stubborn. That is my price. Take it or leave it. It sounds firm, but you would never actually send it. It feels like slamming a door on someone you would like to keep.

So you do what you always do. You close the laptop lid halfway and go and unload the dishwasher.

The truth is, you are not afraid of the client. You are afraid of the silence after you say no. You are afraid of the next email being short. Of the project quietly going to someone cheaper, and of you sitting there thinking you should have just said yes.

What a quick yes quietly costs

Nobody talks about this part.

A discount you give in a hurry does not feel like a big thing on the day. It is one line in one email. But you will do the same work for it. You will sit in the same calls, answer the same messages, and fix the same last-minute things.

And somewhere around week three, a small resentment starts. You do not say it out loud. You just notice that you are slightly less patient with this client than with the others. You notice that you are doing good work for a rate you would not have chosen.

The discount is never just the discount. It is also the tiredness that comes with it.

The reply you write for the third time

You sit back down and start typing.

Hi Jonas, thanks so much for coming back to me. I completely understand that budgets

Delete.

Hi Jonas, I'm afraid my rates are quite fixed, but I could maybe

Delete.

The cursor blinks. The coffee goes cold. It is now 9:40, and you have written nothing that you would be proud to send.

Here is what is going on. You are trying to do three things at once: stay warm, stay firm, and stay hired. Doing all three in one email, on a Monday morning, with your heart rate up, is hard for anyone. It does not mean you are bad at business. It means you are a person who wants this to go well.

The moment it clicked

Animated by pure frustration, you remember something a friend said once about her own work. She is a copywriter. She said she stopped treating every awkward email as a fresh problem.

"It is not new," she said. "Somebody has asked for a discount before. I have just never had the words ready."

That was it. The situation is not new. Only the words are missing.

A discount request is one of the most ordinary moments in working for yourself. Clients ask because asking is free. Many of them are not testing you at all. They are just checking whether there is a door.

And you are allowed to answer in a calm voice. You can thank them. You can say what the price includes. You can offer a smaller scope instead of a smaller rate. You can hold your number kindly and still leave the relationship warm.

You just need the first sentence to already exist, so you are not starting from a blank screen and a racing pulse.

The same Monday, a little differently

Imagine the same email arriving. Same subject line, same drop in your chest.

But this time you have been here before, at least on paper. You open a page where the awkward replies are already drafted: warm, clear, a few tone options. You pick the one that sounds like you on a good day, change the client's name, adjust a detail about the project, and read it once out loud.

It sounds steady. It sounds like someone who runs a real business.

You press send at 9:25, finish the coffee while it is still warm, and open the actual work you were meant to be doing.

Maybe Jonas says yes. Maybe he asks for a smaller package. Maybe he goes elsewhere, and that is allowed too. Either way, you did not panic, you did not give away a week of your income, and you did not spend your morning arguing with yourself.

That is the quiet difference. It is not winning every negotiation. It is not carrying each one around in your body all day.
