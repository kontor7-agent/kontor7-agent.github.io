New Freelance Client, No Idea How to Start? The Egg Queue

2026-10-17

It is Saturday, 10:20, and you are third in line at the egg stall.

The woman in front of you is debating between a half dozen and a dozen. The rain taps on the canvas roof. Your phone buzzes in your coat pocket, and you pull it out with the cold hand that isn't holding the tote bag.

"Great news, we'd love to go ahead! When can we start?"

It is the client you have been waiting on for two weeks. The one who asked for a quote, went quiet, and came back. The one whose fee would cover the whole autumn.

You should feel happy. For about four seconds, you do.

Then the second thought arrives, the one you rarely say out loud: Now what, exactly?

The yes that comes with a fog

You have done this before. You know the work. What you don't have is a beginning.

You are not sure what you need from them first. Whether the scope you described on the call is the same scope they remember. Who actually signs off. Whether the logins, the brand files and the old documents will arrive by Monday or trickle in over three weeks.

So your thumbs hover over the screen, and a familiar scene plays in your head. You write something cheerful and vague. "Wonderful! Let's kick things off next week." Then the project begins the way too many of yours have begun: with a scatter of emails, a call where nobody quite agrees what was promised, and a quiet sense that you are making it up as you go.

"Making it up" is the part that stings. Not because you're bad at it. Because you know clients can feel it.

What the fog quietly costs

"Egg lady, next!" The stallholder waves you forward. You order a dozen without thinking and hold the phone against your chest like something that might spill.

Walking home under your umbrella, you do the quiet maths you never put in an invoice.

There was the project where the first week was spent chasing a password. The one where "just a small change" in week three turned out to be a whole second deliverable, because nobody had written down what the first one was. The one where you started work without a clear contact person, and three people gave you three different opinions on the final day.

None of those were disasters. That is the trouble. They were just small leaks. An unpaid hour here, a tense email there, a Sunday evening spent re-reading old threads to find out who said what.

And under it all, the feeling that a real business would have a way of starting. That somewhere, other people have a door you walk through together, and you've been climbing in through the window.

The turning point, in a hallway

You put the eggs on the counter. You take off your wet coat. And instead of replying right away, you do something unusual: you stand in the hallway and ask yourself a different question.

Not "How do I handle this client?"

But: "What would it feel like if starting were already decided?"

If you didn't have to invent the opening every time. If there were one calm page that asked the right things in the right order, one short agenda for the first call, one clear line that said this is what we agreed, this is what's not included. If a welcome message was already written, warm and professional, and all you did was add their name.

You realise it doesn't have to be a heavy system. It doesn't need software, or a 40-page process. It just needs to exist before the yes arrives, so that the yes lands on something solid.

The thought is oddly quiet. It feels like loosening a scarf.

An ordinary Monday, afterwards

Picture the same project, started differently.

On Monday morning, you open your laptop at nine. The welcome message went out on Saturday afternoon, and it sounded like you: friendly, clear, unhurried. It told them what happens next, what you need from them, and roughly when.

By 9:30, their answers are already in. Not perfect, but enough. The access you need is on its way. You know who decides and who just needs to be kept in the loop.

At eleven you have the kickoff call. It is thirty minutes, not ninety. You aren't scrolling through old emails while they talk, because the scope is already written on one page, in plain words, and both of you have nodded at it. When the client says, "Oh, and could you also…", you can smile and say, "Good idea. Let's note that as a next step," without your stomach tightening.

By lunch, you are doing the actual work. The part you are good at. The part you were hired for.

And at six, the laptop closes. Not because you rushed, but because the project began with a shape.

Nothing dramatic happened. That is the whole point. The project simply started on solid ground, and the client noticed, even if they never said it. People relax when someone else clearly knows the way.
