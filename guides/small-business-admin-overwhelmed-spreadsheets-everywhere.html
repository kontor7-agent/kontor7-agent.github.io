Spreadsheets Everywhere? The Monday You Couldn't Find the Number

2026-10-12

It's Monday, 8:50 am. Your coffee is still too hot to drink.

You're a ceramicist with a small online shop and a few wholesale clients. A café owner has just emailed: "Could you remind me what we agreed for the autumn order, and when I'd get the invoice?"

A simple question. You know the answer is somewhere.

You open the laptop. There's orders_final.xlsx. Next to it, orders_final_NEW.xlsx. Then Kunden 2025, Kunden aktuell, and a file called money?? that you made at some point in March and haven't dared to open since.

You click one. Close it. Click another.

The café owner is waiting. You are, quietly, sweating.

The quiet cost of "it's all in there somewhere"

Nobody sees this part of running a small business. From the outside, you make beautiful things. From the inside, you are also the person who knows where every number lives, or at least used to.

The spreadsheets didn't start as a problem. Each one was a sensible answer to a single worry. One for who owes you money. One for stock. One for the hours you spent on a custom commission. One for the receipts you'll definitely sort before the year ends.

But they never talk to each other. So you become the connection between them. You carry the whole map in your head, and on a tired Monday, the map has holes.

The cost doesn't arrive as one big disaster. It's smaller and more constant:

  • The reply you postpone because you can't find the figure.
  • The Sunday afternoon that quietly turns into "just a quick look at the admin."
  • The low hum of I'm probably forgetting something.
  • The feeling that a real business would have this sorted by now.

That last one is the heaviest. It isn't true, but it feels true.

Eleven minutes of searching

Back at the screen, you find the agreement. It was in an email thread, not a spreadsheet at all. The invoice date? That's in a different file, in a column you labelled "sent?" and filled in only sometimes.

You reply to the café owner. You sound calm and professional. Your shoulders don't feel it.

Then, because the file is open anyway, you scroll. Two invoices from last month have no "paid" mark. Maybe they were paid and you forgot to tick them. Maybe not. You'd have to check the bank account to know, and the bank app wants a code from a phone that is charging in the other room.

You close the laptop. The day hasn't even started, and you already feel behind.

The small realisation

Somewhere around lunchtime, standing in the queue at the bakery, something shifts. The person ahead of you is paying with a card, the machine beeps, and the whole thing takes four seconds. It's all just there, in one place, done.

You think: My problem isn't that I'm bad at admin.

My problem is that my admin lives in eleven places.

It's an oddly gentle thought. You weren't careless. You were improvising, one sensible patch at a time, for years. Of course it grew into a patchwork.

And patchworks can be replaced with something plainer. Not something bigger or cleverer. Just fewer places, laid out the same way each time, so your head can stop being the filing cabinet.

A different Monday

Picture it a few weeks from now. It's 8:50 am again. The coffee is still too hot.

The café owner emails the same question. You open one file. The client is there, the agreement is there, the invoice is there with its date and its status. You answer in two minutes and take your first sip.

Then, because it's Monday, you spend ten minutes looking over the week. Who owes you something. What's running low on the shelf. Which job is eating more hours than it should. You don't dig. You simply see.

By 9:15 you're at the wheel, or the desk, or the kiln, doing the work you actually started this for.

Nothing dramatic happened. There was no overhaul and no weekend lost to "getting organised." The knot you'd stopped noticing just quietly loosened, because the answers were where you expected them to be.

That's what calm admin feels like. Not perfection. Just not having to hunt.
