Client Wants More Edits Before Paying the Final Invoice?

2026-10-24

The email arrives while you are standing in the petrol station queue, one hand on a coffee, the other on your phone.

Hi! We love the gallery. Could you also do a few more edits on the ones from the garden? And maybe a black-and-white set? Then we'll sort the payment.

You read it twice. The queue moves. You don't.

The final invoice has been open for nineteen days. The gallery went out two weeks ago. And now the money has a condition attached to it, written so kindly that it almost doesn't look like one.

The thank-you that costs you

You do what most of us do. You type Of course! and delete it. You type Once the invoice is settled, I'm happy to and delete that too, because it sounds cold. You are not cold. You spent nine hours in a church and a car park and a field, and you remember how the bride's mother cried at the hedge.

So you put the phone away and drive home with the sentence still sitting in your chest.

This is the part nobody puts in the portfolio. The shoot is the easy bit. The hard bit is the quiet afterwards, when the work is delivered and the payment is not, and you are the one who has to say something.

What it quietly costs

It costs an evening, first. You open the editing software "just to see" how long twelve more photos would take. Two hours, you guess. Maybe three, with the black-and-white set.

It costs you a little respect for your own work. If the edits are free and the invoice can wait, then what exactly was the price for?

And it costs you certainty. Because when you sit down to answer, you realise you can't say for sure what was agreed. How many edited photos were in the package? Was the deposit paid before the date or after? When did the final invoice actually go out, and on what terms? The answers are somewhere. In an email thread from March. In a note on your phone. In a folder called Garden Wedding FINAL v2.

You don't want to write a firm reply from a place of I think.

The call that went differently

Picture a different photographer, a made-up one, call her Mira. Same kind of email, same kind of client, but a Monday morning instead of a petrol station.

Mira opens one page before she opens her inbox. It is a plain overview of every client she has: which shoots are done, which galleries are out, which deposits arrived, which final invoices are still open and for how many days.

The garden couple sit in the open column. Package: 40 edited images. Delivered: 40. Final invoice: sent, unpaid, day nineteen.

She doesn't have to wonder. She doesn't have to dig. She reads the facts, once, and the knot in her stomach has somewhere to go.

Her reply is short and kind. She thanks them for the lovely words. She says the 40 edited images in the package have been delivered, and that she'd be glad to quote the extra edits and black-and-white set separately once the final invoice is settled. She attaches the invoice again. No apology. No essay.

It isn't magic. They might grumble. They might pay by Thursday. But the reply was written from a steady place, and that changes the tone of the whole exchange.

A normal Monday, after

This is what "under control" actually looks like, and it is much smaller than people expect.

You drink your coffee while it's still hot. You glance at one overview and know which galleries are due this week, which deposits have landed, and which final invoices are waiting. You see the garden couple's line and you know it, the way you know your own front door.

When a client asks for more, you don't panic and you don't give in out of guilt. You say yes, here's what that costs, or yes, after the invoice is settled. Either way, it's a sentence you can say with a level voice.

And on Wednesday afternoon, when the editing is actually fun again, you are editing the shoots you have been paid for. The extra work becomes a new, priced job instead of a favour you owe.

The evening is yours. The petrol station queue is just a queue.
