Hair Colour Cost Not in Your Price? The Mixing Bowl

2026-11-05

Mara has a client in the chair, foils folded, timer set for thirty-five minutes. She uses them for the thing she never has time for at any other point in the day: standing at the little shelf by the window and looking at the mixing bowl.

It's Thursday, a quarter to one. The bowl sits on a small kitchen scale she bought for baking. The number on the display is bigger than she expected. Then she looks at the tube. Then at the developer bottle, which was full on Monday.

A thought arrives, uninvited, in a very quiet voice: Is this one even making me money?

(Mara is an illustrative character, not a real person. But you may know her.)

The price you set in your first month

Mara rents a chair in a bright little salon. Her colour price is the one she wrote down when she started, partly copied from the studio down the road, partly rounded to something that felt brave but not rude.

She has never put the product in it. Not really. It felt like something you just absorb. Colour is colour. You buy it, you use it, you buy more.

But the tube that lasted two clients last spring seems to last one and a bit now. Her client today has hair past her shoulders and a lot of roots. Mara said "of course, no problem" when she asked for the all-over refresh. She said the same thing she always says.

The price on the list didn't change. The bowl did.

The sum you do while smiling

The worst part is that you can't think properly while you work. You're chatting about the holiday in Portugal. You're checking the foils. Meanwhile, underneath the conversation, a second one is running.

What did the colour cost? What did the gloss cost? The gloves, the foils, the cape wash, the chair rent for this hour? Did I charge for the toner last time, or did I forget again?

She doesn't know. That is the honest answer. She knows the takings at the end of the week, and they look fine, and she has a creeping feeling that "fine" is hiding something.

The feeling is familiar to anyone who works alone. Busy is not the same as paid. A full diary can still leave you quietly out of pocket on certain services, and nobody tells you which ones.

She doesn't want to put prices up for everyone out of a vague worry. She also doesn't want to keep doing the same thing out of the same worry. So she does neither. She stays in the in-between, where the knot lives.

The small thing she sees

When the timer goes, Mara rinses, dries, and the client walks out happy. Hair shining, a hug, a booking for six weeks' time. It was a good appointment by every measure you can feel.

She goes back to the shelf and writes down, on the back of the appointment card, three numbers: roughly what went into the bowl, roughly how long the whole thing took, and what she charged.

Then she stares at it.

She doesn't need a degree to see it. The haircut next to it on the list, the one she dreads least, doesn't use anything but time and scissors. And it looks much healthier than the colour she's most proud of.

Nothing is wrong with her. Nothing is broken. She just never looked at each service on its own. She'd been looking at the week as one big blur of money in and money out.

What a calmer Thursday looks like

Imagine the same shelf, the same scale, the same window. The difference is a single page she can glance at.

Each service sits on its own line. What it costs in product, how long it really takes, what it brings in once the chair rent has taken its share. No dramatic revelations, just clear, ordinary numbers that sit still while she looks at them.

When someone asks for the full-head refresh, she doesn't feel a flinch. She knows what it is. If the price is right, she smiles and books it. If it isn't, she knows that too, and she can change it on her own terms, in a calm week, not in a panic with a client waiting.

Monday arrives differently. She opens the salon door without the vague low hum of I hope this is working. She's allowed to like her busiest days.

And when a client says "oh, is it more now?" she can say the new number the way you say the time. Steady. Not defensive. Not apologising for the existence of tubes of colour.
