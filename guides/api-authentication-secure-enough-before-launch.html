API Authentication Secure Enough? The Soundcheck

2026-11-03

It's Wednesday, 4:40 in the afternoon, and you are sitting on a stackable chair at the back of a village hall.

Your sister's band is setting up on the little stage. Cables everywhere. Someone is tapping a microphone and saying "check, check" in the flat voice everyone uses for it. You promised to help carry the amps. Now you have a laptop balanced on your knees and a cup of tea going cold on the floor.

On the screen: your login route, your token middleware, and a launch that's on Friday.

The app is small. A booking tool for physiotherapists, built over eight months, mostly alone. It works. You've clicked through it a hundred times. But one sentence keeps looping in your head: Is my authentication actually secure enough, or does it only look secure to the person who wrote it?

The worry you don't say out loud

Nobody asked you that question. Not yet. The first customers are friendly. Nobody has mentioned a pen test or a security questionnaire.

But you know how it goes. You read a thread last week about someone whose tokens never expired. Another about an endpoint that trusted the wrong ID. Each time you thought, mine is probably fine, and each time the "probably" sat a little heavier.

You've tried the usual things. You skimmed a long article at lunch and nodded along. You added a check you half remembered. You told yourself the framework handles it. Then you closed the tab, because you couldn't tell whether you'd covered the important parts or only the parts you happened to think of.

That's the quiet cost. Not a disaster, just a low hum under everything. You answer a customer email and part of you is still on the middleware. You lie in bed and mentally re-read a route you wrote in March.

You'd like to feel like someone who ships things properly. Right now you feel like someone hoping.

Check, check, one, two

On stage, the sound engineer, a patient woman in a faded festival T-shirt, is working through the band one by one. Kick drum. Snare. Bass. Each channel gets its own moment. Strum, hold, adjust, next.

Your sister's guitarist plays a quick loud riff, clearly itching to just start the song.

"Not yet," the engineer says, smiling. "The loud ones always sound fine. It's the quiet one nobody tested that crackles in the middle of the second verse."

You stop scrolling.

Because that's exactly it, isn't it? You've been testing the loud parts. The login that obviously has to work. The route you were nervous about. You've been checking by feeling, by whatever came to mind, and whatever came to mind was the guitar.

Nobody had given you the list of channels.

The engineer doesn't trust her ears on a good day. She works through a fixed order, every time, so the show doesn't depend on whether she remembers everything. And she isn't anxious. She's calm. Calm because the order exists.

The same Wednesday, a different feeling

Imagine the same hall, the same cold tea, the same Friday. Only this time you have the list.

Not a vague feeling that you ought to be thorough. An actual sequence, written by someone who has walked through this before, with each item waiting for a plain yes or no. Some you tick in a minute. Some surprise you, and you fix them while the band runs through their first song. Some you decide to live with for now, and you write that down, deliberately, with a date.

By the time the doors open, you've gone through all of it. Not perfectly. You're not claiming the app can't be broken. Nobody can honestly promise that. But you know what you checked, what you didn't, and why. The "probably" is gone, replaced by a record.

Friday morning feels different too. You make coffee. You open the laptop, press the button, and watch the first sign-up arrive. Your shoulders stay where they are, down.

And if a customer later emails, "Can you tell us how you protect access?", you don't feel your stomach drop. You have something real to show. Something that says you run this like a professional, because you do.

What actually changes

It isn't that you become a security expert in two days. It's that the job stops living in your head. A checked list turns a vague dread into a finite afternoon.

The band is playing now, properly. Every channel sits where it should, and the quiet bass is audible under the drums for the first time all evening. Your sister catches your eye from the stage and grins.

You close the laptop halfway and just listen.
