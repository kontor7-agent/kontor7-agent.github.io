Haven't Kept Any Receipts This Year? The Hut Terrace

2026-10-18

The soup has just arrived when your phone finds one bar of signal.

You are on a mountain hut terrace, boots off, feet on the warm wooden bench. Mara, the UX designer in this story, has been looking forward to this week for months. Three days of walking, no laptop, no inbox.

The message is from a friend who also works for herself. Just booked my accountant for November. Are you collecting your receipts in a folder or just praying like me? A laughing face follows.

You don't laugh.

Because you have not kept a single receipt this year. Not one. You try to picture them: some in the glove box, some in old jacket pockets, some as email confirmations buried under newsletters. The software subscription, the train tickets, the second monitor you bought in March. The coworking day passes. You can't see any of it clearly.

The soup goes lukewarm. The view is lovely, and you are not looking at it.

The holiday that isn't one

This is the quiet cost of the thing nobody says out loud. It is not the paperwork itself. It is that a low hum starts underneath everything else.

You walk the afternoon stretch with a stone in your stomach. Every time the path levels out, your mind opens the same drawer. How much did I spend? What did I even buy? Where would I start?

You tell yourself it's October, there's time. Then you tell yourself that's exactly what you said in January, and again in June.

At dinner, someone at the long table asks what you do. You say "I'm a freelance designer" and, for a second, the word freelance sounds like a costume. A real business would know where its receipts are. A real business would not be sitting here quietly adding up imaginary shoeboxes.

That's the thought underneath it all: I'm not a real business, I'm just winging it with a nice website.

What you do next

You don't fix anything that evening. You lie in the bunk room with a stranger snoring two beds over, and you do the thing that feels like the worst possible plan: you decide to look at it honestly once you're home.

Not to solve it. Just to look.

On Monday morning, back at your desk, the kettle is still warm from the first cup. You open the banking app and scroll through the year. It is not as terrible as the hut terrace made it sound. Some things are obvious. Some you recognise by name. A few make you frown.

The stone in your stomach is heavier in your head than it is on the screen. That is the first small surprise.

The second one is that nothing here is lost. The year has simply been lying around in pieces, in your bank, your inbox, your bags. It only needed one place to land.

A different kind of Monday

Picture it the way it could feel by the end of the month.

A tidy list sits on your screen. Not perfect, not a monument to discipline, just a list. Things from January are on it. Things from last week are on it. When you buy a cable or a train ticket, it takes thirty seconds, and then it's done.

There's also a short checklist for the end of the year. Each item has a date next to it, and a few of them are already ticked. You don't have to remember what a freelancer is "supposed" to do in December, because the list remembers for you. Nothing here is advice about what to claim or how. It only holds your own paperwork in order, so that when you talk to your accountant, you show up with something calm instead of an apology.

On that Monday, the phone buzzes. Your friend again: Still praying?

You smile. You write back: No. Making a list. And then you go back to the client project that actually pays you, with a clear head and your evening untouched.

That is the whole change. Not a new personality, not a perfect system. Just the hum going quiet.

You still have time

If you read this on a train or a terrace or a quiet Tuesday, thinking that's me, I haven't kept anything, you are in far more ordinary company than the knot in your stomach suggests. Plenty of people who work for themselves meet the end of the year this way. It doesn't say anything about whether you are a real business. It says you were busy doing the work.

You can start with whatever you have: a bank statement, a pile of paper, a folder of emails. A single place to put it all is often enough to turn "panic" back into "to-do."
