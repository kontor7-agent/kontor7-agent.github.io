Client Said They'd Pay, Still Nothing? Too Many Reminders?

2026-10-22

The engineer's window is eight to twelve. It is 9:40, and you are sitting on the kitchen floor next to a washing machine that has been dead since Sunday.

You aren't really waiting for the engineer. You are looking at your phone.

The message from your client is still open. "Payment will go out this week, sorry for the delay!" It came eleven days ago. There was one like it before that. The invoice itself is now twenty-six days past its due date.

The amount is the rent. You know that without opening the spreadsheet.

Let's call you Jonas, a freelance web developer. This is an illustration, not a real case. The scene may still feel familiar.

The question you keep asking yourself

You've typed a search into your phone before the kettle even boiled: how many reminders is too many?

You don't really want a number. You want permission. Permission to write again without being "that freelancer". Permission to stay quiet without feeling like a doormat.

So you do what you always do. You open a new email, write "Hi Katrin, just checking in on…", and delete it. Too soft. You try "As per my previous two emails…" and delete that too. Too sharp. You have no idea what tone is normal, because nobody ever tells you.

The engineer's van is not here yet. The cursor blinks.

What waiting quietly costs

Nobody talks about the small tax on this kind of limbo.

It's the way you check your banking app while brushing your teeth. It's the grocery shop where you do the sums in your head at the shelf, the cheaper pasta, the "maybe next week" on the good coffee. It's the call with another client that afternoon, where you smile and sound confident and quietly wonder whether you are a real business or just someone who does favours for money.

It isn't only the money. It's the dignity. Chasing a payment feels like asking for a gift you've already earned.

And there's a worse thing underneath. Every day you don't write, the silence gets heavier. Every email you do write has to carry that weight, and so you rewrite it nine times and send nothing.

The knock at 10:15

The engineer arrives at a quarter past ten. He is tall, unhurried, and carries a toolbox the size of a suitcase. He pulls the machine away from the wall, looks behind it, and says something that stays with you.

"Ah. Small thing. Pump filter. Happens all the time. Nobody ever checks it, so it just builds up until the whole thing stops."

He has it out in under a minute. Water, fluff, one lost sock. Four minutes later the drum is turning.

"Do this every few months," he says, "and you'll never see me again. Sorry, bad for business."

You laugh. Then, while he writes up the invoice on a clipboard, you notice something.

He puts the due date on it. He puts a line about what happens if it's late. He doesn't apologise for either. It is just a normal part of the paper, like the price.

No drama. A routine.

It was never a courage problem

You stand in the hallway after he leaves and the thought arrives, quietly.

Maybe the problem isn't that you're bad at chasing money. Maybe nobody ever gave you a routine for it.

The engineer doesn't agonise over whether this is reminder one or reminder four. He has a way of doing it. It's boring, and the boredom is exactly what makes it calm.

You, on the other hand, have been making it up from scratch every single time. New words, new nerves, new guilt. Of course it feels like begging when every message is an improvised plea.

What if the first nudge was already written, friendly and clear, before the due date even passed? What if the second one was too? What if you could glance at one page and see: this invoice is nine days late, so this is the next step, and it's a small, polite step?

Then "how many reminders is too many?" would stop being a question about you. It would become a question about where you are on a timeline.

A different Thursday

Picture the same kitchen floor on another day.

The washing machine hums. The overdue list is a single tidy page, and it tells you what's due to be sent today. One name, one email, already worded in a voice you'd actually use. You change a greeting, press send, and close the laptop.

There's no knot, no rehearsing in the shower. Katrin may pay tomorrow or in a week. Either way you aren't holding your breath anymore, because the next step is already decided and written down.

By ten past ten you're making coffee. The invoice is still unpaid, but it no longer owns your morning.

The money still matters, of course it does. But the dread becomes something smaller: a task. A calm line on a list.
