Client Paid Late Again, Which Invoices Are Open? The Allotment Shed

2026-10-13

Thursday, 10:50 am. You are standing in a borrowed allotment shed that smells of tomato twine and old rain, holding your phone over a watering can.

The banking app has just buzzed. A payment came in. For a second your shoulders drop.

Then you read the reference line: Rechnung 0412 / Okt.

Which one was that?

Let's call you Marta. You translate for a few agencies and two direct clients. Contracts, product manuals, the occasional wedding certificate. You are good at it. People tell you so. What nobody tells you is how much of the job is remembering who has paid.

The reference you can't place

You scroll. The amount is not round. It doesn't match anything you can recall from the top of your head. It could be the manual from August. It could be half of the big agency job, if they paid in two parts, which they sometimes do and never announce.

You stand there with a trowel in your other hand, thinking: I have sent eleven invoices since June. Maybe twelve.

You don't know. That is the uncomfortable part. You are a person who checks every comma in a legal text three times, and you cannot say with certainty how many invoices are still open.

Somewhere in a folder there is a file called invoices_FINAL_neu. Somewhere else there are PDFs named by date. And there is your email, where some of them were sent and some of them, you think, were not.

What it quietly costs

The money itself is only part of it.

It's the low hum that follows you around. The rent is due Friday. You think there's enough. You could check, but checking means opening the three places where the truth is scattered, and that feels like opening a drawer you know is full of loose screws.

It's the email to the agency that you keep not sending, because you can't be sure whether they are late or you simply never sent the invoice. Writing "just a friendly reminder" to someone who already paid is a special kind of embarrassing. So you wait. And waiting is how a 30-day invoice turns into a 60-day one, and how a perfectly nice client starts to feel like an opponent.

It's the small, private shame of not feeling like a real business. Real businesses know who owes them money. You know who owes you something, roughly, probably.

The shed bench

You sit down on the upturned crate. A neighbour is raking leaves somewhere nearby, slow and steady, in no hurry at all.

And a plain thought arrives, not dramatic, just tired: It doesn't actually have to be this hard to answer one question.

The question isn't complicated. Who has been paid, who hasn't, and what is overdue today. That's it. A bank doesn't lose track of that. A café doesn't. Somehow you've been carrying it in your head, like a phone number you once memorised and now half-forget.

You don't need a new personality. You don't need to become an "organised person". You need one place where every invoice lives, with its date, its due date and a clear yes or no next to it.

That's all. A place that does the remembering, so your head can go back to translating.

The Monday after

Picture a different Monday.

You sit down with coffee. You open one file. It takes about as long as it takes the kettle to finish. The overdue ones are marked; the rest are quietly fine. A payment arrives with an odd reference and you just find the matching line, mark it paid, and you're done. No scrolling. No guessing.

One agency is nine days late. You can see that in a glance, so you write a short, calm email with the invoice number and the date in it. It doesn't feel like begging. It feels like a small administrative fact, politely stated. They answer the same afternoon.

On Friday the rent goes out, and you already knew it would be fine, because you could see what was coming in and when.

Nothing about your work has changed. You still translate, still care about the commas. But the hum is gone. The evening is yours, and so is the weekend, and the walk to the allotment is just a walk.
