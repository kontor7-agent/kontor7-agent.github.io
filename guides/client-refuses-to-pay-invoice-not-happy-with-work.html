Client Won't Pay, 'Not Happy With the Work'? Frame Wall

2026-11-02

Thursday, 14:05. You are standing at the frame wall of an optician's, holding a pair of tortoiseshell glasses you don't really like. Your phone buzzes in your coat pocket.

You only meant to check the time. Instead you read the preview: "Hi Mira, we've discussed internally and we're honestly not happy with the work. We won't be able to pay the invoice."

The assistant asks if you'd like to try them on. You say yes. You have no idea what you just agreed to.

The mirror and the email

In the mirror, a person in unflattering frames is reading the same sentence for the fourth time.

Not happy with the work. You rewrote their website copy three weeks ago. They approved the final version. You have the email with the word "perfect" in it. You think you do. Or was that a different client?

Your face is hot. Your first thought isn't about money at all. It's smaller and meaner: maybe I'm just not good enough.

The second thought is about money. The invoice was for the whole quarter's rent contribution, give or take. You had already mentally spent it. Not on anything wild. On being able to relax a little in December.

You put the glasses back and say you'll think about it. You walk out into a grey afternoon and stand by the tram stop, typing replies with your thumbs.

Of course, I'm sorry to hear that. Delete.

This is unacceptable, you signed off on every round. Delete.

Happy to make any changes you'd like! Delete. That one hurts most, because you know it means working for free to maybe get paid.

What it quietly costs

Nobody talks about this part. The unpaid invoice is only half of it. The other half is the afternoon.

You don't go back to the blog post you were writing. You carry the sentence around the supermarket, past the yoghurt, into the queue. You rehearse speeches to a person who isn't there. You check your phone to see if they've written again. They haven't, which somehow feels worse.

And underneath it all runs a quieter fear: that you've been running a business on goodwill and a folder of half-remembered emails. That if someone calls your bluff, you won't even be sure what you're standing on.

You can't say what you've sent them or when. You don't remember if you ever wrote the reminder, or only thought about it. You open your invoice spreadsheet and the column that should say "status" is a mixture of ticks, question marks and one cell that just says "ask?".

That is the moment it lands. The email isn't the whole problem. The fog around it is.

The reply you didn't have to invent

Here's the thing you notice later, once your pulse has dropped.

A message like theirs feels like a verdict. But when you read it slowly, the way you'd read it for a friend, it's a position. They are unhappy. They aren't paying. You may well disagree. Both of those can be true and still need a calm, written, dated answer that doesn't beg and doesn't snarl.

The problem is that you've never had one ready. Every time something like this happens, you start from zero. You write the email with your stomach instead of your head, and that is exactly how the tone ends up too apologetic or too sharp.

What if the wording already existed? Not a script to hide behind, just a steady voice you could borrow on the days your own voice shakes. And what if you could see at a glance where each invoice stood and what the sensible next step was, rather than guessing at 14:05 outside an optician's?

An ordinary Monday, afterwards

Picture a different version of this week.

The same email arrives. It still stings. You are still a person with feelings and a rent contribution.

But you open your overdue list and the invoice is right there: sent, due, reminded, today's date beside it. The next step is named in plain words. You don't have to decide whether you're allowed to be firm. You have a calm reply that acknowledges their concern, asks which parts they mean, and restates what was agreed and approved. It's polite, specific and short. You adjust two sentences so it sounds like you, and you send it.

Then you go and eat lunch.

On Monday morning, coffee in hand, you glance at the list once more. One client has replied with a question. One has gone quiet, and the list tells you when a gentle follow-up would be next. Nothing is a surprise. The whole check takes about ten minutes, and your head stays your own.

That's the quiet difference. It doesn't make clients nicer. It makes you steadier, and a steady person gets taken more seriously.
