Falling Behind in Your Freelance Business? The Puncture

2026-10-29

It is Saturday, a little after eleven, and Mara is crouched at the side of a cycle path with a tyre lever in one hand.

The back tyre went flat two kilometres ago. She has been pushing the bike ever since, past dog walkers and families with picnic bags. Now she sits on a low wall with the inner tube across her knees, feeling the small, stupid sting of a day that has already gone wrong.

She is an illustrator. She works alone, and she has for four years. Her phone is in her jacket pocket, and she already knows what is on it.

She doesn't need to look. The list lives in her head, and it is loud.

The list that never gets shorter

There's an invoice she thinks she sent. Maybe two. There's a client who said they'd pay "after the holidays", and she isn't sure which holidays. There's a quote she promised by Thursday and a receipt folder that is really just a feeling of guilt in a drawer.

There's also a nagging sense that she charges less than she should. She hasn't looked at her rates since the year she started.

She isn't lazy. She worked nine hours on Thursday. She just can't tell whether those hours went anywhere that counts.

And the worst part is this: when she imagines sitting down on Monday to "catch up", she doesn't know which thing to pick up first. So she opens the inbox, answers three small emails, feels busy, and ends the day exactly as behind as she began it.

The tube hisses faintly when she holds it near her ear. She laughs, because at least this problem has a visible hole.

The kind of behind you can't point at

A flat tyre is honest. You find the leak, you patch it, you ride on.

Falling behind in a freelance business is different. Nothing hisses. There's no single hole to press your thumb over. It's a hundred slow leaks, each one small enough to ignore: an unanswered reminder, an hour you never wrote down, a price you quietly swallowed.

And because you can't point at the problem, you start to suspect it is you. That everyone else has a proper system and you are the one winging it. That a real business wouldn't feel like this.

Mara has had that thought more often than she'd admit. Usually at about six in the evening, when the laptop is still open and nobody is waiting for her to stop.

What the man with the pump said

An older cyclist stops and offers her a hand pump. He glances at her tube, then at the spot on the path where she walked, and says, "Always check the tyre itself before you patch the tube. Otherwise whatever got in is still there, and you're back here in ten minutes."

He rides off, and she stays on the wall with the thought he left behind.

She has been patching, for months. Answering the loudest email. Chasing the most embarrassing invoice. Fixing whatever shouted last. But she has never once looked at the whole thing laid out in one place, with the tyre in her hands, so to speak.

What if she didn't need to catch up on everything? What if she only needed one clear picture: who owes her, what she charges, where her time actually goes?

Not a better work ethic. Not another weekend of sorting. Just one place where the answers sit still long enough to be read.

She finds the splinter of glass inside the tyre with her fingertip. It's tiny. All that trouble for something she can hold between two fingers.

A different Monday

Here is the Monday she starts picturing as she pumps the tyre back up.

She sits down with coffee. She doesn't open the inbox first. She opens one page, and it tells her three things: two invoices are open, one is a week late, and the quote for Thursday is the only thing with a deadline.

She writes the reminder in a calm voice, because now she knows the date and the amount. She doesn't have to dig for them.

She spends ten minutes looking at where last week's hours went, and for the first time notices that one small client takes up far more of her time than their invoice suggests. She doesn't panic. She just makes a note, and next time she quotes that client differently.

By half past nine she knows where she stands. Not perfectly, and not with everything fixed. But she can see it, and what she can see stops being frightening.

She closes the laptop at six. The evening is hers.

Back on the path

The tyre holds. Mara rides the last stretch home slowly, listening to the ordinary hum of rubber on asphalt, and makes herself one small promise: Monday starts with the whole picture, not the loudest email.

Nothing is solved yet. But "I don't know where to start" has quietly turned into "I know the first ten minutes", and that feels like a much lighter thing to carry.
