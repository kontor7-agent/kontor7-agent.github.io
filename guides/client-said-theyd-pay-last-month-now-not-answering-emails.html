Client Said They'd Pay Last Month, Now Silent? The Boathouse

2026-10-20

It is Saturday, 8:40 am, and the boathouse smells of wet rope and cold coffee.

Jonas, a freelance sound designer, is holding the end of a rowing boat he is supposed to be lifting. His phone is in his jacket pocket. He has checked it four times since he arrived. Nothing.

Three weeks ago, the client wrote: "Payment will go out by the end of the month, promise!" The month ended. Another week passed. Jonas sent a friendly email. Then a slightly less friendly one. Both disappeared into silence.

(Jonas is an illustrative character, not a real person.)

The boat is heavy, the thought is heavier

"On three," someone says. Jonas lifts. His arms do the work. His head is somewhere else.

Is the invoice lost? Did they read it and decide not to answer? Should he write again, or is that pushy? Maybe he should call. The thought of calling makes his stomach tighten, and he lets it go.

He knows the amount. It is rent for most of a month. He has not said that to anyone, because saying it would make it real.

What bothers him most is not the money itself. It is how small he feels. He did the work. He delivered on time. And now he is the one who feels awkward, as if he were the one asking for a favour.

The cost nobody puts on the invoice

Out on the water, the others talk about the weekend. Jonas rows and counts.

He counts what is in his account. He counts days. He counts how many times he has rewritten the same email in his head: "Just a quick reminder..." "Just checking in..." "I hope you're well, I wanted to follow up..."

Each version sounds like an apology. None of them sounds like a person running a business.

That is the quiet cost of an unanswered payment promise. It is not only the delay. It is the Sunday you half spend, the new project you hesitate to quote because you are not sure the money is coming, and the faint feeling that you are "not a real business" if people can ignore you this easily.

What he realises on the dock

Back at the boathouse, hands red from the cold, Jonas sits on the bench and finally takes out his phone. Still nothing.

He opens his notes and looks at his own history with this client. The promise. The two emails. Dates scattered in three different places, one of them a half-finished draft.

And a thought arrives, quietly: I don't have a next step. I only have a feeling.

That is what has been wearing him down. Not that the client is silent, but that every time he thinks about it, he starts from zero. Is it time to write? Which tone? How firm? Nobody ever told him there is an order to this. He has been improvising something that could be calm and routine.

A friend from the club sits down beside him, towel around her neck. "You look like someone who's waiting for a bank transfer," she says.

He laughs, for the first time that morning. "That obvious?"

"Everyone here has been there," she says. "What helped me was not having to decide how to feel about it each time. I had the wording ready and I knew what came next."

A Monday that feels different

Imagine the same situation, but on a Monday morning.

You open one sheet. It shows the overdue invoice, the date of the last message, and a plain note: next step. No guessing. No rehearsing in the shower.

You pick a short, friendly, firm email that already sounds like you on a good day. It names the invoice, the amount, the date. It asks for a clear reply. It does not apologise for existing.

You send it, close the laptop, and make tea.

If the client answers, wonderful. If they don't, you are not lost. The next message is already waiting, a little more direct, and you know roughly when to send it. You are not begging. You are following a routine, the way a shop sends a second notice without anyone taking it personally.

The money may still take time. But the knot in your stomach loosens, because you are no longer carrying the whole question alone in your head.
