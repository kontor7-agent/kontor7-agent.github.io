Client Feedback by Email, WhatsApp, Phone? The Corridor

2026-11-03

It is 11:20 on a Wednesday, and you are sitting on a plastic chair in a hospital corridor.

Your dad is behind a door with a sign that says X-Ray. You promised to wait. You brought your laptop, mostly out of habit.

Your phone buzzes against your knee. A WhatsApp voice note, 1:47 long, from Marco, the owner of the bike-repair shop whose website you are building. You don't play it. There are people around, and you left your earbuds at home.

Under it sits an email from Monday: "Can we make the header calmer? Also the prices on page 3 are wrong." Under that, a missed call. Under that, a text from Marco's business partner: "Marco says ignore the colours, I think we should keep them."

You stare at the screen. Which version is current?

The pile you carry around

Nobody warned you about this part of freelancing.

The work is fine. You can build the site. But the feedback arrives in four places, in four tones, and sometimes contradicts itself. One thing was said on a call while you were making tea. One thing lives in a voice note you haven't played. One thing is buried in a thread called "Re: Re: Re: Quick question."

So you do what most of us do. You keep it all in your head.

That works until it doesn't. On a quiet day you remember everything. On a day like this one, with your dad behind a door and your mind somewhere else, you can feel the edges slipping.

The worst part is the small, private dread. What if you miss one change? What if Marco says, "I told you about the prices," and he did, and you can't prove where or when? Worse, what if you can't even remember?

You wouldn't call it a crisis. It is just a low hum that sits behind every project, and it makes you feel a little less like a professional and a little more like someone juggling.

The voice note

You put one earbud-less ear to the phone, hold it close and play the voice note at a whisper.

Marco says the header is fine now. He talked to his partner. They want the old colours back after all. And could you also add the Saturday opening hours? He mentioned it on the phone, he thinks. Maybe.

So Monday's email is already out of date. You scroll up to check whether you already changed the colours. You did. You changed them for the email, and now you will change them back.

The door opens. A nurse nods at you. Your dad is still inside, so you look back at the phone.

And a quiet thought arrives, not dramatic, just clear: the work isn't the problem. The start was.

Where it actually began

Three weeks ago, Marco said yes. Within a day you were already working. You never asked him where feedback should go. You never wrote down what "done" looked like. You never said how many rounds of changes were included, or who on his side had the final word.

It felt rude at the time. He was so friendly. Asking felt like adding paperwork to a nice beginning.

But friendly and unclear is exactly how you end up on a plastic chair, trying to rebuild a conversation from a voice note.

None of this is Marco's fault. He is a person who fixes bikes, and he talks to you the way he talks to everyone, by whatever channel is closest. Nobody told him there was a better way. That was supposed to be your part, and you were too busy being agreeable to say it.

You don't feel guilty, exactly. More like you've finally seen the shape of something you've been walking into for years.

A different Monday

Picture the next project. It starts differently.

There is one short page, sent before the first call. It asks a few plain questions and says, kindly, where changes should be sent. One place. One list. The scope is written down in a few lines both of you have agreed to. There is a name next to "who decides."

Then Monday morning, you open one document instead of four apps. Marco's partner's opinion is there, with a date, next to Marco's. You can see the colours question was decided, and by whom.

When a voice note arrives, you smile. You can still play it. You just add its content to the list afterwards, instead of living inside it.

You close the laptop at six. The hum is gone, because nothing is only in your head anymore.

A simple, ready-made starting kit changes that. It doesn't make clients perfect, and it can't read minds. It just means the beginning of a project is already written, so you aren't inventing it in the middle of a busy week.

Back in the corridor

Your dad comes out, a little pale, mostly annoyed about the paper gown. The results will take a few days. You walk him to the car and talk about nothing important.

Later, at home, you create one new note called "Marco: all changes," and you spend ten quiet minutes moving every scrap into it. Colours: old ones. Prices on page 3. Saturday hours: check. It isn't elegant, but you can already feel the difference.

You also make a promise to yourself. The next time someone says yes, you will not start working on a handshake and a good feeling. You'll start with a page.
