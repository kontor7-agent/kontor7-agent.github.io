Finance Team on Holiday, Invoice Unpaid? The Canal Lock

2026-11-08

It is 13:10 on a Thursday, and you are standing on a lock gate beam with a sandwich in one hand and your phone in the other.

You took the day to walk the canal path. Your first day off in three weeks. The water is rising slowly in the chamber, a narrowboat is waiting, and somebody's dog is very interested in your crust.

Then the phone buzzes. It is the reply you have been waiting for since Monday.

"Hi! Thanks for your message. Unfortunately our finance team is on holiday until the 24th. We'll get back to you once they're back. Best wishes."

The 24th. That is sixteen days away.

The invoice was due on the 3rd.

The sandwich goes dry

You read it twice. Nothing in it is rude. That is the problem. There is nothing to be angry at, nothing to push against. Just a friendly wall.

Maya, a freelance video editor (an illustration, not a real person), stood in this exact spot in her head. Her rent goes out on the 1st. A client she likes, a project she is proud of, and a number on her screen that has not moved.

And the thoughts start, in order.

Maybe I should have followed up sooner. Maybe I'm being impatient; people are allowed holidays. Do I write back? Do I say "no problem"? Do I say "I need this paid"? Do I even know who is covering for them?

The lock keeper nods at you. The gate creaks. You laugh politely at nothing and put the phone away, but it stays in your pocket like a warm stone.

What it quietly costs

The holiday is not really the issue. People have holidays. Companies have holidays. Somewhere there is a finance person on a terrace with a cold drink, and good for them.

The cost is on your side of the screen.

It is the walk you keep half-enjoying. The way you check your banking app at the next bridge, then at the one after. The small calculation running underneath every conversation: if it comes on the 24th, then the 1st is tight, and if it comes after the 24th, then...

It is also the nagging sense that a "real business" would know what to do next. That somebody with a back office would simply have a rule for this. Day 1, this. Day 7, that. Instead you have a feeling in your stomach and a draft folder with three versions of the same email, none of them sent.

Chasing money feels like begging when you are guessing. You are not sure whether you are too early, too late, too soft or too sharp. So you wait, and waiting feels safer than choosing, and it costs you a little more calm every day.

The boat goes through

The water levels out. The gate swings open. The narrowboat slides past with a woman on the stern raising her mug at you, and you raise your sandwich back.

And somewhere between the second bridge and the towpath café, a different thought arrives. Quieter than the others.

This isn't a crisis. This is a normal thing that happens. Why do I treat it like an emergency every single time?

Because every time, you start from zero. No timeline. No wording you trust. No single place that says: this invoice, this client, this is where it stands, and this is the next small step.

If you had that, the auto-reply would be information, not a punch. You would see: due on the 3rd, a polite reminder already sent, a note that the finance team is away until the 24th. Next step, an email on the 25th, already written, already calm. Maybe a friendly message to your contact now, asking who covers while the team is out, because a polite question is not begging.

Nothing dramatic. Just a plan that does not depend on how brave you feel today.

A different Thursday

Picture the same walk, with that plan in place.

The phone buzzes. You read the holiday message, and you smile a little, the way you would at a friend cancelling lunch for a good reason. You glance at your overdue list that evening: one row, one date, one next step. You send a short, kind note to your contact. Then you close the laptop at six and make dinner.

The 24th arrives. On the 25th you send the email you wrote ahead of time, or rather chose from a set of calm ones that already sound like you. No rewriting it nine times. No sweaty palms.

The money may still take a few days. That is fine. You know where you stand, and knowing is most of the relief.
