Client Paid Only Part of My Invoice: The Monday Bank Notification

2026-10-14

8:07, Monday, a half-eaten toast

Jonas stands at the kitchen counter with his phone in one hand and a piece of toast in the other. The bank app has just pinged.

Incoming: 1,200.00. Reference: "Rechnung Mai".

He smiles for about two seconds.

Then the thought arrives: Wait. The May invoice was 2,400.

He is a freelance subtitler. Most of his work comes from a handful of studios and agencies, and one of them has been, let's say, relaxed about payment for months. He puts the toast down and opens his laptop. He is no longer hungry.

The search through five places

First, the sent folder. He finds the May invoice, 2,400. But there was also a smaller one in April, and a rush job in June. Did the 1,200 belong to May? Or was it a late half of April and the sender just typed the wrong month?

He opens the spreadsheet he started last year. The "Paid" column holds a mix of dates, a few "yes" entries, and one cell that says "I think". Row 14 has a yellow highlight. He can't remember what yellow meant.

He scrolls through the bank statement. He searches the client's name. He finds two payments from March, one he had already ticked off, one he hadn't.

Twenty minutes later he has three possible answers and no confidence in any of them.

This is the part nobody warns you about. The client paying late is annoying. The client paying a bit, with a vague reference and no explanation, is worse. Now it isn't their mess. It's yours, because you're the only one who has to work out what's missing.

The email he doesn't want to write

He knows what he should do. Write to the studio, say thank you for the payment, and ask politely about the rest.

But ask about what exactly? If he writes "you still owe me 1,200 for May" and it turns out the money was for April, he looks careless. If he writes nothing, he may be waiting for a payment that nobody plans to make.

He types a sentence. Deletes it. Types another.

Just checking in regarding the open balance...

Which balance? He doesn't know the number. That's the real knot in his stomach. It isn't the awkwardness of asking. It's that he can't ask clearly, because he doesn't have a clear picture himself.

And quietly, underneath, a smaller thought: A real business would know this.

It isn't true. Plenty of real, good, hard-working businesses of one person have lost track of a partial payment. But at 8:40 on a Monday, with cold toast, it feels true.

The call that made it plain

At eleven the studio's project manager calls about something unrelated, a deadline for episode six. Jonas is polite and professional on the phone. His voice is steady. His eyes are on row 14.

At the end she says, brightly, "Oh, and accounting says they sent the payment last week, so that should be sorted!"

"Great, thank you," he says.

He doesn't say: It was half. He doesn't say: I'm not even sure which half.

He hangs up and sits there. The cost isn't only the 1,200. It's the call he didn't quite have, the sentence he swallowed, and the afternoon he now spends on detective work instead of on the episode he's actually paid to translate.

The moment it clicks

That evening he tells a friend, another freelancer, about it over a bad video connection. She laughs, not unkindly.

"I used to do that," she says. "Every payment was a mystery. Then I stopped keeping my invoices in one place and my payments in my head. I just put them side by side. Invoice on the left, what's come in against it on the right. The difference sits there by itself."

That's all she says. No lecture. But he keeps thinking about the phrase the difference sits there by itself.

He had never needed a clever system. He had needed one honest place where an invoice and its payments lived together, so that "2,400 sent, 1,200 received" simply read as "1,200 still open", without any detective work, without a yellow highlight he couldn't decode.

A different Monday

Picture the same kitchen a few weeks later.

The phone pings. Incoming: 1,200.00. Jonas glances at it, takes a bite of toast, and opens one sheet. He enters the payment against the May invoice. The balance changes from 2,400 to 1,200 and sits there, quietly marked as still open.

It takes less than a minute.

Then he writes the email. It's short, friendly and specific:

"Thanks for the payment of 1,200 on the May invoice. The remaining 1,200 is still open. Could you let me know when I can expect it?"

No apology. No guessing. No knot.

He finishes his toast. He starts work at nine, and the number in his head is the same as the number on the screen.

What actually changes

Nothing dramatic happens. The client doesn't suddenly turn punctual. Some payments will still be partial, late or oddly labelled.

What changes is that you stop being the person who has to reconstruct the truth every time money moves. You can see it. You can ask for exactly what's owed, in a calm voice, because you know what you're asking for.

And mornings like that one go back to being just mornings.
