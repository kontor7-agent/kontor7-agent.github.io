Client Pays 60 Days Late on Net 30? The Fire Drill

2026-10-24

The fire alarm goes off at 10:42, right in the middle of a sentence you were rewriting.

You grab your phone and your jacket and follow the others down four flights of stairs. Then you stand in the coworking car park with twenty strangers, a cold wind and a sky the colour of old dishwater.

Everyone scrolls. So do you. You open your banking app, because you always do, and the number is the same as it was yesterday.

The invoice was due on the 3rd. Net 30, in writing, on the PDF, in the email, and in the contract you signed back in spring. Today is the 2nd of the month after next. You counted on the stairs. Fifty-nine days.

The pattern you pretend not to see

Let's call you Mara. You write interface copy for software companies. You're good at it, and clients say so.

This client says so most of all. They send work regularly and reply quickly to briefs. They thank you warmly. And they pay on day 60. Every time, as if "net 30" meant "net 30, give or take a month, depending on how our finance team feels".

Nothing about it is dramatic, and that's the problem. You have no villain to complain about. There's just a slow, polite leak.

So you do the maths in your head, standing next to a man in a hi-vis vest holding a clipboard. Rent goes out on the 1st. The tax prepayment is coming. And the money you earned in August is paying for October.

You don't say any of this to anyone. It feels like a thing a "real business" wouldn't have.

What it costs when nobody sends a bill for it

The lateness itself isn't the whole price. The rest is quieter.

It's the small clench when the client's name appears in your inbox, because maybe it's the payment, and maybe it's another request. It's saying yes to a rush job you don't want, because you can't afford a gap. It's the half-written reminder sitting in drafts since last Thursday, where every version sounds either like a doormat or like a lawyer.

You tell yourself they're busy. You tell yourself that chasing would sour a good relationship. You tell yourself it will probably arrive this week.

But "probably this week" has been your cash-flow plan for a year.

The warden finally waves everyone back in. It was a drill, or a burnt toast situation, nobody really says. You climb the stairs more slowly than you came down, and your laptop is still open on the sentence you abandoned.

You can't get back into it. You close it and look out of the window.

The sentence that changed the question

There's a woman at the next desk, an accountant's assistant who freelances on the side. She has been listening without meaning to. At the kettle, she says something small.

She says she stopped asking herself whether the client was being rude. She asked a different question instead: what happens next, and when, if the money isn't there on the due date?

You stand there with a teabag in your hand.

Because you've never had a "what happens next". You've had a feeling. You've had dread on day 31, a flicker of courage on day 38, and a draft you didn't send on day 45. Each time you started from zero, alone, deciding how annoyed you were allowed to be.

None of that is a plan. It's mood-based collecting.

And mood is a terrible system for money. When you feel brave you'd chase a stranger, and when you feel tired you'd forgive anything. A client who always lands at day 60 isn't testing your character. They're simply reacting to whatever happens, or doesn't happen, on day 31.

A different Monday

Picture the same office a few weeks from now.

It's Monday, a quarter past nine, and the coffee is still too hot to drink. You open one page and see your open invoices in a short list. Next to each one is a plain note about what comes next. One says "friendly nudge today". Another says "nothing yet". The late client's invoice says "second reminder, wording ready".

You read the email once. It's polite and clear and sounds like you on a good day. It names the invoice, the date and the amount, and it doesn't apologise for existing. You press send and go back to your work.

There's no pit in your stomach and no hour lost to wording, and you don't have to decide whether you're allowed to ask. The decision was made earlier, by a calmer version of you, when nothing was at stake.

Something else changes too, though it's less visible. Clients tend to notice who follows up on day 31 and who goes quiet. You don't have to turn into a harsh person. You only become a predictable one, and predictable people tend to be paid earlier. We can't promise that for your clients, of course. But it's a much better position to stand in than hoping.

And on a day when the money still doesn't come, you aren't surprised. You look at the next step, do it, and carry on with your afternoon.
