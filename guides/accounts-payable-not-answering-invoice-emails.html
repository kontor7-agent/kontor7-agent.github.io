Accounts Payable Ignoring Your Invoice? The Hold Music

2026-11-02

The corridor outside the cardiology clinic smells of floor polish and weak coffee. Your dad is behind a door with a nurse and a blood pressure cuff. You have forty minutes, a plastic chair, and your phone.

You are not here to work. You told your clients you'd be offline until noon. But your thumb opens your inbox anyway, the way it does a hundred times a day.

Nothing from accounts payable.

That's three emails now. The first one went out five weeks ago, polite and brief, invoice attached. The second said "just checking this reached you." The third had a subject line you rewrote four times and still didn't like: Friendly reminder, invoice 0412.

No reply. Not even an automatic one.

The address that never answers

You are a video editor. Your contact, Marta, loved the cut and said "we'll get this sorted right away." Then the invoice went into a building you can't see, run by people you've never met, behind an address that starts with ap@.

Somewhere in there, a person may be reading your emails. Or no one is. Or the mailbox is a drain.

You don't know. That's the worst part. You can't even be angry at someone, because there's no one to be angry at.

So you do what you always do. You open a draft and type: Hi, I just wanted to follow up again… and the word again sits there like a stain. You delete it. You type: Per my previous emails… and it sounds like a door slamming. You delete that too.

A vending machine hums by the wall. Down the corridor, someone laughs at something on their phone, and the sound feels very far away.

What it quietly costs

The invoice is 2,400. It isn't a fortune, but it is rent, and the electricity bill that's due on the same day your terms said you'd be paid.

You haven't told anyone that you're checking your bank app before you buy groceries. You haven't said that you look at the unfamiliar number in your call log and your stomach tightens, just in case.

And there's the other cost, the one that doesn't show up in any account. Each silent week, you feel a little less like a professional and a little more like someone who's asking for a favour. You did the work. You delivered on time. Why does collecting what you're owed feel like begging?

So you do nothing. You tell yourself you'll chase it Monday. Then Monday comes with a new project, and the invoice slides down your mind like a coin behind a sofa cushion.

The call you could make

A woman in a green cardigan sits down two chairs away and starts a crossword. You catch yourself looking at the way she writes, slow and sure, no hesitation about the next square.

You think about what you'd do if this were a client who simply said no. You'd know what that was. You'd decide. What's eating you isn't the refusal. It's the fog. You don't know whether the next move is another email, a phone call, a different person, or just waiting. You're guessing at the right step, and every guess feels like it might make things worse.

What if you didn't have to guess?

What if the first reminder had a tone you'd already chosen, calm and plain, written on a day when you weren't tired? What if the second one went to a different place than the first, because you already knew accounts payable mailboxes are often not where things get moving? What if you could see, in one glance, that this invoice was twelve days past due, that the next step was a short phone call to a named person, and that it was Thursday's job, not tonight's worry?

Not a threat. Not a fight. Just a rhythm. The kind a business has, even a business of one.

A different Monday

Picture it, a few weeks from now.

It's Monday morning, 8:40. Coffee on the desk, window cracked open. You spend ten minutes with one sheet. Two invoices are overdue. One is a week late, so it gets a light, friendly note, already written, name swapped in. The other, the 2,400 one, is past the point where email is enough. The sheet says: call Marta, then copy her on a short follow-up.

You make the call. It rings twice. You say what you've practised: "Hi Marta, I'm checking on invoice 0412. Could you tell me where it is in your payment run?" It's a normal sentence. It doesn't tremble.

She says she'll forward it to finance directly. You write it down, set the next date, and close the laptop.

The knot you've been carrying behind your ribs for five weeks is not gone, exactly. But it's loosened. You know where things stand, and you know what happens next.

Back in the corridor

The door opens. Your dad comes out buttoning his shirt cuff, a bit pale but smiling. "All fine," he says. "They want me back in six months."

You stand, put the phone in your pocket, and take his arm. The draft is still sitting there, unsent, with its stubborn again. It can wait one more hour.

But on the drive home, you decide something small. The next time money goes quiet, you won't write the email nine ways in your head. You'll have a plan before the silence starts.
