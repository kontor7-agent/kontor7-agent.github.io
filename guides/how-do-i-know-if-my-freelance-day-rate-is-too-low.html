Is My Freelance Day Rate Too Low? The Soft-Play Birthday Party

2026-10-14

The soft-play centre smells of chips and rubber mats. It is Saturday, 2:40 pm, and your niece has just disappeared into a tunnel of purple foam with eleven other six-year-olds.

You are holding a paper cup of lukewarm tea. Beside you stands a man named Jonas, someone's uncle, someone's dad, you never caught which. He mentions he's a freelance sound editor. You say you're a freelance designer. For a minute it's the nice kind of small talk.

Then he asks, easily, the way you'd ask about the weather: "So what's your day rate these days?"

And you hear yourself say the number. Fast. A little too fast. You watch his face for half a second.

He doesn't say anything unkind. He just says, "Right. Okay." And lifts his cup.

That "Okay" follows you down the slide, through the cake, and all the way home.

The pause you read too much into

That evening you replay it. Was it the pause? Was it the eyebrow? You can't tell. You don't know if the number was fine, or low, or embarrassingly low.

Here is the odd part. You've been booked solid for months. Nobody has pushed back on your price in a long time. Some clients say yes before you've finished the sentence.

You used to take that as a compliment. Tonight it feels different.

Because when you're honest, there are a few other things you've been quietly carrying around:

  • You were tired in March. Properly tired, though the diary looked wonderful.
  • A good month still ended with a bank balance that surprised you, and not in the nice way.
  • You can't say what a day of your work actually costs you to run. You know what it earns. That's not the same thing.
  • There's a small, sour feeling when a client says "great, perfect!" to your quote. Not pride. Something closer to oh no, I could have said more.

None of this is dramatic. That's why it's easy to ignore. It just sits there, like a stone in your shoe that you've learned to walk around.

The number you picked once and never questioned

So where did your rate come from?

You think back. Honestly, it came from a forum post, a former colleague's offhand comment, and the rate of the first client who said yes. You rounded it to something that felt polite. Then you stuck with it, year after year, like a haircut you stopped questioning.

Nobody ever asked you to justify it, and you never asked yourself.

The thing is, a day rate isn't really a price tag for your talent. It's the answer to a much plainer question: what does one working day have to bring in so that the whole year holds together? Not just the days you invoice. The sick days. The admin mornings. The weeks of holiday. The slow February. The software subscriptions and the tax you'll owe.

You realise you've never put those pieces next to each other. Not once. You've only ever looked at the number from the outside, from the client's side of the table.

Sunday, with a pen

On Sunday morning you sit at the kitchen table with coffee and a sheet of paper. It feels slightly ridiculous. You're a grown professional doing sums on the back of an envelope.

But something shifts as you write. You start listing what a year really contains. Not the fantasy year where every weekday is billable, but the real one, with its gaps and its dentist appointments and its quiet weeks. The list is longer than you expected. A few lines make your stomach drop a little.

You don't need to do anything dramatic with that feeling. You only need to notice it. The knot in your chest has a shape now, and a shape is easier to deal with than a fog.

At some point you stop and think: I should do this properly, not on an envelope. Somewhere that keeps the pieces together, so you can change one thing and see what happens to the rest.

What a different Monday feels like

A few weeks later, a new client emails. Mid-sized company, friendly tone, a three-week project. They ask for your day rate.

Your heart does its usual little skip. But then something unfamiliar happens: it settles.

You know your minimum, the number below which a day of work quietly costs you money. You know the number you'd like to charge, which sits a comfortable step above it. And you know, for the first time, that the gap between them is yours to decide, calmly, without needing a stranger at a birthday party to tell you whether it's okay.

You type the number. You don't round it down to be polite. You read the sentence once, send it, and go and put the washing on.

The reply comes that afternoon. "Sounds good, let's go ahead."

No drama. No cheering. The world doesn't change. But your evening is a little lighter, and the sour feeling doesn't turn up.

That's all it was. Not a bigger price, necessarily. A number you can stand behind.
