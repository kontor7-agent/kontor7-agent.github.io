Payment 'Coming' but Nothing Arrives? The Fuel Pump

2026-10-17

It is 8:05 on a Wednesday morning and you are standing at a petrol pump with the nozzle in your hand.

The card reader says Please wait. Behind you, someone in a van is pretending not to watch the screen.

It goes through. Of course it goes through. The account is fine today.

But your thumb is already on your phone, because you want to check one thing. You open your banking app and look at the incoming payments. Nothing new. You look again, as if the list might have changed in the last four seconds.

Let's call you Mira. You are a freelance motion designer, and an illustrative one, not a real person. Last Thursday a client wrote: "Payment is being processed, you'll have it early next week!"

It is now the Wednesday after next week's early.

The sentence you keep drafting

In the car you start composing the message in your head.

Hi Jonas, just checking in on the payment... Too soft. Hi Jonas, as promised, the payment should have arrived... Too sharp. Hi Jonas, no worries at all if it's busy... But there is a worry. It's your rent. It's the 1,800 you have already mentally spent, twice.

You know the real problem isn't the wording. The problem is that every version sounds like begging, or like picking a fight, and you don't want to be either with someone you'd like to work for again.

So you do what most of us do. You wait one more day. "Early next week" is stretchy, after all. Maybe it's the bank. Maybe it's a holiday somewhere.

The waiting has a price, though nobody invoices you for it. It's the small tightening in your chest when your phone buzzes. It's the way you say "yes, sure, no rush" in a meeting, while thinking the opposite. It's the quiet maths you do at the supermarket queue, adding up the basket before the payment notification you're still waiting for.

The call you take in the car park

At 10:30 a different client calls to talk about a new project. You park outside the office supply shop and take it with the engine off.

It goes well. They want a six-second logo animation, and then probably more. You hear yourself sounding confident, friendly, professional.

Then they ask, "Can you start this week?"

And there it is. You are about to start unpaid-for work for one client while waiting on money from another, and you can't remember exactly when you last wrote to Jonas, or what he said, or how many days it's been.

You say, "Let me check my schedule and come back to you this afternoon." It's a perfectly good sentence. It's also a small lie, because your schedule isn't the issue.

After you hang up, you sit there for a minute with the radio off.

What you notice, sitting there

It isn't that Jonas is a bad person. He probably isn't. Most late payments are somebody's forgotten approval, an accounts inbox nobody checks, or a Friday that swallowed the batch run.

The real trouble is on your side of the screen. You have no rhythm for this. Every late invoice becomes a fresh emotional event, with a fresh draft, a fresh knot, a fresh decision about whether you're allowed to ask.

You don't have a next step. You have a mood.

And a mood is a terrible tool for asking someone for money you've already earned.

The version of Wednesday where you already know

Picture the same morning, slightly different.

You stand at the pump. The reader says Please wait. You don't open the banking app, because you already know where things stand. Jonas's invoice is on a short list you looked at on Monday. It says: promised for last Tuesday, one friendly reminder sent, next step: a clear second note today.

Nothing dramatic. Just a line on a page, telling you what a calm person would do next.

And the note itself is already sitting there in a folder, in plain, warm, professional words. You change the name, the amount and the date. You read it once and it sounds like you on a good day: kind, specific, not apologising for existing.

You send it from the car park. It takes about ninety seconds. Then you go and answer the new client with a real answer.

Maybe Jonas pays that afternoon. Maybe he writes back with an apology and a date. Either way, you are no longer carrying it around. It sits on the list, doing its quiet job, and you get your attention back.

That is all a small system changes. Not the client. Not the bank. Just whether you have to invent your courage from scratch every time.

A Monday that feels different

The nicest part is not the money arriving, though that is nice.

It's the Monday where you open your laptop and, in ten minutes, see everything: who has paid, who is due this week, who is a few days past, and what the sensible next message is. No digging through the sent folder. No "wait, did I already chase this one?"

You close the laptop and make coffee. Your chest is loose. When a client says "it's on its way," you can answer, "Lovely, thank you, I'll check on Friday," and mean it, because Friday is already written down.

Chasing money starts to feel less like begging and more like what it is: ordinary business admin, done politely, on a schedule.
