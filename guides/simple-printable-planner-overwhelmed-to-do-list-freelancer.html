Overwhelmed by To-Do Lists? The Monday Call Question

2026-10-15

It's Monday, 9:05. The video call has just started, and the client is smiling in her little square.

"So, what's the main focus for you this week?"

You open your mouth. Nothing useful comes out.

Let's call you Mira. You write copy for a few steady clients, and you're good at it. But right now your head holds the landing page draft, the invoice you meant to send Friday, the dentist email, the half-finished newsletter, the idea for a new service page, and the strange guilt about a podcast you were going to start in spring.

"Oh, a few things," you say. "The landing page, mostly. And some other bits."

She nods. It's fine. Nobody noticed. But you felt it: the little slip of not knowing your own week.

The list that kept growing

After the call you look at your to-do list. It's a long one, and it has been long for months. Items from February are still on it, looking at you politely.

You've tried the apps. Each one felt like a fresh start for about four days. Then the colours, tags and recurring reminders became a job of their own. You spent a Sunday setting up a system and a Wednesday abandoning it.

So the list lives partly in an app, partly in a notebook, and partly in your chest, as a low hum that says you're forgetting something.

That hum has a cost. You start the day with email because it's easy. By eleven you've answered nine messages and moved nothing that mattered. At five you feel busy and behind at once, which is an odd and tiring combination. Dinner arrives with half your mind still on tomorrow.

The worst part isn't the workload. It's the quiet suspicion that other people have this figured out, and you're just winging it with a nice font.

The question underneath

That evening you do the dishes and keep hearing the client's question. What's the main focus this week?

It's a simple question. A real business can answer it in one breath. Yours couldn't, because every task looked equally loud.

You realise you don't need a bigger list. You need a smaller one. Something that says: today, these three things. Not twenty. Three.

You think about how that would feel. Three lines on a page. You do them, or you don't, and either way the day has a shape.

You don't need a clever system for that. You need paper, a pen and a layout that gets out of the way.

A different Monday

Picture the same Monday, a few weeks later.

You sit down at 8:40 with coffee. Beside the laptop there's a printed page, slightly curled at one corner. You write three things. The landing page draft. Send the invoice. Reply to the one email that has been waiting since Thursday.

That's it. The newsletter idea, the podcast and the new service page go on a small "later" list, where they can wait without nagging you.

You don't open email first. You write the first paragraph of the landing page, and the hum from your chest goes quiet.

At 9:05 the call starts, and the client asks her question.

"Landing page first, then I'm sending your invoice, and I'll clear the backlog of replies by Wednesday."

She says, "Perfect." You say it without any effort, because it's just what is written on the page beside you.

What a small page quietly changes

Nothing dramatic happens. Your work doesn't shrink and your clients don't change.

But Friday has a different feel. You flip through the week and see what you actually did, not just what you meant to do. A few things slipped, and that's fine. The page shows you, calmly, and you move them to next week instead of carrying them around as guilt.

On Sunday evening there's a short look back: what worked, what was too much, what to leave out. Five minutes, not a ceremony. You write the plan for Monday and close the notebook.

The list no longer follows you into the supermarket queue. You're just standing there with a basket of tomatoes and a quiet head. It's a small thing, but it may be the one you were really after.
