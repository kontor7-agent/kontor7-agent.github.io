Plan 2027 on Unpredictable Freelance Income: The Eye Test

2026-10-19

The optician has you covering your left eye with a plastic spoon.

"Read me the third line," she says. The letters on the wall are soft at the edges. You squint, guess, and get two wrong.

"Mm," she says. "Do you spend a lot of time on screens?"

You laugh. You're Mira, a freelance UX writer, and you spend nearly all of your time on screens. This is a lunch-break appointment squeezed between a client call and a deadline. Your phone is face-down on your knee, and you can feel it without looking at it.

(Mira is an illustrative character, not a real customer.)

"And how does next year look?"

The optician is only making small talk while she swaps lenses. "Busy year ahead? Work-wise?"

You say, "Yes, fine, busy," because that is what you always say.

But the true answer is that you have no idea. October is full. November is half full. December is a pale grey shape, and 2027 is nothing at all. It isn't empty, exactly. It's just out of focus, like the bottom line on the wall.

You don't say that you've been avoiding the question for weeks. Every time you open a blank sheet to plan the new year, you type the word "Income" in a cell, and then you stop. What would you even put next to it? Some months this year were generous. Some made you check your balance twice. The average of those two feelings isn't a number you can plan with.

"Better or worse?" the optician asks, clicking the lenses.

"Worse," you say. "No. Wait. Better. I don't know."

She smiles. "That's the whole point of the test. Nobody sees clearly until we find the right lens."

The quiet cost of a blurry year

You carry that sentence back to the pavement and onto the tram.

The blur has a cost, and you've been paying it in small ways. You say yes to a project you don't want because the pipeline feels thin. You don't book the week in the mountains your partner keeps mentioning, because what if January is quiet? You take a call on a Friday afternoon you promised yourself off. Every decision gets made from a low hum of worry, not from a plan.

There's an odd shame in it too. Other people seem to have a year. They say "Q2" in conversation like it's a place they've been. You have a feeling about the next six weeks and a prayer about the rest.

You used to think the problem was your income. It's unpredictable, so planning must be pointless, and you'd just have to wait and see. But sitting on the tram, you wonder if that's the wrong way round. Maybe you can't predict the income because you can't see it. And you can't see it because it lives in four places: an inbox, a half-remembered conversation, a folder of old invoices, and your head at 3 am.

The lens, not the crystal ball

At home you make tea and open the laptop, but you don't type "Income" this time. You start with something smaller and kinder: what do I need a normal month to cover? Then: how many weeks do I actually want off? Then: who has said maybe, and who has said yes?

None of it is a prediction. It's just putting the shapes where you can see them. A target that isn't a promise. A line for the weeks you'll be away, drawn first so work has to fit around them, not the other way round. A short list of the conversations that might turn into projects, with honest guesses about how likely each one is.

The odd thing is how quickly the grey shape starts to have edges. The planning doesn't make the year certain. Clients still decide when they pay and when they vanish. But a year with a plan in it can absorb a slow month. A year without one panics at the first quiet week.

And you notice that a range feels better than a number. A good scenario, a modest scenario, and a floor you could live on while you hunt for work. You can sleep next to a floor.

A Monday that feels different

Picture a Monday in February, not this one.

You open one file with your coffee. It takes about ten minutes. You see what you planned for the quarter and what has actually arrived. You see which pipeline conversations moved and which went quiet. You see that the spring break you booked is already sitting there in the plan, protected, with the work arranged around it.

January was thin, and the file shows it. But it doesn't ambush you, because you left room for it back in October. You can look at the gap without the knot in your stomach. You can make a plain decision: send two follow-ups today, or take the smaller job. Either way, it's your choice, made in daylight.

Then you close the laptop and go and do the work you actually like.

Back in the chair

The next week, the optician hands you a prescription and a small cloth.

"Try them for a few days," she says. "Everything will look sharper, and it takes a moment to trust it."

You put the glasses on in the car park and look at the sign across the street. The letters are crisp. You can read the opening hours, the phone number, the tiny line underneath.

You think: I could do this with the year, too.
