Scope Creep: Client Keeps Adding Work? The Campsite Bank Holiday

2026-10-14

The only place with phone signal is the bench by the campsite's recycling bins.

You are standing next to it anyway. It is bank holiday Monday, a quarter past ten. Behind you, someone is frying bacon on a camping stove and the kids are arguing happily about a torch. You told yourself this was a proper day off.

Then the phone buzzed in your pocket.

Let's call you Jonas. You build websites for small businesses, mostly on your own. The message is from a client you like, which makes it worse.

"Hi Jonas! Loving it so far. Quick thing: could we also add a blog section? And maybe a small booking form? Shouldn't take long, right?"

You read it once. Then you read it again, as if the words might rearrange themselves into something smaller.

The "quick thing" that isn't

This is the fourth "quick thing" this month.

The first one was a different font. Fine. The second was an extra page, and you did it that same evening because it seemed easier than discussing it. The third was a rewrite of the contact section after the client's cousin had opinions. You swallowed that one too.

None of them felt big enough to mention. That is exactly how it works. Each request is small, and each yes is only one afternoon. But now you are standing by a bin in walking sandals, doing the sum in your head, and the sum is not small.

You also notice what else is happening. Your chest has gone tight. Your thumbs hover over the screen. Part of you wants to type "Of course! No problem!" because it is the fastest way to make the feeling stop.

Another part of you remembers the quote. You wrote it in a hurry, three weeks ago, in one paragraph. It said "website". It did not say what the website was or wasn't.

So now "just one more thing" has nothing to bump into.

What it quietly costs

Nobody warns you that scope creep isn't really about the work. It is about what you do with the feeling.

You don't resent the blog section. You resent being someone who cannot say, calmly, "That's outside what we agreed. Here's what it would take." You resent the little flinch before every reply. You resent that you have started to read your own client's messages with your shoulders up around your ears.

And there is the money. Not a dramatic loss, just a slow leak. An hour here. An evening there. A project you quoted for twelve days that quietly became seventeen, with the invoice still showing twelve.

You have told yourself it is good for the relationship. Some of it is. But a relationship where only one person is stretching tends to get tired from one side.

The pause on the bench

So you do something unusual. You don't answer.

You put the phone face down on the bench, walk back to the tent, and drink a coffee that is slightly too hot. A child hands you a marshmallow. For ten minutes the message just sits there, and the world does not end.

While you stir your coffee, a thought arrives that is almost embarrassingly simple: you do not need to be braver. You need the words to already exist.

The problem on that bench was never that you don't know your own boundaries. You do. The problem was that, standing there with bacon smoke in the air and a client waiting, you had to invent a polite, firm, friendly sentence from scratch. Under pressure. On your day off.

Most people cannot do that well. Most people say yes.

A different Monday

Picture the same message arriving on an ordinary Tuesday, in a version of your working life where this is already handled.

You read the request. You feel the old flicker, but it passes quickly, because you know what you will do. You open a reply that already has a calm shape. It thanks the client warmly. It says the blog and the booking form are good ideas. It says, plainly and kindly, that they sit outside the current project, and that you would be happy to put together a short add-on quote.

You change a name, adjust one sentence so it sounds like you, and press send. The whole thing takes four minutes.

Then you close the laptop and keep your lunch break.

What strikes you is how undramatic it is. The client replies, "Sure, send me the quote." Or they say they will leave it for later. Either way the project keeps its edges, your evening keeps its shape, and nobody feels cheated. You feel like someone running a small, real business, because you are.

That is all the relief really is: a boundary that doesn't cost you an hour of dread every time.

Back at the bench

In our little story, Jonas picks up the phone again after the coffee. He writes something short and kind. He says he'd love to look at the blog and the booking form as a next step, and that he'll send a quick quote after the holiday. He adds a smiley, because he means it.

He presses send and puts the phone in the glovebox, where it belongs.

The afternoon goes to a lake, a very bad game of cards and a sunburnt nose. It is not perfect. But for the first time in weeks, a bank holiday feels like one.

If you saw yourself on that bench

If that tight chest and hovering thumb felt familiar, you are not bad at this. You were just never given the sentences.
