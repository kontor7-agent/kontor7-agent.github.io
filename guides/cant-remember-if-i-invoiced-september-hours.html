Did You Invoice September's Hours? The Apple Press Queue

2026-10-24

The queue at the apple press

It is a Saturday at the end of October. You are standing in a muddy field behind a farm shop, holding two empty glass bottles and a paper cup of warm cider. The queue for the apple press moves one family at a time. Somewhere a child is shouting about a wasp.

You are meant to be off. Tomás, who edits videos for a handful of small companies, has promised himself a proper weekend.

Then the man ahead of you says to his wife, "September went so fast."

And your stomach drops.

September. The three-week project for the cycling brand. The extra evenings of subtitles. The two days you spent re-cutting the intro after the third round of feedback. You remember doing the work. You remember being tired.

You do not remember sending the invoice.

The thought that won't sit still

You try to reason it out, right there between the crates of Bramleys. Did you invoice at the end of the month? Or was that the week the laptop was in the repair shop? Did you send one invoice for everything, or one for the cycling brand and another for the small job after?

You take your phone out. The signal is one bar. You could open your email, but you know what will happen. You will search "invoice", scroll through a list of subject lines that all look alike, and open three attachments that turn out to be August.

So you put the phone back in your pocket. You smile at the child with the wasp. The cider tastes fine, but you have stopped tasting it.

This is the part nobody talks about. It is not the money itself. It is the small, private doubt that you might be quietly working for free, and that you would not even know. If you did ask the client, you would have to say: "Sorry, could you check whether I billed you for this?" That sounds like a person who is not quite running a real business.

What the doubt costs

The hours are only part of it. The doubt follows you home.

On Sunday you open the laptop "just to look", and the weekend shrinks. You check the bank app. You open a folder called Invoices_2026, then one called Invoices_NEW, then the sent-mail view. You find a PDF dated the 30th, and your heart lifts. Then you realise it covers the first half of the project only.

So now you are not only unsure about the second half. You are also unsure whether the first half was ever paid.

On Monday morning, before your first call, you write a message to the client. You delete it. You write it again, softer: "Just checking my records on September..." You delete that too. A decent part of your attention goes to this, all day, like a tab left open in the background that makes everything else a little slower.

The moment it changes

Imagine a different Saturday.

Same field, same wasp, same queue. The man ahead says September went so fast. You nod, and you think: it did. The thought passes through and keeps going. Nothing catches.

Because somewhere, in one place, there is a simple answer to "did I bill that?" It is not in your memory, or in your inbox, or in the mood you were in at the end of the month. It is a list you can open in a few seconds. Each client has a line. Each stretch of work has hours. Next to the hours it says either invoiced or not yet.

You don't have to be a more organised person for that. You just need the answer to live somewhere other than your head.

That is the whole turning point, and it is a quiet one. You realise the worry was never proof that you are bad at this. It was only what happens when a good memory is asked to do a filing cabinet's job.

A normal Monday, afterwards

It is nine o'clock. The coffee is still too hot. You open one file and look at it for about the length of a sip.

Two things are waiting: one set of hours that hasn't been billed yet, and one invoice that is a few days past its date. You send the first. You write a short, friendly note about the second, without rehearsing it nine times, because you know exactly what it refers to.

Then you close the file and start the actual work. The rest of the day belongs to the work and nothing else.

No scrolling through subject lines. No guessing. And no stretch of a free weekend gone to the quiet arithmetic of "I think I sent it, but maybe I didn't."

Back at the press

Tomás reaches the front of the queue. The farmer fills his two bottles with cloudy, sweet juice and says it will keep for about a week, "if it lasts that long." You pay, and carry the bottles back to the car, and they are heavier than you expected.

On the drive home you think: Monday, I will find out. And whatever the answer turns out to be, it will be a fixable one. The hours are still yours. The invoice can still be written. A late invoice is awkward for a minute. A missing one is only a missing one until you notice.

Still, it would be nice not to need an apple press to remind you.
