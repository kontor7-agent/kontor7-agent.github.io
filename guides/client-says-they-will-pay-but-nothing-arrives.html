Client Says They'll Pay, Nothing Arrives? The Piano Tuner

2026-10-29

The piano tuner is on his knees in your hallway, one ear almost touching the wood, tapping the same note again and again. It is 10:20 on a Wednesday. You are at the kitchen table with your laptop open, pretending to work.

On the screen is the email you have not sent. It starts with "Just a quick follow-up" and has been rewritten so often that the words have gone slippery.

The invoice is 38 days old. Last Thursday the client wrote: "Payment is going out this week, sorry for the delay!" That was the third "this week" in a row. You thanked them. You even added a smiley.

Nothing has arrived.

The sentence you keep rehearsing

Let's call you Nora. You illustrate for small publishers and a couple of start-ups. The client in question is lovely. Every call has been friendly. That is what makes it so hard. They are not a villain, just a person who keeps saying "soon", and you have no idea what to do with a kind person who does not pay.

So you do what many of us do. You wait one more day, because maybe today is the day. You check your banking app while the kettle boils, in the queue at the bakery, and once more in the bathroom with the door locked, as if the number would change in private.

It costs more than the money. It costs the first hour of every morning. It makes you slightly short with people who have done nothing wrong. It makes you answer new requests from the same client faster than you should, because asking for payment and being difficult feel like the same thing.

And under all of it sits a small, ugly thought: a real business wouldn't be in this position.

What the tuner does

The tuner finishes. He plays a slow chord and nods at it, satisfied. Then he pulls a small card out of his jacket. It has his name, a bank number and a price. He says, "Whenever suits you this week, no rush."

You transfer it on the spot, right there in the hallway, phone in one hand. It takes eleven seconds.

He zips his bag and leaves. Nobody apologised. Nobody said "I hope this is okay." He did a job, he named a price, and he expected to be paid, without any drama.

You stand there with the phone still warm in your hand and realise something uncomfortable. You are not bad at your work. You are not even bad at asking. You are just making it up fresh every single time, alone, at the worst possible moment: when you are anxious and the sentence has to be perfect.

The tuner has a way of doing it. You have a blinking cursor.

The email that finally went out

Back at the table, you look at the draft again. You delete the apology. You delete "I hope you're well" because it was doing the work of a shield. You do not need a shield. You need to be clear, and clear can still be friendly.

You write a few short lines. The invoice number. The date it was due. A date you would like to see the payment by. You read it once, not nine times, and press send before the old feeling can argue.

Then comes the strange part. Your shoulders drop an inch. Not because anything has been paid, but because something has been done. The ball is on the other side of the net, and you know when you will look at it again. Until then, you do not have to.

The client replies that afternoon. Not a miracle, just a normal human answer with a date in it, and an apology that sounds like a real one. You do not need to read it five times to find the tone.

A different Monday

Picture the Monday after that. Coffee, window open, the street still quiet. You open one page and look for ten minutes. You can see which invoices are open, which are comfortably within their dates, and which have crossed the line. Next to the late ones sits the next small step, already decided. A friendly nudge here. A firmer note there. Nothing that needs a mood or a courageous morning.

You send what needs sending. You close the page. You go and draw.

Nobody has become a debt collector, and you have not lost a single client's goodwill. You have simply stopped carrying the whole thing in your head, and the knot in your stomach, the one that arrived with the 38th day, has no reason to come back.

That is all a simple system changes. It does not make clients pay faster by magic. It takes the dread out of the asking, because the words and the next step are already there, waiting, the way the tuner's card was in his jacket.
