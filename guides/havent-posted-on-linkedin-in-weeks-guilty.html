Haven't Posted on LinkedIn in Weeks? The Pottery Stall Ask

2026-10-27

It's 8:20 on a Tuesday, and you're standing in the queue at the farmers' market, holding a paper bag of plums, when a woman you barely know says it.

"I keep seeing your name pop up. Oh, wait. Actually, I haven't seen you in a while, have I?"

She smiles. She means nothing by it.

You smile back. Something small and cold turns over in your stomach.

Because she's right. You last posted on LinkedIn in the first week of September. It's nearly November.

The number you don't want to look up

Let's call you Mara. You're an illustrative character, a freelance illustrator, six years in, working from a corner of the spare room. You're good at what you do. Clients say so, quietly, by coming back.

But at the market, in the queue, you do the maths you've been avoiding. Seven weeks. Maybe eight.

There was no decision behind it. You didn't choose to stop. A project ran long, then a deadline, then a week when everything felt slightly too loud. Each day the post got a little heavier. Each day it seemed to need to be better, because it had been so long.

Now a silence that began as nothing has grown a shape. It has a name. Guilt.

What the silence is quietly costing

Nobody tells you this part. The guilt isn't really about LinkedIn.

It's the small voice that says: a real business would be visible. It's the thought that a past client might wonder if you've closed up shop. It's the little flinch when someone in your field announces a new project and you realise you haven't announced anything.

And it's the Sunday evening dread. You open the app, you stare at the empty box, and you close it again. You tell yourself you'll do it properly tomorrow. Tomorrow is another Sunday.

The cost isn't dramatic. It's a low hum. It's the energy you spend avoiding a thing that would take twenty minutes if you hadn't made it enormous.

The call that changed the question

A week later you're on a video call with a client, a small bakery group you did a label series for last spring. The work is done and paid. This call is just a check-in.

At the end, the owner says, "We're planning something for the new year. I was going to message you. I wasn't sure if you were still taking work."

You laugh, a little too fast. "Of course. Yes."

She didn't see anything wrong. She wasn't judging. She simply hadn't seen you lately, and so she'd wondered.

After you hang up, you sit with the closed laptop for a minute. The room is quiet. The kettle clicks off in the kitchen.

And you notice that the problem was never that you had nothing to say. You'd shown that label series to three people just last week, out loud, with real pride. You have plenty to say. What you've been missing is somewhere for it to land before the Sunday panic arrives.

It doesn't have to be a performance

Here's the thought that arrives, almost gently: what if posting wasn't a mood you had to wait for?

What if the next four weeks were already sketched out, loosely, in one calm place? A project you finished. A thing you learned. A small behind-the-scenes photo of the desk. Not a campaign. Just a rhythm that doesn't depend on how you feel on any given Sunday.

You wouldn't need to catch up on the seven weeks. You wouldn't need a grand return post. You'd simply need the next small thing, and the one after.

That's the whole shift. Guilt thrives on a blank page. It loses most of its grip when there's already something written in the next few boxes.

A Monday that feels different

Picture it a few weeks on.

It's Monday, 9:05. Coffee beside the keyboard, the radiator ticking as it warms. You open one file and see the week laid out. Tuesday has a short post about the label series. Thursday has a photo you took last month and forgot about. Friday is empty on purpose.

You tweak one caption, change a word, and move on. Eight minutes, maybe ten.

Then you open the real work, the client work, with a quiet head. No background hum. No low-grade shame stuck to the edge of the day.

When someone at the market says, "I keep seeing your work," you just say thank you.

If you recognise yourself in Mara

If it's been weeks and the guilt has started to feel like part of the furniture, you're not lazy and you're not bad at this. You just don't have a place where showing up is already half done.
