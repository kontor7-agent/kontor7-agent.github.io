SOPs Take Forever to Write? The Picnic Table Phone Call

2026-10-27

Hanna had carried the laptop to the lake "just in case."

It was Thursday, a little after eleven. The picnic table was warm, the water was flat and blue, and her phone had one bar of signal if she held it near the bin. She was on holiday. That was the plan, anyway.

The message from her brother-in-law Jonas arrived while she was peeling an orange.

"Sorry! How do you wrap the big ferns again? And a woman wrote that a leaf is yellow. What do I say?"

Hanna put the orange down.

She sells houseplants online, mostly alone. For three days Jonas had been packing parcels in her garage so she could finally rest. She had shown him everything on Sunday, walking around the shelves and talking fast. He had nodded a lot. He had written nothing down. Neither had she.

The document with three lines in it

She knew exactly which file she should have opened. It was called How we do things. She had created it in March.

She opened it now, balancing the laptop between a sun hat and a bag of crisps. Three lines, then a blank page.

1. Orders come in. 2. Check stock. 3. Pack.

That was the whole procedure. Every Sunday evening since March, she had promised herself she would sit down and write it properly. Every time, she got as far as "Pack." Then she wondered where to begin. With the box sizes? The paper? The heat-pack rule for winter? The right words for the customer who is cross but polite?

She would stare at it. Then she would remember an invoice, or a supplier email, or dinner. She would close the laptop and tell herself that real businesses have this sorted.

That last thought was the heavy one. Real businesses have this sorted.

What it costs to keep it all in your head

Nobody warns you that being the only person who knows how things are done is its own kind of tiring.

It means you can't be ill for long. You can't take a week off without a phone in your pocket. When someone offers to help, you feel grateful and slightly dreadful, because explaining takes longer than doing it yourself. So you do it yourself. Again.

Hanna typed a reply to Jonas with her thumbs, slowly, like a person dictating a will:

"Big ferns: the tall cardboard sleeve, not the flat box. Paper first, then... hang on, I'll send a photo."

There was no photo. The ferns were in the garage and she was by a lake.

She typed it all out anyway, in nine messages, with the signal blinking. The yellow leaf answer took four rewrites, because she wanted it kind but not grovelling. By the time she was done, the orange was warm and a family in the next bay had started their barbecue.

She wasn't angry with Jonas. She was angry at the blank page. It felt as if everyone else could just write things down, and she had forgotten how.

The sentence that changed the question

Later that afternoon, with her feet in the water, she scrolled past something a friend had shared. It was a post about using AI for the dull first draft of business paperwork. Not to run anything, only to get words onto the page.

She almost scrolled past it. She had tried an AI chat once and got something so generic it could have been about any company on earth. Ensure customer satisfaction at all times. She had closed that tab with a small sigh.

But one line stayed with her. The point wasn't that the machine knows your business. It was that you start from something already shaped, fill in what only you know, and fix what's wrong. Fixing is a very different job from inventing.

She thought about the three lines in her document. Writing them had never been the problem. The problem was that every Sunday she tried to produce a perfect finished manual from nothing, in one sitting. No wonder it never happened.

What if the first version could simply be wrong in the right shape? She could cross out the bits that weren't hers and keep the rest.

The water was cold around her ankles. For the first time since March, the document didn't feel like a verdict on her. It felt like a draft.

A different Monday

She didn't solve anything at the lake. She ate a sandwich, swam badly, and read half a novel.

But she did picture the Monday afterwards. It looked like this.

The coffee is still hot. A folder on the desktop holds a handful of short pages, each one small enough to read in two minutes. Packing the big ones. Replying about a damaged leaf. What to do when a courier loses a box. They don't sound like a corporate handbook. They sound like her, only tidier.

When Jonas, or a friend, or some future helper asks a question, she says, "It's in the folder, page three," and goes back to her tea. Nobody has to hold the whole business in their head. Nobody has to stand in a garage guessing.

She wouldn't be "a real business" because of the folder. But she would be a person who can take a Thursday off without a phone in her hand.

Where this leaves you

If so, it isn't laziness. A blank page is a lot to ask of a tired person.
