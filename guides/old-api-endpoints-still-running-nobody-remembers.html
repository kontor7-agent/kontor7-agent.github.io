Old API Endpoints Nobody Remembers? The Loft Full of Cables

2026-11-05

Saturday, 10:20 in the morning. You are kneeling in your parents' loft with a torch in your teeth and a cardboard box in your lap.

The box is full of cables. Black ones, white ones, a flat grey one with a plug you have never seen. Nothing is labelled. Your dad, from the bottom of the ladder, says what dads say: "Keep that one, it might be important."

You ask what it belongs to. He doesn't know. Nobody does.

Your phone buzzes in your back pocket. It's Tomas, the founder of the small booking platform you've built the backend for these past three years. You're a freelancer, so Saturdays are supposed to be yours, but you answer anyway.

The line in the log nobody can explain

"Sorry to bother you. Our new part-time developer was reading the server logs and asked about something called /api/v1/export-all. Something is still calling it. Do you remember what it does?"

You sit back on a joist. Dust floats through the torch beam.

Export-all. You think you remember. A quick hack, maybe, from the first month, when a client wanted all their bookings in one file before the proper reports existed. Or was it the thing you built for the migration? Was it behind a login? You really are not sure.

"I think it's old," you say. "I'll check on Monday."

That's the honest answer, and it sits badly in your chest all the same.

What the cables taught you

You carry the box downstairs. In the kitchen your mum has made tea, and you tip the cables onto the table, a small tangled pile of "it might be important."

Here's the thing about the pile. Every single cable was once somebody's sensible decision. Someone needed it, plugged it in, and it worked. Then the device went away, the cable stayed, and the memory of what it was for quietly left with the device.

Nobody did anything wrong. That's what makes it hard to throw anything out.

Your API is the same kind of box. A route added on a busy Thursday. A test endpoint that was only going to live for a week. A version one that version two was going to replace "soon." Each one made sense at the time. None of them came with a label.

And what you feel, sipping your tea, isn't really fear. It's a low, steady unease. A sense that there are doors in your own house you can't name, and that you are the person who is supposed to know.

The part nobody says out loud

You're a freelancer. There's no security team down the corridor. No colleague to say "oh, that one, yes, that's the old reporting thing." There's you, the repo, and your memory of three years of decisions.

So the cost of the unlabelled box isn't dramatic. It's small and constant. It's the pause before you tell a client "yes, we're tidy." It's the Sunday afternoon where you open the project, scroll the routes, and close it again because you don't know where to begin. It's the awkward moment when Tomas's new developer asks a perfectly reasonable question and you answer with "I think."

You don't want to say "I think" about your own work. You want to say "yes, I checked, and here is the list."

The turning point, at the kitchen table

By the second cup of tea, something shifts. You stop trying to remember and start thinking about how a careful person would handle this if they weren't relying on memory at all.

Not a heroic weekend of rewriting. Just a list. Every route written down, one line each. Who is meant to call it? Does it need a login? Does it return more than it should? Is it still needed, yes or no? A simple pass, the same questions asked of every door, so that nothing depends on whether you happen to recall it.

The unease turns out not to be about the old endpoint. It's about not having a routine for asking the questions. Once you see that, the knot loosens a little. A routine is something you can build. A bad memory is not.

You wrap the cables in a bag for the recycling centre. The grey one you test on an old router, and it fits. Somehow that feels like a good omen.

A different Monday

Picture the next Monday, then. It's 9:15 and the coffee is still hot. You open the project with a list beside it, not a feeling. You go down the routes one by one, and each gets a tick or a note. This one is current. That one has no reason to exist, so it goes. This one stays, but it needs a login, so you write that down for later.

/api/v1/export-all turns out to be exactly what you half-remembered: an old convenience route. You retire it, write one line about why, and send Tomas a short message. "Found it, explained it, closed it."

That's the whole Monday. Nothing exploded. Your shoulders are lower than they were on Saturday. When his new developer asks the next question, you have a document to point to, not a guess.

And the next launch, the one you used to dread at half past eleven the night before, becomes a normal afternoon with a checklist.
