Monthly Retainer: How Much to Charge? The Pot on the Stove

2026-11-06

The pasta water is just coming to the boil when the call comes in.

It's Monday, 11:15. You work from the kitchen table, so lunch and work share a room. Tove, a client you've done three small projects for, sounds cheerful. You're a freelance web designer, and she has good news.

"We love working with you. Could we make it a regular thing? Like a monthly retainer? Just so we always have you on call."

Your stomach does two things at once. It lifts, because regular work means a calmer calendar. Then it drops, because the next sentence is obvious.

"What would that look like for you? What would you charge?"

The number that wasn't there

You open your mouth. Nothing useful is in there.

You know what you charge per project. You know roughly what you'd like to earn. But a monthly retainer is a different animal. How many hours is "on call"? What happens in a month when she sends nothing? What about the month she sends everything at once?

The pot starts to rattle its lid.

"Let me think about the best way to structure it," you hear yourself say. "I'll send you something this week."

"Perfect!" says Tove. "No rush."

There is always a rush. You just can't see it yet.

What you do with the pasta

You turn the heat down and stand at the counter, not eating.

The thoughts come in a queue. If you ask for too much, she'll say no and you lose a client you like. If you ask for too little, you'll be stuck with it for a year, quietly resenting every Tuesday email that starts with "quick question".

You think about a friend who took a retainer once and called it "a nice little number". Six months later she was answering messages on Sunday afternoons for the same nice little number.

So you do what many people do. You open a browser tab and type: client wants monthly retainer how much should I charge freelance.

The answers are all confident and all different. One says a flat percentage on top of your usual rate. Another says never discount. A third has a table with numbers that belong to somebody else's life. None of them knows what your rent is, how many weeks you want off, or what you need in a thin month.

You close the tab. The pasta is soft now, past the point you like.

The question underneath the question

Around two o'clock, after you've eaten standing up, something shifts.

The real question was never "what do retainers cost?" It was "what do I cost?"

You can't price a month of someone else's access to you if you don't know what an hour of you has to cover. Tax. Unpaid admin. The days nobody pays for. The holiday you'd like to take without checking your phone on a beach.

You realise you've been quoting from a feeling. A feeling that says "this seems fair", with nothing behind it. A feeling is easy to talk down. It folds the moment a client sounds mildly disappointed.

A number you've worked out yourself doesn't fold. It just sits there, calmly.

The same call, a different Monday

Picture the same phone call, but you already know your floor. The lowest hourly and daily rate that keeps your business honest, written down and worked out on a day when nobody was asking.

"What would you charge?" Tove asks.

The pot is still on the stove. But you don't flinch.

"Let me look at what you'd typically need from me," you say. "I'll send a clear proposal by Thursday. A set number of hours each month, a price, and what happens when you need more."

That's not a trick. It's a sentence you can say because you aren't guessing. The offer might still need adjusting. Tove might counter. You might say no to a version that doesn't work.

But you'd be negotiating from solid ground, not from a wobble in your chest.

Afterwards, you'd eat your lunch sitting down. The pasta would be fine.

Starting from your own floor

You don't need a pricing course or a retainer template with someone else's numbers in it. You need your own minimum, the one thing every proposal can be measured against. Once you have it, "how much for a monthly retainer?" becomes a smaller, friendlier question: how many hours, at what rate, and is that worth it to me?

And a clean invoice at the end of each month, with the amount and the due date plain on the page, helps the arrangement feel like a proper business, not a favour.
