Ordered What You Ran Out Of? The Lighthouse Stairs

2026-10-23

It is a Wednesday, your first day off in three weeks, and you are on step 87 of 112.

The lighthouse smells of cold stone and old paint. A school group is somewhere above you, shrieking happily. Your partner is ahead, one hand on the rail, saying something about the view.

Your phone buzzes in your back pocket. You don't want to look. You look.

New order: 6 × Smoked Chilli Jam, 14 jars total for the Harbour Deli.

Your stomach drops one step lower than your feet.

Smoked Chilli Jam is your best seller. And you are fairly sure, standing there with your hand on a stone wall worn smooth by a century of other hands, that the last jar left the shelf on Saturday.

The arithmetic you do on the stairs

You don't know. That is the worst part.

You think it was Saturday. You think there might be a box in the garage. You think the supplier said something about peppers being late, but was that this batch or the last?

So you stand in a stairwell doing sums in your head. The deli wants delivery Friday. A new batch needs two days, if the peppers arrive. If they don't arrive, you are writing an email that begins with "unfortunately".

You have written that email before. You know exactly how it feels to type the word, and how it feels to be the person who typed it.

What it quietly costs

The order itself is not the real loss. It is everything around it.

It is the view you don't see, because the sea is a blur behind the thought of the garage. It is the lunch you eat half-listening. It is your partner asking, "Everything okay?" and you saying "Fine", the way people do when it isn't.

It is also a small, private feeling that you run a business the way you run a kitchen drawer. Things go in. Things come out. Nobody is quite sure what is in there.

You are good at the making. You are proud of the jars, the labels, the way the deli owner once said they sold faster than anything else on the shelf. But somewhere between making and selling there is a gap, and the numbers fall into it.

So you find out you're out of stock the way you always find out. A customer tells you. Or a customer orders, and you realise too late.

At the top

The lamp room is bright and windy. Your partner points at a ferry crawling across the horizon. You nod. You take a photo you will never look at.

Then you do the only thing you can do. You open the notes app on your phone, where "stock" lives in a list called jam things. The last entry is from two weeks ago. It says "approx 20 left?" with the question mark you added because you weren't sure even then.

That question mark is the whole problem, sitting there in your own handwriting.

Standing at the top of a lighthouse, with the wind pushing your hair across your face, a plain thought arrives. It is not a dramatic thought. It is almost gentle.

It doesn't have to be like this. I should just be able to look and know.

Not a better memory. Not a more careful version of you. Just one place where the numbers live, where what came in and what went out are written down, and where something taps you on the shoulder before the shelf is empty rather than after.

A different Wednesday

Imagine the same lighthouse. The same wind. The same school group, still shrieking.

Your phone buzzes. New order: 14 jars, Harbour Deli.

You look at the screen for a moment, and then you already know the answer. Last Thursday, a quiet little flag had told you the jam was running low. You had put in the pepper order that evening, in about two minutes, with your tea going cold beside you. The batch is cooking on Thursday. The order is fine.

You reply from the lamp room: Yes, Friday works. You put the phone away and look at the sea properly, because there is nothing left to work out.

That is all. No grand transformation. Just a day off that stays a day off, and an order that feels like good news again instead of an accusation.

On a normal Monday it feels like this too. You open one sheet, you see what is low, you see what it's worth sitting on the shelf, and you close it. Ten minutes, perhaps. Then you get on with the part you actually love.
