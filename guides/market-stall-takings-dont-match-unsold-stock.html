Takings Don't Match Unsold Stock? The Church Hall Key

2026-10-29

The caretaker is standing in the doorway of the church hall, jangling his keys. He isn't rude about it. He just does it a little louder every minute.

It's 4:25 on a Sunday. The Christmas market has been over for twenty minutes. You are folding the trestle cloth with one hand and holding the cash box against your hip with the other.

Three crates of chutney are left, plus one of the small gift sets. You count them twice, and the number doesn't feel right.

You started the day with a list on the back of an envelope. You remember writing it. You don't remember where it went.

The sum you do standing up

So you do it in your head, the way you always do.

You took forty-eight jars this morning. Or was it fifty-two? You sold, what, thirty? Thirty-something. The gift sets went well. A man bought three and asked for a discount, and you said yes because he was nice and it was nearly closing time.

You open the cash box and count the notes. Then the coins, in little towers on the lid. Then you count them again.

It's less than it should be. Or maybe it's right and your idea of "should" is wrong. You honestly can't tell, and that is the part that sits in your stomach.

There was a card reader today too, and its payments will show up later in a different app. The notebook with the Saturday figures is in the van. The jars you gave away as tasters were never written down anywhere.

The caretaker clears his throat.

"Two minutes," you say, and smile, and feel your face doing it.

The drive home

The van smells of vinegar and cardboard. You put the cash box on the passenger seat and the crates in the back, and you sit for a moment with the engine off.

Nobody talks about this part of running a stall. They talk about the table display, the sign, the lovely lights. They don't talk about sitting in a car park at half past four, unable to say whether today was a good day.

Because it felt like a good day. People stopped. People tasted. A woman said her mother used to make something just like your plum one, and she bought two.

But feeling good and being paid are different things. Somewhere between the jars you made, the fees you paid, the pitch you booked and the hours you stood there, there is a real number. You haven't met it in a while.

You suspect you're afraid to.

Last month you told yourself the Sunday market "basically covers itself." You said it to your sister on the phone, the way you'd say a thing you hoped was true. She said, "Great!" and you changed the subject.

The quiet cost

The cost isn't dramatic. That's why it lasts.

It's the evening you spend at the kitchen table with a calculator and the envelope, trying to rebuild a day from memory. It's the Monday you don't restock the best seller because you're not sure it is the best seller. It's the tasters and discounts and small kindnesses that all vanish into the same fog.

And it's the creeping thought that other makers must have this figured out, and you are somehow just winging it with nicer labels.

You are not winging it. You are good at the making, the talking, the standing in the cold. Nobody ever gave you a calm place to put the numbers.

A different end of day

Picture the same church hall, the same keys, the same 4:25.

This time you fold the cloth without hurrying. The float you started with is one line. The cash in the box is one line. The stock you carried in and the stock you carry out are two numbers you wrote down as you went, not after the fact.

The gap, if there is one, is small and visible. Maybe it's the tasters. Maybe it's the man with the three gift sets. Either way it has a name, and a name is much easier to carry than a fog.

You say goodnight to the caretaker and mean it.

Back home, you don't open a calculator. You put the kettle on and fill in a few cells while it boils, and a picture appears of what the day actually did. It shows which jar earned its place on the table and which one is just taking up room. It shows what the pitch really cost you, and what your own hours were worth.

It takes about as long as the tea does. Then you close the laptop. It's still light enough to see the garden.

On Monday morning you know what to cook, what to reorder and what to quietly stop making. When someone at the next market asks for a custom set, you don't guess. You say a price, calmly, and it's yours.
