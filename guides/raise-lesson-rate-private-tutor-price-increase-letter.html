Raise Your Lesson Rate Without Dread: The Piano Stool at 3:40

2026-10-14

The kettle in the hallway of Number 9 clicks off, and Marta is still sitting on the piano stool with her phone in her hand.

It's 3:40 on a Wednesday. Her next student arrives at four. On the screen is a draft message that begins, "Hi both, I hope you're well, I just wanted to mention, and I completely understand if..."

She has been trying to write this message since September.

This is an illustration, not a real person. But you may know her.

The number that never moved

Marta teaches piano to adults and a few teenagers. Her rate is the same as when she started four years ago. Since then, the room she rents has gone up. So has the bus pass, the sheet music, the electricity bill for the keyboard she practises on at home.

She knows the lesson fee is too low. She knows it the way you know a tooth is sore: you just avoid chewing on that side.

So she doesn't think about it. She smiles at the end of each lesson, takes the payment, and says "see you next week."

The quiet cost doesn't show up as one big loss. It shows up as a faint resentment on Thursday evenings, when she's tired and still has three people to schedule.

The draft that wouldn't send

She has written the price-rise message maybe six times. Each version fails in a different way.

One sounds like an apology. One sounds like a legal notice. One starts warm and ends with a paragraph about "market conditions" that she doesn't believe herself.

And behind every draft sits the same small thought: what if they leave?

Not all of them. Just the one who's been with her longest, who brings her a jar of plum jam at Christmas. The one who always pays a few days late and always says sorry.

There's a second worry underneath, one she rarely admits. Last month a student cancelled forty minutes before the lesson. Marta said, "No problem at all!" and then sat in the empty room for an hour doing nothing, telling herself it was fine.

It wasn't fine. But there was no rule to point to, so there was nothing to say.

The sentence she couldn't find

The doorbell goes at four. A student comes in, coat half off, already talking about the hard bar in the left hand.

Marta teaches. She is good at this part. Her voice is steady when she says, "Slower. Listen to the bass." She never hesitates about what a student needs to hear.

It's only when the topic is her own time that the words go missing.

After the lesson, walking him to the door, she thinks: why can I say a hard thing about his playing and not about my price?

The answer, she realises, isn't courage. She doesn't lack nerve. She lacks the first sentence. Once there's a first sentence, the rest follows, the way a piece does once you find the opening bar.

Something that already exists

That evening she doesn't open the draft again. She thinks about what would help instead.

Not a lecture on pricing. Not another article telling her to "know her worth." Just a few well-made starting points in a calm, kind tone, which she could bend into her own voice. One for the long-standing student. One for the new enquiry. One for the reply that says "oh, that's a bit more than I expected." One for the person who's always late with payment. One for the cancellation rule she's never quite stated out loud.

And somewhere to keep track of who's been told what, and when the new rate starts, so it doesn't live only in her head at 3:40 on a Wednesday.

That's all. A little scaffolding for a conversation she already knows she needs to have.

A different Wednesday

Picture the same hallway a few weeks later.

The letters went out on a Sunday morning with a coffee beside her, not at midnight with a racing heart. Two students said "of course." One asked whether the new rate began in January, and Marta had the answer ready. One went quiet for a day and then wrote back, "Thanks for letting me know."

Nobody exploded. The world kept its shape.

The cancellation rule now sits at the bottom of her booking message, one friendly paragraph, written once. When someone cancels late, she doesn't need a speech. She just sends the line she already wrote.

At 3:40 she's on the stool again. But this time she's running a scale, warming up, not rehearsing an apology. The kettle clicks off in the hallway. The afternoon feels like hers.

She isn't richer in some dramatic way. She's lighter. The sore tooth has stopped aching because she finally stopped avoiding it.
