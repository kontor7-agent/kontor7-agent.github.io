Client Asked to Cut Your Price? The Stairwell Landing

2026-10-19

Mara is standing on a stairwell landing between two conference sessions, phone in one hand, a paper cup of lukewarm coffee in the other. Someone laughs on the floor below. A lanyard swings past.

The email arrived at 11:42. She reads it for the third time.

"Thanks again for the quote! We're really excited. Our budget is a bit tighter than expected though. Is there any chance you could do it for a bit less?"

Three weeks ago, she sent a number. She had sat with it. She had checked it twice and then hit send with her stomach tight. The client wrote back, "Looks great, let's go." She had felt a small, private victory.

Now the number is soft again, like it was never real.

The reply that writes itself, badly

She has already drafted three answers in her head.

The first one says of course, no problem. It's fast, friendly and cheap. It also means she'd spend the next six weeks quietly resenting a logo.

The second one is a long explanation. Her hours, her experience, why the price is the price. She can feel how defensive it sounds before she types a word.

The third one is a flat "no". It feels clean for about four seconds. Then she imagines the silence that follows, and the project drifting away.

She puts the phone in her pocket and takes a sip of coffee that tastes like paper.

The worst part isn't the money, or not only. It's the little voice that says: a real business would know what to say here. A real business would have a sentence ready. She has a laptop bag and a feeling.

What it quietly costs

You may know this landing.

You quote. You say yes to the work. And then, after the yes, the price gets another round. It comes softly, wrapped in "just checking" and "is there any flexibility", and it lands right when you've already mentally spent the money.

So you hesitate. You answer too late, or too fast. You give a discount you never decided to give. Or you hold your ground in a tone that sounds sharper than you meant, and you lie awake wondering if you've ruined it.

Nobody sees that part. They see a polite email thread. You carry the afternoon of doubt, the lunch you don't taste, the conference talk you half-hear because your mind is still composing sentences.

And under it all sits the old fear: if I say no, I'll lose the job. If I say yes, I'll lose something else.

The pause on the landing

A colleague from the design community walks up the stairs, sees her face and stops. They've known each other for years, the kind of friendship made of nods at events.

"You look like someone just asked you for a discount," she says.

Mara laughs, a bit too loudly. "After I'd quoted. After they'd agreed."

"Ah." The colleague leans on the railing. "That one. I used to answer those the second they came in. Now I let them sit while I get a coffee."

"And then what do you say?"

"Honestly? Nothing clever. I just don't write it fresh every time. I don't start from the knot in my stomach. I start from something calm that's already half there." She shrugs. "A no can be kind. It can even be warm. It just helps if it isn't written at 11:42 on a staircase."

Then the next session bell goes, and she's gone.

Mara stays one more minute. The sentence stays with her: start from something calm that's already half there.

She had been treating every difficult email like a new exam. Every price question, every scope wobble, every awkward ask: a blank page and her pulse. As if nobody before her had ever needed to say, politely, this is the price, and here is what I can adjust.

A different Thursday

Imagine the same email, the same Thursday, a few weeks later.

Mara reads it. The knot appears, briefly, because it's a body thing and bodies are slow to learn. But then she opens a document where the words are already waiting. A friendly opening. A clear sentence about the quoted price. An offer to adjust the scope instead of the number, if that helps with the budget. A warm close.

She changes the client's name. She softens one line, sharpens another, makes it sound like her. It takes six minutes.

She sends it, finishes her coffee, and actually hears the afternoon talk.

Maybe the client says okay. Maybe they trim the project. Maybe they walk away, and that is allowed too. The difference is that she answered as someone who runs a business, not as someone hoping to be forgiven for having a price.

The number stays real. Her evening stays hers.
