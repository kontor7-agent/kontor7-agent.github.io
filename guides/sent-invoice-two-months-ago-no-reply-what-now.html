Invoice Sent Two Months Ago, No Reply? The Radiator Key

2026-10-19

The radiator key is stuck in your hand and a thin hiss is coming out of the valve. It is Monday, 8:40 am, in the spare room that doubles as your office. October has finally turned cold.

Your phone buzzes on the windowsill. It is an old calendar reminder you set in a hopeful mood: Invoice 2041, Hartmann & Weiss, follow up?

You read it twice.

That invoice went out in mid-August. A brand identity, three rounds of revisions, a logo pack you were proud of. You sent the PDF with a friendly line about how much you enjoyed the project. They replied with a thumbs-up on the logo files. Then nothing.

Two months of nothing.

The silence has a sound

You know the kind of thought that comes next. Maybe it got lost. Maybe it went to spam. Maybe someone is on leave. Maybe they're annoyed and haven't said so. Maybe I should have been clearer about the payment terms.

The radiator hisses a bit more. You stand there with the key and run the loop again.

You don't want to be the person who nags. Writing "just checking in" feels like knocking on a door that has already been answered with silence. Asking for money you have already earned somehow feels like asking for a favour.

So you do what many self-employed people do. You tell yourself it will sort itself out, and you go and make coffee.

But it sits in the background all day. At the supermarket queue at lunchtime, you check your banking app, just in case. Nothing. In the evening you check it again, though you know a payment would have sent a notification.

What the waiting quietly costs

The money itself is only part of it, though it matters. It is a rent month, or most of one.

The rest is harder to name. It is the low hum of not knowing. It is taking on a smaller project than you wanted because you can't quite trust the cash. It is feeling like a hobbyist who is allowed to do the work but not allowed to be paid for it on time.

And there is a second worry, the one you don't say out loud: is this the only one?

Because you are not sure. There was that workshop invoice in September. There was a small copy job in July. You think they paid. You are fairly sure. But "fairly sure" is not a feeling you want to build a business on.

The folder you open at 9:15

You finally sit down. You open the folder where the invoices live, the one with files named invoice_final, invoice_final_v2 and Rechnung_neu.

You scroll. You open a bank statement in one window and an old email thread in another. You start matching amounts with your eyes, a finger on the screen, like checking a till receipt.

The hiss from the radiator has stopped, and you hardly notice. The loop in your head has moved to a different question: how did I end up doing detective work on my own income?

Nothing here is a disaster. That is what is so draining. It is just a hundred small uncertainties, and each one wants a little attention you were going to give to the work.

The turning point is a small one

Imagine a different Monday.

The same radiator, the same cold morning. The reminder on your phone buzzes. This time you don't read it with a sinking feeling, because you already know what it says. You open one file and the overview is right there: who has paid, who is open, which invoice is now a few weeks past its due date, and since when.

No scrolling through bank statements. No guessing. One row for Hartmann & Weiss, quietly marked as overdue, with the date you sent it and the date it was due.

That changes the email you write. You are no longer a person wondering whether you might be allowed to ask. You are someone with a clear record, writing a calm, factual note: here is the invoice, here is the due date, could you let me know when to expect payment? It is two sentences and no apology. You press send, and your shoulders come down an inch.

A system like that does not make clients pay faster by magic, and it does not decide anything for you. What it does is take the guessing out of the picture, so the awkward part shrinks to what it really is: a normal message between two businesses.

The rest of the day

By 9:30 you are working on a pitch, not on a bank statement. The invoice question has a home now, with a date, a status and a next step. It no longer lives in your chest.

At lunch, the queue at the supermarket is just a queue. You don't pull out your phone to check anything. You think about what to cook.

It is not a dramatic change. Nobody would see it from the outside. But a quiet part of you has stopped holding its breath.
