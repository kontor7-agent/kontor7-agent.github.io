Shop Keeps Overselling Stock? The Wedding Pew Buzz

2026-10-15

The organist has just started the second hymn when your phone buzzes against your leg.

You are in the fourth pew at your cousin's wedding. You are wearing the good shoes. The phone is in your jacket pocket, on silent, and still it buzzes, a small insistent shudder you can feel in your hip.

You know what it is. You don't have to look.

You look anyway.

New order: 2 x Moss, hand-dyed merino, 100 g.

Your stomach drops, quietly, like a lift between floors. You haven't had Moss since the Thursday before last. You remember the last skein going into a padded envelope. You remember thinking, I'll update the shop tonight.

That was nine days ago.

The hymn goes on without you

Everyone around you is singing. You are doing sums in your head.

The shop says three skeins of Moss are available. You think the true number is zero. Or maybe one, if the batch you put aside for the market is still on the shelf. You can't remember which shelf. You can't remember whether you counted it, or only meant to.

So you imagine the apology email. You've written it before. So sorry, there's been a mix-up with stock. I'll refund you straight away, or I could dye a new batch, it'll take about three weeks.

It is a perfectly polite email. It is also the third one this month.

Nobody warns you about that part of running a small shop. The selling is the easy bit. It is the quiet, constant doubt afterwards: is what the website says the same as what is actually in the box under the stairs?

What it costs, in small coins

It doesn't cost you in one big way. It costs you in small coins.

A refund, and the fee that doesn't come back. A customer who says "no worries!" in a tone that means I'll buy somewhere else next time. A review you never see, because the person simply doesn't return.

And it costs you the feeling of being a proper business. You wanted a shop people could trust. Instead you are a person who checks her phone at a wedding with her heart in her throat.

After the ceremony, in the churchyard, everyone is throwing confetti. Your aunt hugs you and says, "You look tired, love." You say you're fine. You are standing in the sunshine with a paper cone of rose petals, mentally walking through your stockroom, shelf by shelf, trying to recall it.

The number you keep in your head

Here is what you realise on the walk to the reception, a little behind the others because of the shoes.

Your stock lives in your memory. Some of it is in a notebook with a coffee ring. Some of it is in the shop settings. Some of it is just a feeling: I think I've got plenty of Heather. None of these talk to each other, and none of them is wrong on purpose. They are just never all right on the same day.

The problem was never that you're careless. You are a person who dyes wool at six in the morning and wraps parcels by hand. The problem is that you were asked to be a warehouse, a supplier call and a counting machine as well, with nothing but your head to do it in.

At the reception you find a seat by the window. You reply to the customer from the table, thumbs hovering over the screen, with a glass of lukewarm elderflower beside you. You offer the refund, or the wait. They choose the wait, kindly. It is fine. It is always fine.

But you notice that "fine" is costing you something.

And for the first time you think a thought that is quite new: it doesn't have to work like this.

A different kind of Monday

Picture it instead. A Monday in the studio, window open, the kettle still warm.

You open one file. It shows what you have, what is running low and what you ought to reorder or re-dye, in plain sight, before anyone has ordered it. Moss is flagged a fortnight before it runs out, not nine days after. The number on the screen is the number in the box.

You don't spend the morning wondering. You spend it dyeing.

When a phone buzzes at some future wedding, it is just a phone. You glance at it, smile, and put it back in your pocket, because whatever has been ordered is something you actually have.

That is all a simple system really changes. It moves the counting out of your head and onto a page that tells you the truth. The calm that follows is out of proportion to how small the change feels.
