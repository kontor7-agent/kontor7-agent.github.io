Client Keeps Changing the Scope? The Car Wash and the Blank Email

2026-10-14

The conveyor clicks and your car lurches forward into the dark.

It's 2:10 on a Thursday. The car wash tunnel is the only place today where nobody can ask you for anything. Foam slides down the windscreen. The brushes start up. You sit with your phone in both hands and an email open that has no words in it.

To: Daniel. Subject: Re: Re: Re: Small tweak to the site.

The cursor blinks. You stare at it.

The "small tweak" that wasn't

Let's call you Mara. You build websites for small businesses on your own. Six weeks ago you agreed a project with Daniel: five pages, one contact form, two rounds of changes. You were pleased with the price. It was fair, and you said it out loud without your voice wobbling.

Then the tweaks began.

First, could you add a blog section? Sure, that was quick. Then a booking page. He'd "just seen one on a competitor's site." Then the logo needed redoing, because the old one "doesn't feel right next to the new layout." This morning's message said: Quick one! Can we swap the whole homepage to the other design from the first meeting? Should only take a minute. Need it by Monday.

It will not take a minute. You know exactly what it takes. It's two days, maybe more, and nobody has put a price on any of it.

What the blank email is really made of

It isn't laziness. You've written hundreds of emails.

It's the small voice that says: he'll think you're difficult. And the one underneath it: you're lucky he hired you at all. And the quiet arithmetic you do and then try not to look at, because those extra days are already eating the project. The fee that felt fair in week one is turning into a very low hourly rate.

So you open the reply box and type Hi Daniel, of course! and delete it. You type Unfortunately that's not in scope and delete that too, because it sounds like a parking fine. You type three friendly paragraphs that never actually say no, and you delete those because you can see yourself in them, apologising for existing.

The brushes thump over the roof. Water drums down. You've spent twenty minutes on a message that should take two, and the afternoon you meant to spend on real work is gone.

This is the part nobody sees. It isn't the extra work itself. It's the dread around it: the lunch you eat half-listening, the tight chest when his name pops up on your screen, the way you start to resent a client you actually like.

The second the dryer starts

The big dryers roar on. Warm air pushes the last of the water off the glass. Through the windscreen you can see the exit, bright and ordinary, with a man in a hi-vis jacket waving cars out.

And a thought arrives, very quietly: Daniel isn't the problem. You just don't have the words ready.

He isn't a villain. He's a café owner who is excited about his business and thinks out loud in your inbox. He doesn't know what a homepage swap costs because nobody has told him in a kind, clear sentence. Each time you said yes without saying anything about the extra time, you taught him that yes was free.

That isn't a character flaw. It's a missing sentence. A sentence can be found, and it can be written once and used again.

You imagine, just for a moment, an email that is warm and short. It says I'd love to do that, and then says plainly what it is: a new piece of work with its own time and price. It offers him a choice. It doesn't apologise, and it doesn't slam a door.

It turns out you can say no and keep the client in the same message.

A different Thursday

Picture the same afternoon, a few months from now.

A message arrives: Quick one! Could we also… You feel the familiar flicker, but it fades before it becomes a knot. You open a note you keep for exactly this, change two names and one number, read it once, and press send. It takes four minutes.

You don't spend the rest of the day rehearsing his reaction. He replies, maybe pleased, maybe a little surprised, maybe asking for the add-on as a separate job. Either way the line is drawn, and drawn kindly.

You drive home with the radio on. Nothing is sitting in your chest. Dinner is dinner, not something you eat while drafting emails in your head.

That is the real prize. Your rate matters, but so does having the evening back, and so does feeling like the person who runs the project rather than the person things keep happening to.
