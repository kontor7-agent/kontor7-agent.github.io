Profit on Handmade Items After Etsy Fees? The Frame Shop

2026-10-16

The question at the counter

Thursday, a little after eleven. Rain on the shop window, a radio playing somewhere behind the shelves.

Ines is at the frame shop with four embroidered hoops wrapped in tissue. A customer wants them mounted as a set for a nursery, and she thought a proper wooden frame would make the order feel special.

The framer is a kind man with pencil dust on his sleeve. He lays the first hoop on the counter, turns it in the light and says, "These are lovely. How's the shop going? Selling well?"

"Really well," Ines says. "Busy. Good."

The words come out smoothly, as they always do. Then comes the small, familiar drop in her stomach. Because she doesn't know. She knows the orders are coming in. She knows the little cha-ching notification on her phone. She knows she stitched until her wrist ached last week.

She does not know whether any of it is profit.

What "busy" hides

It's a strange thing, running a shop where the numbers feel like someone else's language.

There's a listing fee here, a transaction fee there, a payment processing cut that arrives separately. Shipping is sometimes free because the buyer expected it to be. The thread, the hoops, the backing felt, the little card with the thank-you note, the tissue paper, the packaging she bought in a bulk bag and forgot about the moment she paid.

And then there's her time. The hours she doesn't count because counting them feels like admitting something.

So each month goes the same way. Money comes into the account. Money goes out. What's left looks fine, or at least not alarming, and she tells herself that fine is the same as working.

But when a friend asks "are you making a living from it?", she laughs and changes the subject. When she sees a similar hoop listed for less, her chest tightens, and she can't say whether she should match it or whether that would mean she's working for almost nothing.

She has never said this out loud to anyone: I think I might be paying to do this.

The pencil on the counter

The framer is measuring now, writing numbers on a scrap of paper. He does it without fuss. Mount width, frame depth, glass, the backing board. Each piece gets its own line, and at the bottom he adds it up and draws a neat box around the total.

"There," he says. "That's what it costs me to make it right, and that's what I need to charge."

He says it as plainly as you'd say the time.

It lands in Ines like a small stone dropped in still water. Of course. He knows what each thing costs him. He doesn't feel greedy about it, and he doesn't feel guilty. It's just a fact, written on a scrap of paper, in pencil.

She thinks of her own pricing. A number she picked once, because it felt about right and sat between two other sellers. She hasn't looked at it since.

She doesn't go home and rebuild her business. She just thinks: I would like to be able to draw a box around my own total.

Not a crisis, just a quiet decision

Here is the thing about not knowing. It feels enormous, but it's usually not a catastrophe. It's more like a window you've been meaning to wipe.

Part of the weight is the dread of finding out. If the bestseller barely pays for itself, then what? If one of the hoops loses money every time it sells, does that mean the whole thing was a mistake?

It doesn't. It means you finally have a number to work with. You can nudge a price. You can drop the product that only looks popular. You can stop apologising for a figure you now understand. A hobby that quietly costs you is a very different thing from a small business that knows where it stands, and the difference is mostly a matter of seeing.

It's the same thing the framer does with his pencil. One line per cost, one total, and then a price that doesn't need defending.

A simple system for this doesn't have to be clever. It just has to put cost, fees and your price side by side for each thing you make, so the answer is in front of you rather than in the back of your head.

A different kind of Thursday

Picture the same counter a month from now.

The rain is the same. The tissue paper, the hoops, the radio. The framer asks, "How's the shop?"

And Ines says, "Good. Some pieces are better than others, honestly. I've just worked out which ones, and I raised the price on the one I was underselling."

She isn't boasting. Her voice is just level. There's no tightness behind it, because the answer is no longer a guess.

On the walk home she isn't running sums in her head. At her table that evening she stitches because she wants to, and the market-day cash box and the online orders sit in one place instead of two notebooks and a mental fog. When the phone makes its small notification sound, it's just a nice sound again.

Nothing dramatic changed. She just knows.
