Sold Loads on Etsy, No Money Left? The Dinner Bill

2026-10-23

Your sister's birthday dinner is going well. Candles on the table, someone has ordered the good bread, and then your aunt turns to you and asks, "So how's the shop going?"

"Great," you say. "Best month yet. Over a hundred orders."

Everyone cheers. Your sister squeezes your arm. You smile and reach for your water glass.

Then the bill arrives, and your stomach tightens. You are doing sums under the table, quietly, with your thumb on your phone. Not about the dinner. About your bank balance. A hundred orders, and somehow the account looks like a quiet month in January.

The best month that didn't feel like one

You make hand-poured candles. Or linocut cards, or ceramic mugs, or knitted hats. It doesn't matter which. The shape of this story is the same for all of us who make things.

The orders came in. The little notification sound followed you around all month. You packed parcels at the kitchen table until your wrists ached. You queued at the post office twice a week. You felt, for the first time, like a real business.

And now there's this strange, flat feeling. The money came in. You saw it come in. But it also went out, in pieces you can't quite point to.

A listing fee here. A transaction fee there. A payment processing cut. Postage you offered "free" because the shop said it helps sales. A box of jars. Wax, wicks, tissue paper, the sticker labels you reordered twice because the first batch came out blurry. A market stall fee you paid in cash and never wrote down.

None of it is dramatic. That's the problem. It's a hundred small leaks, and no single one is loud enough to make you stop.

The question you don't say out loud

Walking home from the restaurant, you let the others go ahead. You look at the dark shop windows and finally let the real thought up.

What if the bestseller doesn't actually earn anything?

It's the one everybody loves. The one with the five-star reviews. The one you're a little proud of, and a little afraid to check.

Because if you check and the answer is bad, then what? You've been working weekends for it. You've told your family it's going well. You've started to think of yourself as someone who makes a living from this, and you don't want that picture to crack.

So you do what a lot of makers do. You look at the sales total, the big happy number, and you let that be the answer. Revenue feels like proof. It isn't, but it's much kinder than the alternative.

There's a quieter cost too. You haven't raised a price in a year, because you're not sure you're allowed. You say yes to a custom order at a number you picked on the spot, then feel a small resentment while you're making it. You mentally add up the hours and then stop adding, because the result is awkward.

None of that is a failing. You're good at making things. Nobody ever taught you to see what each one leaves in your pocket.

The Tuesday it shifted

The change, when it comes, is undramatic.

It's a Tuesday morning, the kettle clicking off, a parcel waiting by the door. You sit down with one product. Just one. You put in what the materials cost you, what the fees take, what the postage really runs to, and you don't look away.

The number is lower than you hoped. It stings for about a minute.

Then something unexpected happens: you feel lighter. Because now it's a fact, not a fog. A fact you can work with. Maybe the price was simply too low. Maybe the free shipping is eating the margin. Maybe the mug that sells slowly is quietly your best earner, and the candle that sells like crazy is the one you've been subsidising.

You don't need to be a finance person for this. You need one place where the cost, the fees and the profit sit side by side, and where the market stall and the online orders stop living in two different notebooks.

A normal Monday, afterwards

Picture it a few weeks on. Monday morning, same kettle, same parcels.

You open one sheet. You can see which products earn well and which only look busy. You raise one price by a small amount, and the world doesn't end. You stop offering free postage on the item where it was costing you more than you thought. When a customer asks for a custom piece, you have a number, and you say it calmly.

At the next family dinner, someone asks how the shop is going. You say, "Good. Actually good," and you know what you mean by it. When the bill comes, you don't do sums under the table.

That's the whole change. Not a bigger shop. Not more hours. Just knowing what you keep.
