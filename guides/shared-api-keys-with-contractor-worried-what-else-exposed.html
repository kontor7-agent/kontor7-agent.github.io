Shared API Keys With a Contractor? The Padlock Aisle

2026-11-07

It is Saturday, 10:20, and you are standing in the padlock aisle of a hardware shop. You came for a bike lock. You are holding three of them, and you cannot decide.

The real reason you can't decide is on your phone. It is a chat thread from March with a contractor called Jonas. In it, you pasted an API key so he could get started. Then you pasted a second one for the payment sandbox. Later, when something wouldn't connect, you sent a third. It was the live one, because it was quicker.

His contract ended in June. You have not thought about that thread since. Now you can't think about anything else.

The question under the question

The obvious worry is simple: does he still have access? But it spreads quickly.

What else did you share while you were busy being grateful that someone was helping? Was there an admin login in that thread? Was the staging server open to anyone? Did he commit something somewhere that you never saw?

You don't think Jonas did anything wrong. He was careful and kind, and he sent invoices on time. That makes it worse. You can't even be properly suspicious. You just have a vague, whole-body unease that you have no name for and no list to hold it against.

So you do what many people do. You stand there, feel it, and tell yourself you'll deal with it Monday. Then you buy the second-cheapest padlock, because buying something feels like progress.

What it quietly costs

Nothing has gone wrong. That is the strange part. No alert, no angry email, no unexpected bill.

But the doubt has a price anyway. It comes with you to the supermarket queue, where you check your phone for a notification you don't want to see. It sits in the back of your head during the client call on Tuesday, when someone casually asks, "And you're confident the integration is secure?" You say "yes, of course" in the voice of someone who is mostly sure.

You are a professional. You run a real product with real customers. And you feel, for a moment, like someone who handed out house keys at a party and lost count.

That feeling is rarely about the keys themselves. It is about not knowing what you would need to know. You don't know where to begin, or what "done" would look like. A worry with no edges is heavier than a worry with a list.

The turning point, in a car park

Back at the car, you put the padlock on the passenger seat and open your notes app. You are not going to fix anything yet. You just want to write down what you actually know.

The keys that were shared, and where. The people who ever had access. The places that talk to the outside world. The things you have never looked at, ever.

Partway through the second line, something shifts. The worry stops being a fog and becomes a list. It is a long list, a bit embarrassing, and entirely finite.

You realise the problem was never that you are careless. You are one person with a product, a few customers and no security team. Nobody ever handed you a checklist, so you were reviewing your own work from memory. Of course it felt like standing in a dark room.

What you want is the same thing you would want from a good colleague. Someone who says, "Here is what people usually check. Go through it once, calmly, and write down what you find." Then you could look at your own system without that tight feeling in your chest.

A different Monday

Picture the Monday after. Not a perfect one, because you probably found a few things worth tidying up. But those things have names now. They sit in a row, some ticked, some waiting, none of them a secret.

The old keys are replaced. The ones that should not be reachable are not. When the Tuesday client asks, "Is it secure?", you answer with a small, honest sentence. You say what you reviewed and what you changed. You don't have to perform confidence, because you have something real to point at.

The unease doesn't shout itself away. It simply has nowhere left to hide. You leave the laptop at six, and the bike lock does its quiet job outside the café.
