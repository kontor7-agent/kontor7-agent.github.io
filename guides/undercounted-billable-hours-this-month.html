Undercounted Billable Hours? The Tea Van Lay-By Doubt

2026-10-29

It is 8:50 on the last Friday of the month. You are parked in a lay-by with the engine off, a paper cup of tea warming your hands, and your phone propped against the steering wheel.

On the screen is the invoice draft for your biggest client. The line says 86 hours.

You stare at it. Something is wrong with that number.

Not wrong like a typo. Wrong like a shirt that doesn't quite fit. This month felt bigger than 86 hours. You remember the Tuesday that swallowed the whole day. You remember the revision round that kept coming back like a boomerang. You remember answering messages while the pasta water boiled over.

You have a client meeting in forty minutes, and the thought arrives, quiet and unwelcome: I think I undercounted.

The honest kind of guessing

Let's call you Mira. You design websites for small companies, and you are good at it. Your clients say so, and you believe them on most days.

But hours are slippery. Some were written on a sticky note that is now somewhere under your keyboard. Some live in your calendar as vague blocks called "Client work". Some exist only as a feeling in your shoulders.

So at month-end you do what many of us do. You scroll back through your sent emails, squint at file timestamps, and build a number from clues, like a detective with a headache.

The number you land on is always careful. You'd rather be a little under than risk looking greedy. A small voice says, Better to be safe. They might question it.

The voice never mentions who pays for being safe.

What the careful number costs

The tea van man hands another customer a bacon roll. You do some quiet arithmetic you'd rather skip.

If you lose even three or four hours a month to "I'm sure it was about that", that is a whole working day gone every quarter. It is a day you worked and didn't bill, which means a day you gave away without ever deciding to.

There is a second cost too, and it's harder to name. It is the small flinch when a client asks how long something took and you can't say with a straight back. Your answer comes out as "roughly", "about", "around". You know that word doesn't sound like a professional with a clear picture of her own business.

And there is a third cost. You start to resent the work a little. Not the client, not the project, just the strange feeling of doing a lot and ending the month unsure it counted.

Nobody talks about this one. It sits in the stomach like a stone from a sandwich you didn't enjoy.

The meeting you walk into

You don't fix it in the lay-by. You can't. You have no way to check without a day of digging, and the digging would still end in a guess.

So you send the invoice with 86 hours and drive on. The meeting goes fine. The client is friendly and talks about next quarter, and you nod and smile.

All the while a small corner of your head keeps counting. It counts Tuesday. It counts the revision round. It counts a call that ran long and a Saturday morning you pretend was a day off.

When you get home you are not angry, only tired in the particular way of someone who suspects they were generous by accident.

The turn

Some weeks later, a friend who also freelances mentions something in passing while you're both waiting for a coffee. She doesn't give a speech or lecture you about productivity. She just says that her month-end has become boring.

"Boring?" you ask.

"I open one page," she says. "The hours are already there. I look at them, I send the invoice, I go and make lunch."

That word stays with you the whole drive home. Boring. You realise it's what you've wanted all along. Not clever, not optimised, just boring: no detective work, no little stone in the stomach.

The idea underneath is almost embarrassingly simple. The hours don't have to be rebuilt from clues at the end of the month. They can be written down once, in one place, while they're still fresh, next to the client and the project they belong to. Then the invoice becomes a summary of something you already know, not a reconstruction.

A different last Friday

Picture the same lay-by a few months later. Same tea, same grey sky.

You open the invoice draft, and the number on the screen is 97 hours. It's higher than you would have guessed, and this time you don't wince. You know where every one of those hours went, because they were put down on the day they happened.

The Tuesday that swallowed everything is there. So is the revision round. So is the Saturday morning you pretended was a day off.

You don't feel greedy. You feel accurate, which turns out to be a much quieter and much nicer feeling.

You send the invoice, finish the tea, and look at the road ahead. Your shoulders sit a little lower than they used to. The meeting in forty minutes is just a meeting now.
