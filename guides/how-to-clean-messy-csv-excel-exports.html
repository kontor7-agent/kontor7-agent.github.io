The Export That Ate Your Evening: Cleaning Messy CSV and Excel Files

2026-10-12

It's 6:40 on a Thursday. The office has gone quiet, and the only light left is the glow of your screen.

The file is open. customers_export_final_v3.csv. You double-clicked it, and Excel did what Excel does: crammed everything into one column, turned a name into a row of little question-mark diamonds, and decided that a phone number starting with 0 should start with nothing at all.

You scroll. Row 212 and row 847 look like the same person. Are they? Some dates say 03/04/2026. Others say 2026-4-3. One just says "last Tuesday."

You think: I only need this for ten minutes.

It is never ten minutes.

The Quiet Tax of Messy Exports

Nobody puts this on a job description. Nobody invoices for it either.

It's the hour you spend before the real work can start. It's the find-and-replace you do by hand, the column you widen for the fortieth time, the little voice asking whether you really fixed all the duplicates or just the ones you happened to see.

It costs evenings, mostly. But it also costs something harder to name: a low hum of doubt.

Because you know that somewhere in those 3,000 rows, something is probably still wrong. A customer who gets two emails. An invoice sent to a half-typed address. A date that flipped month and day, and nobody will notice until someone does.

And then there's the awkward moment. A client says, "Hm, this list has some odd entries." You smile and say you'll take a look. Inside, you feel a little less like a professional and a little more like someone holding a leaking bucket.

The Spreadsheet You Don't Trust

You've tried the usual things. You've saved a macro you no longer understand. You've got a folder of old attempts, each one slightly different, each one named with increasing desperation. You once wrote down your own steps in a notebook, then lost the notebook.

Every new export arrives with a fresh personality. This one has semicolons instead of commas. That one comes from a system that exports in a different encoding, so every "ü" becomes something you'd rather not read aloud.

So each time, you start from zero. You tell yourself it's just how data is.

It's 8:15 now. Your dinner is in the fridge, looking patient.

The Moment It Clicks

The turning point rarely feels dramatic. It's more like a small, tired thought:

Why am I doing the same repair job by hand, again?

Not because you're lazy. Because the mess isn't new. It's the same handful of problems wearing different clothes: duplicates, odd dates, ragged phone numbers, broken characters, columns that need splitting, files that need merging. You've met every one of them before.

And anything you've met a dozen times can be handed to something that doesn't get tired at 8:15 pm.

The idea is simple. A small set of careful steps runs the same way every time. Before it changes anything, it shows you what it would change. Afterwards, it hands you a plain list of what it did. You stay in charge, but you stop being the conveyor belt.

A Different Kind of Monday

Picture the next export arriving on a Monday morning.

You make your coffee first. That alone is a small miracle.

The file lands in a folder. You run one thing, and while the kettle clicks off, a clean copy appears beside the original, untouched, still there if you ever need it. Next to it sits a short report: these rows were merged, these dates were standardized, these phone numbers were tidied, these three entries need a human eye.

Three. Not three hundred.

You read those three with your coffee. You fix one, shrug at another, and send the file on. When the client asks about the list, you can say, calmly, exactly what was cleaned and what was checked.

The hum of doubt is just gone. The evening is yours, and so is the dinner that was waiting in the fridge.

What Changes Isn't Only Time

Saved hours are nice. But the bigger shift is how work feels when you trust the foundation under it.

You stop dreading the export. You stop apologizing in advance. You start saying yes to the bigger list, the messier file, the client whose system spits out chaos, because the chaos no longer lands on your evening.

It's a quieter way to work. Less heroic, more reliable. And honestly, much nicer to live inside.
