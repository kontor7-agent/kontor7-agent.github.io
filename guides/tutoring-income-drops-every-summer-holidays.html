Tutoring Income Drops Every Summer? The Sun Cream Shelf

2026-11-03

It's a Tuesday in the second week of July, a little after eleven. You are standing in the chemist's, in front of the sun cream.

You only came in for plasters. But there is a bottle in your hand now, factor 30, and you are turning it over as if the answer to something were printed on the back.

Your phone buzzes in your pocket. You already know it isn't a payment. It's another message, polite and cheerful: "We're off to Portugal from Saturday, so no lessons until September, enjoy your summer!"

You type a thumbs-up. You mean it kindly. Then you put the cream back on the shelf, because suddenly it feels like a decision.

The summer you never see coming

This is the fourth summer in a row. You would think you'd be used to it by now.

Every year there's a week in June when everything is still normal. Then someone is away. Then two people. Then exams are over and nobody needs you for anything, and the diary you've known by heart for ten months goes white, like a wall someone has painted over in the night.

And every year it feels like a surprise. That's the part you're a little ashamed of. You teach people to plan, to look ahead, to check their working. Yet your own income does this, and you stand in a shop holding sun cream and doing sums in your head that won't come out.

You haven't told anyone how it feels. People assume tutors love the summer. Six weeks off! Lucky you.

It isn't off. It's unpaid.

What it quietly costs

The empty weeks are only half of it.

The other half is the fog around them. Who actually still owes you for June? You think it's two people, but you'd have to scroll back through your bank app and a notes file called lessons_new_FINAL to be sure. Someone cancelled on the morning of a lesson in May, and you let it go because it felt rude to mention. You can't remember how many times that has happened this year, or what it added up to.

So you do what many self-employed people do in a thin month. You say yes to everything in August, in a slightly tight voice. You book lessons at times you hate. You quietly decide that raising your rate is out of the question, because how could you, with the diary like this.

And the knot sits in your stomach on the walk home. Not a crisis, just a low hum. What if September doesn't fill back up?

The Thursday that changed the shape of it

Let's call you Daniel, because it helps to picture someone. You teach maths and exam prep, mostly to adults retaking things and a few older students. The details don't matter. What matters is a Thursday morning a few weeks later.

You are at the kitchen table with coffee, in daylight, with no panic attached. For once you aren't hunting for a number. You have sat down to look at the year properly.

And you can see it.

Every lesson you taught, in one place. Every one that was paid, and the three that weren't yet. The cancelled slots, and what they really cost you, written plainly instead of felt as a vague sting. And a shape you had never quite looked at head-on: the way your lessons rise through spring, crest in June, and thin out like a tide going back.

It wasn't a disaster. It was a pattern. Patterns can be planned for.

You realise the summer never ambushed you. You just never had it written down where you could see it coming.

A different July

Picture the next year.

It's the same Tuesday in July, the same chemist, the same shelf. You pick up the sun cream, and this time you put it in the basket without a second thought.

Your phone buzzes. "We're off from Saturday, no lessons until September." You reply with a warm message and mean it. The thin weeks were already in your picture months ago. You set a little aside in the good spring weeks, you know roughly what August looks like, and you know who is behind with payments, so you send one calm note instead of carrying the worry around.

You also notice something you hadn't expected. Because you can see what a year of late cancellations cost, and what an hour of your time is really worth, the idea of a slightly higher rate in September no longer makes your chest tighten. It's just a sentence you can say.

You take a day off, a proper one. You walk with the sun cream in your bag and your phone in your pocket, and nothing in it is waiting to ambush you.
