What to Post for Your Small Business? The Late Pupil

2026-10-29

It is 14:05 on a Wednesday. Marek's next pupil is ten minutes late, again, and he is parked outside a garden centre with the engine off and the heater ticking as it cools.

He opens the app for his driving school. The last post is eleven days old. A photo of a pupil holding up a pair of new L-plates, taken with permission, with a caption that took him half an hour.

The cursor blinks in the empty box.

He types: Tip of the week: Then he stops. Which tip? He has said all of them. Mirrors, signals, roundabouts. He deletes it and scrolls through other instructors' feeds instead. One has a bright graphic. One has a video with music. One has a post every single day.

He puts the phone face down on the passenger seat.

The thought nobody says out loud

Here is the part that doesn't show up on the feed. Marek isn't bad at his job. His pupils pass. Parents send him messages with exclamation marks.

But every week the same thought arrives, usually at an awkward moment: I should be posting. I don't know what to post. Everyone else seems to know.

So the week goes like this. Monday, he promises himself he'll sort it out. Tuesday, he is too busy. Wednesday, he sits in a car park with a blank box and a small knot under the ribs. Thursday, he posts something rushed, or nothing at all. Friday, he feels vaguely behind, as if homework is due.

It costs him less time than you'd think and more peace than he'd admit. It is never the thirty minutes that hurts. It is the way the question sits in the back of his head all week, like a radio left on in another room.

And there is a quieter worry underneath. A new learner searching for an instructor will glance at his page. Eleven days of silence might look like a business that has stopped. He knows it's not true. It still stings.

What he doesn't see

The pupil texts: Sorry, five more minutes! Marek sighs and opens his notes app instead, looking for anything he might have jotted down.

There is something. A line from three weeks ago: Pupil's mum said she cried when she passed. Good-cry kind. He forgot he'd written it. There is another: Everyone panics at the big roundabout by the retail park. Why? And another: Answer the question about test-day nerves, people ask it every time.

He sits there and looks at three ideas that were never missing. They were just lying in a place where nobody could find them, and none of them had a day.

That is the thing he hadn't noticed. He didn't have an idea problem. He had a where does this go and when problem. Every Wednesday he was inventing the whole week from nothing, in ten spare minutes, in a car park, with a cold heater.

The turning point is small

It isn't dramatic. He doesn't decide to become a content person. He just thinks, quietly: What if I decided once, on a calm day, and then the week simply knew what it was doing?

Not thirty posts. Not a daily video. Just a few honest things, spread out where he can see them, a place to keep the good lines before they vanish, and a clear picture of what is ready and what is still half a thought.

The pupil arrives, flustered and apologetic. Marek smiles and says it's fine. For the first time in a while he means it, because the knot has loosened. It has loosened without anything being posted. It's the plan, even just the idea of one, that did it.

A different Monday

Picture the same driving instructor a few weeks later.

Monday, 8:10. Coffee, the diary open, a quiet gap before the first lesson. He glances at the week. Three posts are already sitting there with their days next to them. One is half-written, one is ready, one is just the roundabout question waiting for a sentence or two.

He spends ten minutes finishing the half-written one. He closes the laptop.

Wednesday, 14:05. The pupil is late again. Marek sits in the car park outside the garden centre, watches a man wrestle a palm tree into a hatchback, and doesn't open the app at all. There is nothing to decide. The post went out this morning. He eats a banana.

That's the whole difference. The pupils are still late. The roundabout is still frightening. But the week isn't asking him a question every time he stops moving.

And on the quiet days, when he has nothing to say, the page still looks like someone is home. Nobody has to wonder whether the business is still going.

If you recognised yourself

Maybe you aren't a driving instructor. Maybe you bake, braid hair, fix boilers, edit manuscripts or sell candles. The moment is the same: the phone in your hand, the empty box, and the belief that everyone else has it figured out.

Most of them don't. They just stopped deciding it fresh every week.
