Recruiter Email, Can't Recall the Job? The Car Boot Sale

2026-10-20

The folding table wobbles every time someone picks up a paperback. It is 9:40 on a Saturday morning, the grass is still damp, and Tomas is selling three boxes of old paperbacks and a lamp he never liked.

His phone buzzes in his jacket pocket.

He pulls it out with one hand while the other holds a customer's change. The subject line reads: Following up on your application – next steps.

He taps it. Hi Tomas, thanks again for your interest in the role. We'd love to set up a short call. Are you free Monday or Tuesday? Best, Sarah, Talent Partner.

Which role?

The blank where the job should be

He reads it again. No job title. No company name beyond a logo in the signature, a blue square he half recognises.

He has sent a lot of applications over the past weeks. Some on his lunch break, some at midnight, a few from the sofa with the laptop going hot on his knees. Some were careful and long. Some were a quick "why not" click.

Was this the logistics software company? The one with the product manager role? Or the small agency that wanted a portfolio link? He remembers a cover letter that mentioned "your mission". Every company has a mission. That doesn't help.

"Is this one a pound?" a woman asks, holding up the lamp.

"Two," he says, too fast. "Sorry. Yes. Two is fine."

She pays. He barely sees her go.

What it quietly costs

Here is the part nobody talks about. It isn't only that he might sound confused on Monday. It is the small cold drop in the stomach when someone is kind to you and you cannot place them.

Sarah wrote warmly. She is clearly expecting a person who knows why she is writing. If he replies "Great! Which position was this again?" he will sound like someone who sends applications into a void. Which, if he is honest, is a little how it has felt.

So he does what many of us do. He opens his email search on a four-inch screen, in a field, between customers. He types "Northfield". Nothing. He types "application". Eighty-one results. He scrolls with his thumb past confirmations, rejections, newsletters and one email from his bank.

A man asks whether the paperbacks come as a set. Tomas says yes without knowing whether they do.

By ten o'clock he has sold seven books and learned nothing. By half past, a quiet dread has settled in. It isn't panic. It's the tired feeling of a job search that lives in too many places: browser tabs, half-remembered emails, a note on his phone called "jobs??", a PDF called CV_final_v7.

The worst part is that he has to prepare. A call on Monday means thinking about what to say. How can you prepare for a role you cannot name?

The thought that changed the morning

Around eleven, the crowd thins. A stallholder next to him, a retired man selling vinyl and fishing gear, is writing something in a small notebook after every sale. Item, price, done. A line each time.

"Doesn't take long," the man says when he catches Tomas looking. "But I'd never remember otherwise. By lunchtime it's all a blur."

Tomas laughs. Then he stops laughing, because the thought lands plainly.

He has never lacked effort. He has lacked a place where each application leaves a line behind. One row each. The company, the role, the date, the link to the ad, what he sent, when to nudge them. Nothing fancy. Just somewhere that remembers so his head doesn't have to.

It doesn't have to feel like this.

A different Monday

Picture the same email arriving on a different kind of morning.

It is Monday, 8:15. Coffee on the desk. Sarah's message appears, and instead of a cold drop you feel a small, almost boring click of recognition. You open one sheet, find the company in a list, and there it is: the role, the date you applied, the version of your CV you sent, the line from the ad you liked.

You reply in two minutes. "Thanks, Sarah, Tuesday works. Looking forward to talking about the product role." It sounds like someone who knows what they're doing, because you do.

Then you spend the rest of your prep time on the part that matters. What you would say about a project that went wrong. Which story shows how you handle pressure. You aren't playing detective in your inbox. You are getting ready.

And on the days when nothing comes back, you can see that too. Who you haven't heard from in two weeks, who is worth a polite nudge, who you can quietly let go. The silence stops being a fog and becomes a short, readable list.

That's all it is. A calmer job search, not a faster one. One where the evenings go back to being evenings.

What Tomas did that afternoon

Back home, with the unsold lamp under his arm, Tomas did one thing. He found the logo in his sent folder, worked out the job, and wrote down the week's applications one line at a time. It took forty minutes and it was not fun. But when he finished, the dread had a shape and then it had an edge. He knew what he was walking into on Tuesday.

He didn't need to do it that way. A tracker could have been waiting for him already.
