Scared Your Freelance Income Will Drop? Planning 2027

2026-10-18

Tuesday, 12:20. You are third in the queue at the supermarket, a basket of lentils, oat milk and a lemon on your arm.

Your phone buzzes. A client you have worked with for two years writes: "We're reviewing budgets for next year. Will let you know in January."

The queue moves. You don't. Your thumb hovers over the screen, and a single thought drowns out the beeping of the tills: what if 2027 is the thin year?

The question nobody asks out loud

Nobody at the checkout can see it. You pay, you say thank you, you carry the bag to the car. But on the drive home the maths starts without being asked.

That client is maybe a third of your income. Or is it a quarter? You genuinely don't know. You know roughly what came in this year. You know it felt fine in spring and tight in August. You know there was one week in September when two invoices landed and you exhaled for the first time in weeks.

What you don't have is a picture. Just a feeling in the stomach, and the feeling is not specific enough to do anything about.

That is the strange part of this fear. It is rarely about a number. It is about not having a number. So your head fills the gap with the worst version, and the worst version is always very vivid.

What the fog quietly costs

You don't say it to friends, because they have salaries and would say something kind and useless, like "you'll be fine, you always are."

But the fog has a price, and you have been paying it in small coins.

You say yes to a project you don't want, because turning it down feels reckless. You hold off on raising your rate, because now is not the time. You skip the week off in the summer, because you can't tell whether you can afford it. And when you do take a day off, it doesn't feel like one. The laptop sits on the sofa, open, like a dog waiting at the door.

You start to feel like someone winging it. Not a real business. Just a person who has been lucky so far.

That last thought is the unkind one, and it is also not true. You have been working for yourself for years. Luck doesn't pay that many invoices.

The kitchen table, a week later

On Saturday morning, tea in hand, you do something small. You don't open the bank app. You open a blank page and write three plain questions:

What would a decent year look like? What would a slow year look like? What would I do in each?

It is oddly calming. Not because the answers are good, but because they are yours, written down, with edges. A slow year with a plan has edges. A slow year in your head has none.

You realise something while the tea cools. Most of what scared you was never the possible drop itself. It was the idea of meeting it unprepared, of being surprised in March with an empty calendar and nothing to say to yourself.

And surprise is something you can take away. Not the uncertainty, which belongs to every self-employed person who has ever lived, but the surprise.

What a different Monday feels like

Picture it a few months from now. Monday, 9:05. You make coffee and open one page that shows the year on a single screen. What you hoped to earn by quarter. What has actually come in. Which projects are likely, which are only conversations. Where your weeks off sit, already marked, already counted in.

You see that the first quarter looks lighter than you'd like. That's fine, because you saw it in October, not in February. So you spend an hour this week writing to two past clients, just a friendly hello and a note that you have space in spring. It doesn't feel like begging. It feels like gardening.

Then you close the page. It took ten minutes. The knot that used to sit under your ribs is smaller, and it is no longer unnamed.

The summer week off? It is on the plan, with the money for it accounted for. You can book the cottage without a small voice asking whether you're allowed.

The news from that client in January might still be disappointing. A plan can't promise otherwise, and nobody honest would say it can. But you'd meet that news sitting up straight, with a page in front of you, instead of standing in a queue with a lemon in your hand.
