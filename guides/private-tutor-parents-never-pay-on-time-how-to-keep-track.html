Tutor Parents Never Pay on Time? The Dog Field Saturday

2026-10-22

It is Saturday, 9:10 am, and you are standing at the edge of a muddy field holding a lead.

Your dog is meant to be learning to sit. You are meant to be watching. Instead you are looking at your phone, because the banking app has just told you that no, nothing came in overnight.

Somebody owes you for four lessons. Maybe five. You tutor maths and exam prep, and you are fairly sure it is the parent whose messages always start with "So sorry!" But you couldn't swear to it.

The question you can't answer on a muddy field

The woman next to you is chatting about her spaniel. You nod. In your head, you are scrolling through last month.

There was the Tuesday lesson that moved to Thursday. The Wednesday one that was cancelled with two hours' notice. The week you said "don't worry about it" and then worried about it all night.

You know roughly what you're owed. You just don't know it exactly. And a number you can only feel is a number you can't say out loud.

So you do what you always do. You tell yourself you'll sort it tomorrow.

What late payment really costs a tutor

It isn't only the money, although the money matters. The rent doesn't wait for the parent who "always forgets on the first of the month".

It's the small, quiet things.

It's the way you start a lesson slightly guarded, because you're thinking about the unpaid invoice instead of the work in front of you.

It's the message you draft four times. "Just a gentle reminder" sounds like begging. "Payment is overdue" sounds like a lawyer. So you send nothing, and the silence gets heavier.

It's the thought that a real business would have this under control. That other tutors have a system. That you are somehow just bad at the money part.

You're not. You teach, you prepare, you show up on time in the rain. Nobody ever gave you a calm place to put the rest.

The notebook, the app, the memory

Here is what you have right now. Lesson dates in your calendar. Payments in your banking app. Cancellations buried in a chat thread. A half-finished list in a notes app, titled "who owes??" with two question marks, because even you weren't sure.

Three places, and none of them talk to each other.

So every time someone asks, even yourself, the answer is a small excavation. Did that lesson happen or get moved? Was it paid or just promised? Was the late cancellation charged or waived?

You end up waiving things by accident. Not out of kindness. Out of fog.

The turning point (and it's very small)

The trainer calls the group into a circle. The dogs, briefly, are all sitting.

Someone says something offhand about their own work. "I just write it down the moment it happens. Then I never have to remember anything."

That's all. Nobody is talking about you, or about tutoring.

But the sentence lands. Write it down the moment it happens. You have been trying to keep a whole month in your head, and your head was never meant to be a ledger.

What if each lesson simply had one line? Taught. Paid, or not yet. If it was cancelled late, that too. And what if all those lines lived in one place, so that "who is behind?" was something you could see in a few seconds instead of something you dreaded?

Nothing dramatic. Just a quieter way to keep the facts.

A different Monday

Picture it a few weeks from now.

It's Monday morning. The kettle is on. You open one file, and the overview is already there. Two parents are a little behind. One is a week late, the other three weeks. You can see the amounts, with no guessing.

You write two short messages. They're friendly, specific and calm, because you aren't pleading anymore. You are simply stating what's on the page. "Hi, I have lessons from the 3rd and 10th still open. Could you send that over this week?"

Done in four minutes.

Then something odd happens. You don't think about it again. It doesn't sit in your chest during the first lesson of the afternoon. You are just a tutor, teaching.

And at the end of the month, when a parent says "I thought that cancellation was fine," you don't flinch. You can see what was agreed, and you can answer kindly and clearly.

Over time you start to notice other things too. What late cancellations really cost you across a term. Which days of the week are quietly your best. Whether your rate, unchanged for two years, still matches the work you do. Those become calm questions, not frightening ones.

Back at the field

The dog sits. Briefly, triumphantly. Everyone claps, and so do you.

On the walk back to the car you don't open the banking app. You make a small decision instead: this week, every lesson gets its one line. Not a perfect system, just a start.

The parents who pay late will probably still pay late. That part isn't yours to control. But not knowing is, and that was always the heavier half.
