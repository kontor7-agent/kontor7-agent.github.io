Worried Your API Has Security Holes? The Train to the Demo

2026-10-12

The train is somewhere between Hannover and Göttingen, and the window shows nothing but wet fields.

Your laptop sits on the little folding table, next to a paper cup of coffee that went cold an hour ago. Tomorrow at ten, you demo the booking API you built for a small physiotherapy chain. Today it works. Every endpoint answers. Every test is green.

And still, one thought keeps circling like a fly in a closed room: what if there's a hole I haven't looked for?

The question you don't say out loud

You are good at your job. You know that. But you are also the only developer, the only reviewer, the only person who would ever notice a forgotten check.

So you scroll through your own routes. Login. Appointments. Patients. Invoices. You wonder whether every one of them really asks who are you? before it answers. You wonder whether one of them, added in a hurry on a Thursday, quietly doesn't.

You open a browser tab and type something like is my API secure before launch. The results are long. Forty-minute talks. Lists with abbreviations you half remember. A forum thread where three people disagree politely and one disagrees rudely.

You close the tab. Not because the answers are wrong. Because you can't tell where to start, and starting feels like admitting you might find something.

What the worry quietly costs

Nobody sees this part of launch week.

It's the Sunday walk where you're half listening to a friend. It's lying awake at 3 a.m., mentally replaying a route you wrote six weeks ago. It's the slightly too bright "yes, all good!" you give the client, while your stomach says probably.

It also costs something professional. A client who trusts you with patient appointments is trusting your judgement. And you know that "I looked at it, and here is what I checked" sounds very different from "I think it's fine."

You don't need to be terrified. You just want to know.

The station at Göttingen

The train stops. People shuffle with their bags. You look at the screen and notice something small: you have been trying to review everything at once, from memory, in your head.

That is the problem. Not your skill. The method.

A pilot with decades of experience still reads a checklist before takeoff. Not because they forgot how to fly. Because calm comes from not having to remember.

You think: what if the review wasn't a feeling, but a list? Something with an order. Something you could tick through, route by route, and end with a page that says what was looked at and what was found.

You don't need to become a security researcher before tomorrow. You need a clear, finite way to look.

A different Monday

Picture the same launch, a week later.

It's Monday morning. The coffee is hot this time. The API has been live for three days, and the first thing you do is not check the logs with a held breath. You check them the way you check the weather.

Before going live you had worked through a list. You knew which routes were open on purpose and which were open by accident, because you had looked. The few things you found were small, and you fixed them on a Tuesday afternoon, in daylight, with no one waiting.

When the client asked, "Is it secure?", you didn't improvise. You sent a short, tidy summary of what had been reviewed. They read it, nodded, and asked about the next feature instead.

That's the whole change. Not a guarantee that nothing will ever go wrong, because no one can honestly promise that. Just the quiet of having looked properly, and being able to show it.
