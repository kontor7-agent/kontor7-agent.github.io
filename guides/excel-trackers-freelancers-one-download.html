Need a Tracker for Every Problem? The Bus Shelter Call

2026-10-16

The drizzle is the fine kind, the kind that doesn't fall so much as hang. Mira stands in a bus shelter with her phone against her ear and a tote bag of groceries at her feet.

It is Wednesday, 11:40. The client on the line is friendly. That makes it worse.

"Quick question," he says. "How many hours did we use last month? And was the August invoice settled? My bookkeeper is asking."

Mira is a freelance virtual assistant. She knows the answer exists. She just doesn't know where it lives.

Three files, three moods

The hours are in one spreadsheet she made two winters ago. The invoices are in another, a download she grabbed one panicked January. The rate calculations are in a third, with a tab named "final_v2" that is not final.

Each file solved one problem on the day she needed it. Each was a small, sensible decision. Together they have become a quiet household of strangers who never speak to each other.

"I'll check and send you a note this afternoon," she says. Her voice is bright and professional. Her stomach is not.

The bus arrives. She lets it go.

The cost nobody puts on an invoice

There is no line item for this. No one bills for the twenty minutes of opening files, scrolling, and copying a number from one place to another, then doubting the number.

There is no line item for the small flinch when a client asks a simple question. Or for the feeling, hard to say out loud, that a real business would just know.

Mira isn't disorganised. She is the person her clients rely on to remember everything. That is exactly why it stings that her own records feel like a drawer of loose keys.

So she does what many of us do. She tells herself she'll build one proper system someday. A big, clever workbook. She will start it on a quiet weekend that never arrives, because quiet weekends are when she catches up on the work she couldn't finish during the week.

The afternoon that went differently

Back home, towel on the radiator, tea going cold, she sits down with the three files open side by side. The answer takes her forty minutes to piece together. Yes, August was paid. Yes, the hours were close to what she'd estimated. She sends the note.

Then she doesn't close the laptop. She just sits there.

It isn't the hours or the invoice that bothers her. It's that the answer was correct and still took forty minutes. It was only available because she happened to remember which file to trust.

And she realises she has never once asked the obvious question: why are these separate in the first place? Not because anyone chose it. Because each one arrived alone, on a different bad day, and she never had the energy to make them neighbours.

It doesn't have to be a project. It doesn't have to be a weekend. It could simply be a set of tools that were made to sit together, with the same calm look, the same logic, and the same place to start.

What a normal Wednesday could feel like

Picture the same call, a different Wednesday.

The drizzle is the same. The bus shelter is the same. The client asks the same friendly question about hours and the August invoice.

Mira says, "Give me a moment," and opens one file on her phone. Not three. She finds the month, the hours, the paid mark. She reads it out in a steady voice.

"Forty-one hours, invoice settled on the ninth."

"Perfect," he says. "Thanks, that's all I needed."

The call lasts ninety seconds. The bus pulls up and she gets on, groceries and all, with her shoulders where shoulders belong. She is thinking about lunch.

That is the whole difference. It isn't glamour or a new workflow to learn. It's the small, dignified feeling of knowing, and of being able to say so while standing in the rain.
