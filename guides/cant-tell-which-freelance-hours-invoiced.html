Can't Tell Which Hours You Invoiced? The Beehive Veil

2026-11-02

Ines has one glove on and one glove off when the phone buzzes in her jacket pocket.

It is Saturday, 11:20. She is standing in her brother-in-law's allotment in a borrowed bee veil. A frame of honeycomb hangs in front of her face, and the hive hums low and steady. She is not supposed to be thinking about work.

The buzz is an email from a client. She reads it through the mesh, squinting.

"Quick question: did the invoice from last week include the onboarding rewrite? Our finance person is closing the month."

She freezes. The bees don't notice. Her stomach does.

The answer you almost know

Did it? She thinks so. She remembers writing the invoice. She remembers a line that said something about "copy revisions." She remembers working on the onboarding rewrite on a Thursday, and maybe part of a Friday.

But did those hours go on that invoice, or the one before it? Or are they still sitting in a notes file, waiting?

She hands the frame back to her brother-in-law with a shaky smile. "Work thing," she says. He nods like a man who has also had a work thing.

She tries to answer from memory right there between the hive boxes. Yes, I think it was included. She types it. She deletes it. She types, Let me double-check and come back to you. She deletes that too, because it sounds like someone who doesn't know her own business.

In the end she sends nothing. She puts the phone away and tells herself she will look at it properly later.

What "later" costs

Here is the thing nobody says out loud: the question itself is not the problem. Clients ask this kind of thing all the time.

The problem is the small, cold drop you feel when you realise you can't answer. A professional is supposed to know. And you did the work. You just can't tell where it went.

For Ines, "later" means a Saturday afternoon spent digging. A calendar open in one window. A folder of old invoices in another. A notes app with half-finished lines like Tues – call + edits (3h?). She scrolls. She adds things up. She gets a number, then doubts it, then gets another number.

There is a second fear underneath the first, and it's quieter. Maybe she billed something twice and the client was too polite to say so. Or maybe she did the opposite, and an afternoon of her work has been floating around unpaid for weeks, with nobody keeping an eye on it. She can't tell which would be worse.

The honey harvest is the highlight of her weekend. She spends it with a knot in her chest.

The sentence that changed the question

On Sunday, she tells her sister about it over lunch. Not as a complaint, more as a confession: I don't actually know what I've billed.

Her sister, who runs a small physio practice, doesn't gasp. She just says, "You mean you don't have one place where it all lives?"

Ines opens her mouth to defend herself and finds she has nothing to defend. She has a calendar, a notes app, a folder, and her memory. All of them are decent. None of them talks to the others.

That is the turning point, and it is almost embarrassingly small. The problem was never that she was careless. Her work was spread across five places, and she was acting as the glue between them.

A different Monday

Picture the same email arriving on a Monday morning a few weeks later.

It is 9:05. Ines has a coffee and the window open. The client asks the same question about the onboarding rewrite.

She doesn't go hunting. She opens one sheet, finds the project, and sees the hours for that week sitting next to the invoice they belong to. It takes her about as long as it takes to sip the coffee.

Yes, those hours were on invoice 2026-041, sent on the 14th. Happy to forward it again if it helps your finance person.

Send. Done. The reply is calm and specific, and it sounds like her.

What strikes her afterwards isn't the speed. It's the absence of that cold little drop. Nothing in her chest tightens. She goes back to her actual work, the writing she is good at, and the question is already behind her.

There is a quieter benefit too. On Friday afternoons she can look at the week and see what is billed, what is still open, and what is not yet invoiced. She closes the laptop with nothing hanging in the air. Her Saturdays go back to the bees.
