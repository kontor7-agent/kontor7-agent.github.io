Client Says 'Processed', Money Never Came? The Stamp

2026-11-05

It is Tuesday, 10:15, and you are third in line at the post office.

In your hand is a small padded envelope. Inside: a printed proof for a client who still likes things on paper. Dani, the illustrator in this story, has done this walk a hundred times. Today her phone is buzzing in her coat pocket, and she already knows who it is.

She checks it while the woman ahead of her argues about a customs form.

"Hi Dani, just a quick note: the payment for invoice 0412 has been processed on our side. Should be with you shortly. Best regards."

That is the third version of this sentence. The first one came eleven days ago. The second said "released." This one says "processed."

She opens her banking app right there, between the stamp display and a rack of birthday cards. The balance is the same as it was at 7:30. And at 7:35. And last night.

A word that means nothing

"Processed." She turns the word over.

Processed by whom? Entered into a system? Approved by someone who then went on holiday? Sent to a bank that is quietly sitting on it? She has no idea, and that is the problem. The word sounds like an answer, but it carries no date, no reference, nothing she could point to.

And every time it arrives, she feels the same two pulls at once.

One says: thank you, great, I'll wait. The other says: it's been eleven days.

The second one never makes it into an email. Writing "I still don't see it" feels like accusing someone. What if they really did send it? What if she looks impatient, or worse, desperate? What if the next brief quietly goes to someone who never asks about money?

So she types "Thank you!" with an exclamation mark she does not feel, and the day moves on.

The stamp

"Next, please."

The clerk takes the envelope, weighs it, and presses a stamp onto the label. A dull thunk. Date, place, done.

Dani watches the ink dry, and something small clicks.

That stamp means something. It has a date on it. If the envelope goes missing, there is a clear starting point: sent Tuesday, 10:17, this counter. Nobody has to wonder what "sent" means.

She thinks about invoice 0412 and realises she has nothing like that. No date she asked about. No reference she requested. No moment when she said, calmly, "Could you send me the transfer date?" Just a pile of friendly sentences that all end in "shortly."

She is not being ignored. She is being soothed. And she has been letting it work.

What it quietly costs

Outside, standing by the bike rack, she does the sum she has been avoiding.

The invoice is for a month of work. It is not a huge amount, but it is her rent-sized amount. Because it hasn't arrived, she has been putting off a small purchase for her tablet. She has been saying "let me check" when a friend suggested dinner. Last Sunday she lay awake rehearsing a message she then did not send.

None of that shows up in any spreadsheet. It shows up in her shoulders.

And the worst part: she has no idea how many other invoices are in the same grey zone. One client said "next run." Another said "with accounting." She would have to dig through old emails to even list them.

That is the part nobody warns you about. The chasing is not hard because of the words. It is hard because you are doing it from memory, in a bad mood, at the exact moment you least want to.

The same Tuesday, a different way

Here is the version she imagines on the walk home. It is not dramatic.

She opens one page. Each client who owes her something is a row. Next to each row sits a date, and next to the date, one plain line: what happens next.

For 0412 it says: Reply today. Ask for the transfer date and a reference. The wording is ready. Warm, clear, a little bit formal, and written by someone who was not annoyed at the time. If the client is German-speaking, there is a German version too, in the same calm voice.

She does not have to decide whether she is allowed to ask. The page has already decided that asking is ordinary. A professional asks for a date the way a clerk puts a date on a stamp.

She sends the email in two minutes. She closes the laptop. She goes to the market for bread.

No wondering, no rehearsing, no exclamation mark she does not mean.

And on the following Monday, when the money has or hasn't arrived, she doesn't have to remember where she left off. The page remembers. It says what comes after the first reminder, and what comes after that, in gentle steps, so that nothing ever jumps from "silence" straight to "strongly worded."

What a normal Monday could feel like

Imagine opening your bank app and feeling mostly curious instead of braced.

Imagine "processed" arriving in an email, and your reaction being a calm lovely, and what's the date? instead of a stomach drop.

Imagine knowing, without digging, who owes you, since when, and what the next small step is. Money arrives or it doesn't, but either way you are not the person standing in the cold waiting to be told.

That is all chasing needs to be. Not begging. Just the steady, friendly habit of asking for a date.
