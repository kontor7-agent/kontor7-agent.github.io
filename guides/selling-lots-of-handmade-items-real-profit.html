Selling Lots of Handmade Items but No Real Profit?

2026-10-17

Your neighbour leans over the hedge with a mug in her hand. It is a grey Tuesday, just after nine, and you are carrying a box of wooden spoons to the car.

"Business must be booming," she says. "I see the courier here every other day."

You smile. You say, "It's going well, yes."

And it is. That is the strange part. The orders are coming in. Last Saturday's market was your best in a year. People pick up the spoons, turn them over in their hands and say they're beautiful.

But as you close the boot, a small thought sits down in your chest and refuses to leave: Am I actually earning anything?

The thought you don't say out loud

You would never say it to the neighbour. It sounds ungrateful. Busy is supposed to mean good.

But you know what busy feels like. Busy is sanding until your thumbs ache. Busy is driving to the timber yard, queuing at the post office and wrapping each spoon in tissue paper because it looks nicer. Busy is the platform's fee, the card reader's fee, the packaging, the stall pitch and the petrol.

Busy is also not knowing. You know what came in. You can see it in the account. What you cannot see is what each spoon actually left you with once everything else was taken out.

So you do what most makers do. You carry a rough feeling around instead of a number. The big spoons probably do fine. The small ones, maybe not. The chopping boards sell well, so they must be good.

You have a price list. You set it two years ago, partly by looking at what other people charged and partly by what felt rude to ask. It has never been wrong enough to scare you, and never checked enough to reassure you either.

The kitchen table, later

At lunchtime you sit down with a sandwich and open the laptop. You tell yourself you will just have a quick look.

There is a notebook from the market, with cash and card sales in pencil. There is the online shop dashboard, which shows sales but not what they cost you. There is a folder of receipts for oil, sandpaper and wood, some in a drawer, some photographed, some surely lost.

You try to add it up, and the numbers will not sit together. Market money is in one place and online money is in another. The fees arrive as small deductions that you never really looked at, because each one is only a little.

After forty minutes you have a headache and one conclusion: you are tired, and you cannot say whether you are doing well.

That is the quiet cost. It is not only the money you may be leaving on the table. It is the low hum of doubt every time someone says "you must be so busy." It is the hesitation before you say yes to a custom order because you cannot tell whether the price you are about to quote is fair to you. It is the feeling that a real business would surely know this by now.

You are not a bad business. You are a person making things by hand, with no back office, and nobody ever handed you a clear picture.

The small turn

A few weeks later you are standing at the stall, rearranging spoons that do not need rearranging, when a woman asks about a custom board.

"How much for something like this, but bigger?"

Before, you would have looked at the ceiling and invented a number. A comfortable number. Maybe a low one, because you wanted the sale.

This time, you realise you could simply know. Not guess, not hope. Know what that board costs you to make, what the fees take, what is left, and what price would leave you with something that feels fair.

It is a quiet realisation, nothing dramatic. It doesn't have to be a feeling. It can be a number I can look at.

A Monday that feels different

Picture a Monday a little way ahead.

The kettle is on. You open one file instead of three tabs and a notebook. Each product sits in a row, and next to it is what is left after wood, packaging, fees and your own time. You see that the small spoons are doing quietly better than you thought. You also see that one bestseller, the one you were proudest of, has been earning almost nothing.

You are not upset. You are relieved. A hard thing that was hidden is now just a fact, and facts can be changed. A small price adjustment. A cheaper way to pack. A different board size.

When the neighbour leans over the hedge and says, "Booming, I hear?", you can smile and mean it. Not because you have grown rich overnight, but because you finally know where you stand.

The afternoon is yours. The numbers have stopped following you around the house.
