Quoted a Job With No Wages in It? The Tuesday Site Visit

2026-10-23

It is 10:20 on a Tuesday, and you are standing in a stranger's garden with a cold cup of tea. You are Marek, a landscaper with one part-time helper and a van that needs new brakes. The client has just said, "Great, so we're all set?"

You nod. You smile. The quote went out last week, and they accepted it this morning.

Then you glance at your phone, at the list you made in the notes app. Turf. Edging. Two tonnes of gravel. Delivery. A skip. It is all there, line by line, and every number is right.

There is one thing missing. You.

Not the hours of the job. You counted those, roughly. What is missing is the money for your own wage. The part that should have been left over after the gravel and the skip were paid for.

The sum you do in your head

The client is talking about where the compost bins should go. You nod again and start doing sums. Four days of work, maybe five. Take off materials. Take off the helper. Take off fuel.

What is left is a number you do not like.

It is not zero. That is almost worse. Zero would be an emergency. This is a small, quiet number, the kind that lets you say the job went fine while the account stays flat.

You have done this before, haven't you? Priced the things you could see and touch, and let your own time slide into "it'll work out."

What it costs when nobody sees it

There is a particular kind of tired that comes from working hard for a job that pays you almost nothing. It does not show up on any invoice. It shows up on the Thursday you snap at your helper, or on the Sunday you cannot enjoy because the week was full and the money still feels thin.

And there is the other thing nobody says out loud. You feel a bit foolish. You have run this business for years, and you still sent a quote with no wages in it. It feels like something a real business would never do.

But it is very common. When you quote on site, your head is full of the work, not of yourself. Materials have prices on them. Your own time does not.

The pause by the van

You say you will send over the start date by evening. You walk to the van. You sit with the door open and the tea going cold on the dashboard.

You ask yourself the question you have been avoiding: what now?

The honest options are all a bit uncomfortable. You could do the job as quoted and treat it as a lesson. You could go back to the client and have a calm, clear conversation about what changed. You could look at which parts of the quote are fixed and which parts have room.

What you do not have to do is panic. The job has not started. You still know more than you did an hour ago, and that is worth something.

A good first step is small and quiet: write down what the job actually needs to earn you, before you decide anything else. It is hard to decide well when the number is only a feeling in your stomach.

(What you decide to say to the client is yours to decide. If the quote is a binding contract, it can be worth checking where you stand, and a professional can help with that.)

The part where it clicks

Later that week, at the kitchen table, you notice something. Every time a quote goes wrong, it goes wrong in the same place. Not in the materials. Not in the maths. It goes wrong because you have no fixed spot where your own wage lives.

The gravel has a line. The skip has a line. You do not.

It does not need to be complicated. It needs a place where the job is priced the same way every time, with materials, hours, a margin, and you in it, so that you can see the real number before you say yes to anyone.

A different Tuesday

Picture the same garden, a few months on. Same cold tea, same client asking about compost bins.

This time you take out your phone. You enter the turf, the gravel, the hours. The total comes up, and next to it, in plain figures, what is left for you. It looks fair. Or it does not, and you say so now, kindly, before anything is signed.

You say, "I'll have the full quote with you by this evening." And you mean it, with no knot underneath.

On Friday you keep the receipts for the job together, so the year-end scramble is not waiting in the glove box. You can see who has paid and who has not. You close the laptop at six, and the evening is yours.

It is not dramatic. It is just quieter.
