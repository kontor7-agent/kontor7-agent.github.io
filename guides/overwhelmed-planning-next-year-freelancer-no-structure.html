Overwhelmed Planning 2027 as a Freelancer? The Spa Day

2026-10-21

You are wrapped in a white towel, sitting on a heated stone bench, and you cannot relax.

It is a Saturday in late October. Your sister booked this spa day for your birthday, and you said yes because it sounded like a good idea. The room smells of eucalyptus. Someone nearby is whispering. There is a sign on the wall: Phones stay in the lockers.

Your phone is in the locker. Your mind is not.

Your mind is in next year.

The year with no outline

You are Ines, an illustrator, five years in. Nobody at the spa knows that. Nobody here asks what your plan for 2027 is.

But you ask yourself, over and over, on the warm bench, with your eyes closed.

How many projects do you need? Which months were thin this year? Was it March or April that nearly broke you? When do you take a real holiday, the kind where you don't check the inbox in the toilets of a rest stop?

You don't know. That's the honest answer.

You have a notes app with three half-lists. You have a calendar full of client calls but nothing that says this is the year I want. You have a vague feeling that you should raise your prices, and an equally vague feeling that you can't, not yet.

Every time you try to plan, it turns into a fog. So you stop. Then the fog is still there the next day.

What the fog costs

It doesn't look like much from outside. Nobody sees it.

It looks like saying yes to a project in December because you're afraid of January. It looks like a week off that you spend half-working because you never decided it was really off. It looks like a Sunday afternoon where you open a blank document called "2027 plan", stare at it, and close it again.

It costs you a little calm every single week. And a feeling you rarely say out loud: that other people running their own business seem to have a map, and you have a mood.

In the cooling room, you lie on a lounger and think, I'm supposed to be resting, and I'm doing admin in my head.

The woman with the pencil

On the next lounger is a woman reading a paper notebook. She has a pencil behind her ear. She is not reading, actually. She is filling in a small grid, checking something against the page before it, and humming a little.

You are not someone who talks to strangers in towels. But the fog has made you slightly reckless.

"Is that a planner?" you ask.

She laughs, a bit embarrassed. "It's my year. I do it here every autumn. It's the only quiet place I have."

She doesn't give a speech. She doesn't tell you what to do. She says only this: "I used to think planning meant predicting. It doesn't. I just need somewhere to put things so they stop circling."

Then she goes back to her grid.

You lie there for a long time with that sentence. Somewhere to put things so they stop circling.

The small, quiet turn

Here is what lands, slowly, like warmth coming back into your hands.

You have been treating the whole year as one enormous decision. It isn't. It's twelve months, and each month is four or five weeks, and each week is a page. A page can hold a few things. It doesn't have to hold everything.

You don't need a perfect forecast. You need a place that shows the shape: where the holidays go, which weeks are full, which clients owe you what, so the big question breaks into small ones you can actually answer.

It doesn't have to be like this. The fog isn't a character flaw. It's what happens when a year has no container.

A different Monday

Picture a Monday in February, next year.

You make coffee. You open a printed page on your desk. It's a weekly spread, with a few lines for the week and a small space for what matters most. You write three things. You tick one off by ten.

A client emails about a spring project. You glance at the month: the second week of April is already half-full, and you've pencilled a few days off in May. So you answer calmly, with real dates, and you don't wince.

There is a log beside you where invoices go. You don't need to remember who owes what. It's written down in your own handwriting.

At six you close the laptop. The year isn't solved, but it is held. Your head is not the only place it lives.

That's the whole difference. Not more discipline. Just somewhere for it all to sit.

Back at the lockers

After the spa, you collect your phone. Eleven notifications, none urgent. You stand in the car park with wet hair, and for once the fog feels smaller. Not gone. Smaller.

You don't need to plan all of 2027 tonight. You only need to start with one page.
