Client Said Payment Is Coming, 3 Weeks On? The Taxi Rank

2026-10-25

The taxi rank outside the station has one car and one driver, and neither of them is in a hurry. You stand beside your suitcase with your phone in your hand. It is 8:40 on a Thursday morning, and you are on your way to a workshop for a different client.

You are not looking at the train times. You are looking at your banking app.

Nothing. Again.

Three weeks ago, Jonas, a freelance copywriter with a small, tidy business, sent an invoice for a product-page project. Four days later the client wrote: "Thanks, payment is on its way!" Jonas replied with a smiley. Then the days went by, and the smiley started to feel like a mistake.

The sentence that settles nothing

Here is the odd thing about "payment is on its way." It is not a lie, and it is not a promise. It sits in the middle, where nothing can be done with it.

If you write again, you might look impatient. If you don't write, you look like you've forgotten. And if you ask when exactly, it feels like accusing a nice person of something. They have always been friendly. They said thank you twice.

So Jonas does what many people do. He opens a new email, types "Hi Anna, just checking in on…", and deletes it. He types "Quick reminder that…" and deletes that too. One version sounds like begging. Another sounds like a debt collector. The third sounds like nothing at all.

The driver finally opens his window. "Where to?"

"Not yet," Jonas says, which makes no sense, and he laughs at himself.

What three weeks quietly costs

The invoice is not huge. That is almost the problem. If it were huge, Jonas would make a call. Because it is only medium, he lets it sit, and the sitting has a price.

It costs a little concentration in every meeting. It costs a small flinch each time the phone buzzes. It costs the Sunday evening when he told himself he'd write the message "tomorrow." He also notices he has started to plan his month around money that has not arrived: rent on the first, a new monitor he is pretending not to want.

And it costs something harder to name. A faint feeling of not being a real business. Real businesses, he thinks, have a department for this. They have someone whose job it is to say "Our records show…" without their stomach turning over.

He has no department. He has a phone at a taxi rank.

The turn

The driver is patient, so Jonas lets himself stand there a minute longer. Something occurs to him that he has never quite put into words.

The problem is not that he doesn't know how to write a polite email. He has written product pages for nervous brands for years. He can make a return policy sound warm.

The problem is that every time, he starts from zero. Each late payment arrives as a fresh little emergency, with no plan and no wording, and no sense of whether this is the moment for a friendly nudge or something firmer. He has to invent the whole thing, while already feeling uncomfortable.

What if the wording already existed? What if there were a calm first message, a calm second one, and a rough sense of when each one belongs, all decided on a quiet day instead of at a taxi rank?

Then "three weeks" would not be a mood. It would just be a step on a list. Ordinary and neutral, like checking a parcel's tracking number.

A different Thursday

Picture the same morning, only calmer.

Jonas sees the invoice is now past its date. He doesn't feel his chest tighten, because he knows what comes next. A short, friendly message goes out, one that mentions the invoice number, the amount and the original due date, and asks a simple question about when he can expect it. It takes two minutes. It sounds like him, only steadier.

Then he closes the laptop and walks to his workshop with his hands free.

The rest of the week is unremarkable, which is exactly the point. The client replies with an apology and a date. Or perhaps they don't reply yet, and Jonas already knows the following step and roughly when it is due. Either way, he is not carrying the question around in his head anymore. He is not rehearsing sentences in the shower.

On Monday morning he looks at one overview and sees who is on time, who is a little late, and who needs a next step. He doesn't feel like someone chasing money. He feels like someone running a business, which is what he has been all along.

The kind of calm you can borrow

Nobody enjoys this part of freelancing. But it gets lighter when you are not improvising it every time. Clear wording takes the sting out of it, and a simple order of steps takes the guessing out of it.

The taxi driver, by the way, is still waiting. Jonas finally gives him the address of the workshop, gets in, and puts the phone face down on his knee. The message can wait twenty minutes. He knows roughly what it should say.
