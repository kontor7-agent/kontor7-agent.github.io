Sold Out and Didn't Know? The Driving Test Waiting Room

2026-10-23

10:42, a plastic chair, and a phone that won't stop

You are sitting in the waiting room of the driving test centre. Your nephew is somewhere out on the ring road with an examiner and a clipboard. You promised his mother you would be there afterwards, whatever the result.

The chairs are bolted together. There is a poster about mirror checks. Your phone buzzes against your knee.

It is an email from Henrik, who runs a small deli two towns over. You know the name well. He bought from you twice last winter.

"Hi, I'd like 24 jars of the smoked chilli oil for our Christmas hampers. Can you send them by the 5th? Went to your shop and it says sold out. Is that right? Thought you always had it."

You read it twice. Then you read the last line again.

Sold out. On your own shop. For how long?

The number you didn't have

You don't know. That is the part that makes your stomach drop. Not that you ran out, since running out of a good thing is almost a compliment. It is that you can't say when it happened.

You think back. The last big batch went out to a market order. A few single jars sold online after that. You meant to check the shelf in the spare room. You meant to look at the notebook, the one with the tally in the margin, the one that is currently in a different bag.

So you do what you always do. You try to rebuild it from memory, in a plastic chair, with a stranger's toddler kicking the leg of the table in front of you.

Twelve left on the 14th? Then the weekend order. Then those three from the newsletter...

It doesn't add up. It never does when you do it from memory.

The worst part is the quiet arithmetic underneath. Twenty-four jars is not a small order for you. It is a good week. And somewhere in the last three weeks, other people probably clicked on that same page, saw the same word, and left without telling you.

Henrik at least wrote. Most people don't.

What it quietly costs

Nobody warns you about this kind of loss. It doesn't show up anywhere. There is no red line in a bank statement for the order that never came.

But you feel it. You feel it as a small flush of embarrassment, the same one you get when someone asks a simple question about your own business and you can't answer. Do you have it? When can you get more? You make the thing with your own hands, and you can't say how many exist.

It makes you feel like a hobbyist. It is not true, and you know it is not true. But on a plastic chair at 10:50 with a customer waiting, it feels very true.

You type a reply and delete it. You type another. You write "so sorry, let me check" and stop, because checking is exactly the problem. You would have to go home and count.

In the end you write something honest and short. You tell Henrik you'll confirm by this evening and that you're sorry for the confusion. You hit send. It isn't a disaster. It just isn't the way you wanted to meet a good customer.

The door opens

Your nephew walks out ten minutes later. He is trying not to smile, which means he passed. He fails completely at not smiling. His mother will cry on the phone in the car park, and you will pretend you aren't also a little teary.

In the car, with the engine off, he asks what's wrong. You say, "Nothing. Work thing. A customer wanted to buy and I was out of stock and didn't know."

He shrugs, seventeen and newly licensed. "Can't you just look at how many you have?"

You laugh. Then you stop laughing, because he is right. That is exactly what you want. Not a clever system, not software with a monthly fee. Just to look, and see it. How many are left, what's running low, what you should have started making last week.

The idea is almost embarrassingly simple. You have been running the shelf out of your head, and your head was never meant to be a warehouse.

An ordinary Monday, later

Picture it a few weeks on. Nothing dramatic has happened. That is the point.

It is Monday morning. The kettle is on. You open one file, and it takes less time than the kettle does. You see the numbers as they actually are. The smoked chilli oil is running low, and it is already marked, quietly, as something to restock. Nobody had to remind you. Nothing is a surprise.

A message arrives from a customer who wants twelve jars. You glance at the screen, you know the answer, and you reply in one minute with a date you can stand behind. No let me check. No evening spent with a notebook and a torch in the spare room.

The shelf stops surprising you. That is the whole change. You still make the oil by hand, you still hate labelling, you still run out of tape. But the not knowing, the low hum of it, has gone.

And when someone like Henrik writes, you can say yes, or tell him exactly when.
