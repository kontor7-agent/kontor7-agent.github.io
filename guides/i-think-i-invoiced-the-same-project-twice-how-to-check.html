Invoiced the Same Project Twice? The Vending Machine Wait

2026-10-22

Your phone buzzes against the plastic chair at 10:12. You are in a hospital corridor, third floor, waiting for your dad's appointment to finish. The laptop is in your bag. The coffee in your hand came from the machine by the lift, and it tastes of the cup.

The email is from the accounts person at your client. Two lines.

"Hi, we have two invoices that look like the same project. Could you confirm which one is correct?"

Your stomach drops before your brain catches up.

Two invoices. Same project. You read it again, as if it might change.

The Bit Where You Replay Everything

You think back. There was the first invoice, sent in September, right after the handover. Then the project dragged. A few extra rounds, a late sign-off. Maybe you sent a "final" invoice in October, because the first one felt too early, or too small, or you simply forgot it existed.

Did you? You honestly don't know.

That is the worst part. It isn't that you might have made a mistake. Everyone does. It's that you can't say, right now, standing in a corridor that smells of disinfectant, whether you did.

So you do what tired people do. You open your email on the tiny screen and scroll through Sent. You search the client's name. Eleven results. Some are invoices, some are "just checking in", one is a PDF called invoice_final_v2. You squint at it. The numbers blur.

A nurse walks past with a trolley. You close the app, open it again, and close it once more.

What It Quietly Costs

Nobody warns you about this kind of worry. It's not dramatic. It's small and sticky, and it follows you into the rest of the day.

If you did bill twice, you have to write an awkward email and fix it. If you didn't, you have to say so without sounding defensive. Either way, the person who pays you now has a reason to wonder how organised you are. And you've spent twenty years of your life, or maybe just two, trying to feel like a real business, not someone winging it.

You think about the 12 euros of parking you'll owe. You think about the other client who still hasn't paid, whose invoice you also can't quite place. You realise you could not, if asked, name everyone who currently owes you money.

That is the real knot. Not the double invoice. The not knowing.

The Answer Was Always Sitting Somewhere

Your dad comes out at 11:20, slightly irritated, slightly relieved, asking whether there's a decent sandwich anywhere. There is. You buy two. You tell him it's just a work email, nothing serious. He nods, the way he does when he knows it's a bit serious.

In the car, you take a breath and do the unglamorous thing: you open your invoice folder, then the bank statement, then the project notes. It takes forty minutes. It takes longer than it should, because everything lives in a different place.

And there it is. Two invoices, yes. But one is for the design phase and one for the revisions. Different numbers, different amounts, different dates. Not a duplicate. Just two descriptions so similar that nobody could blame the accounts person for asking.

You write back, kindly and clearly. You attach both, say what each covers, and say you're happy to help if anything looks off. The reply arrives within the hour: "Perfect, thanks!"

The relief is real. But so is something else, sitting underneath it. The suspicion that you were lucky. That next time the answer might not be so tidy, and you'd have found out the hard way.

The Turning Point Is Quieter Than You Think

It doesn't arrive as a revelation. It arrives as a small, tired thought while you're eating a cheese sandwich in a car park: it shouldn't take me forty minutes to know what I've invoiced.

It shouldn't. Not for one project. Not ever.

Somewhere there is a simpler way to work. One place where each client has a name, each project has its invoices lined up underneath, each invoice has a number, a date and a status. Where "did I already bill this?" is a ten-second glance, not a forensic investigation. Where an email from accounts is just an email.

Nothing clever. No software to learn. Just a calm overview you open on Monday morning and close again.

An Ordinary Monday, After

Picture it. It's Monday, 9:05. The coffee is yours, from your own kitchen. You open one file. The overview shows what went out last week, what's due, what's overdue and what's been paid. Your eye catches one line in amber, a client who is three days late. You make a note to send a friendly nudge after lunch.

Nothing else needs you. No scrolling through Sent. No guessing.

When the accounts person writes, "quick question about two invoices," you answer in under a minute, because you can see exactly what each one is. You don't even put down your toast.

That is what we mean by calm admin. Not perfection. Just not being surprised by your own business.
