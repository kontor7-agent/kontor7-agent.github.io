How Many Weeks Off Can I Afford as a Freelancer? The Ferry Queue

2026-10-13

The ferry queue moves one car length. Then stops again.

You are sitting in the passenger seat with a paper cup of coffee gone lukewarm. Your partner taps the steering wheel and says, lightly, "So. How long do you think we could go next summer? Three weeks? Four?"

You say, "Probably. Let me think about it."

You do not think about it. You smile at the gulls and feel your stomach tighten.

The answer you don't have

Let's call you Mara. A freelance illustrator, six years in. Good clients, a decent year. Nobody would call her business shaky.

And still she cannot answer a simple question: how many weeks off can I actually afford?

She knows roughly what came in this year. She knows roughly what goes out. But "roughly" is a fog, and a holiday is exactly the kind of thing that makes fog feel dangerous.

So she does what many self-employed people do. She takes the time off with one eye on her phone. Or she doesn't take it at all and tells herself the sea will still be there next year.

What the fog costs

The cost is rarely dramatic. It is small and daily.

It is the week at the lake where you check email at breakfast "just in case." It is saying yes to a project in August because turning it down feels irresponsible, even though you were so tired in July you reread the same page three times.

It is the quiet resentment, too. Employees get their weeks without a calculation. You get yours with a knot attached.

And it is a strange feeling of not being a "real business." A real business, you think, would simply know.

The question underneath the question

Back in the queue, Mara realises something while the engine ticks.

"How many weeks can I afford?" is not one question. It is several quiet ones wearing a single coat.

How much do I want to earn this year? How much is already on its way? Which months are usually thin? When do clients tend to go quiet anyway? And what does a week of nothing actually cost me, once I look at it plainly rather than imagining the worst?

She has never put those in one place. They live in her head, her bank app, three old quotes, and a vague memory of last March.

No wonder the answer feels like a guess. It is a guess, because the pieces are scattered.

The turning point, at the railing

The ferry finally loads. Mara gets out, stands at the railing, and watches the harbour slide away.

It occurs to her that people who seem relaxed about time off are not necessarily richer or braver. They simply can see their year. The target is written down. The work already booked is written down. The weeks off are written down too, as part of the plan and not as a guilty exception.

Holidays stop being a gamble. They become a line in the year, sitting next to everything else.

She does not need a perfect forecast. She needs one calm page where the year lives, so the answer to her partner can be something other than "let me think."

A normal Monday, afterwards

Picture a Monday some weeks later.

Mara opens one file with her coffee. It takes about ten minutes. She sees where the year stands against what she hoped for. She sees what is in the pipeline, and which quarter looks light. She sees the three weeks in August marked as time off, and next to them, a plain picture of what that means for the rest of the year.

No drama. Perhaps she decides to nudge one quote, or to line up a bit of work for the thin autumn stretch. Perhaps she realises a fourth week is fine, or that two long weekends in spring would feel better than one long stretch.

The point is that she chooses. She is no longer bracing.

When her partner asks again, over dinner, she answers in one sentence, and it is a sentence she believes.

And in August, the phone stays in the bag. Breakfast is just breakfast.
