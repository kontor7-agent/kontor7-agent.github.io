Overwhelmed by Social Media? No Plan for What to Post This Week

2026-10-13

The dryer has 22 minutes left. You are sitting on a orange plastic chair in the laundromat, phone in your hand, thumb hovering over the little plus sign.

It's Tuesday, a quarter past ten. Your last post went up eleven days ago. A photo of a finished project, three words of caption, one heart from your sister.

You open your notes app. There are twelve ideas in there. "Behind the scenes?" "Tip about colour." "That quote." "Ask question???" None of them is a post. They are all just the feeling of I should.

So you scroll instead. Other people's weeks look so tidy. Someone's Monday has a carousel, Wednesday has a reel, Friday has a cheerful little recap. You wonder if they have an assistant. Or a secret. Or just fewer things to worry about than you.

The dryer turns. Your towels turn with it. Nothing gets posted.

The quiet cost of "I'll do it later"

Nobody warns you that posting is a second job with no hours.

You are good at your actual work. Say you're an illustrator, like Mara in this story, a character we made up for this page. When she's drawing, the day makes sense. When she's deciding what to post, the day gets foggy.

The fog has a price, and it's rarely money you can point at.

It's the Sunday evening dread. It's the half-written caption that sits in drafts for a week. It's a potential client who looks you up before replying to your email and finds a profile that stops in September. It's the small, unfair voice that says: a real business would have this sorted.

You don't tell anyone about that voice. You just post a blurry photo of your coffee on a random Thursday, feel faintly embarrassed, and go quiet again for ten days.

Then the cycle repeats. Silence, guilt, a burst of posts, exhaustion, silence.

The folded towel moment

Back to the laundromat. The dryer buzzes. The warm towels come out in a heap, and while Mara folds them she does something small: she stops asking what should I post? and asks a different question.

What did I actually do this week?

She has a half-finished commission on her desk. She has a sketchbook page she likes. She has a client who said something kind in an email. She has a question that three people have asked her this month, about how she picks her colours.

That's not twelve vague ideas. That's already a week.

The problem was never that she had nothing to say. It was that every morning she began from a blank screen, with no picture of the whole week. Each post felt like a fresh decision, and decisions are tiring when you make them alone, in a hurry, with a dryer ticking.

What she needed wasn't more inspiration. It was somewhere calm to put the things she already had, and a way to see them side by side.

A different kind of Monday

Imagine the same week, a few weeks later.

Monday, 8:40. Coffee, window open, the street still waking up. Mara opens one page and looks at the week ahead. Three posts, maybe four. One shows the commission in progress. One shares the colour tip. One is simply a thank-you to a client, with permission. They are not perfect. They are planned, which feels completely different.

She writes the captions in one quiet half hour, while her mind is fresh, not at 23:00 on the sofa. She sees at a glance which ones are ready and which still need a photo. A gap on Thursday doesn't make her heart race. It's just a gap, and she can see it from Monday.

Then she closes the tab and goes back to drawing.

That's the whole change. The posting didn't become glamorous. It became small. It stopped following her around the house.

On Wednesday, when a stranger from a bakery asks if she does labels, they look at her profile and see someone who is around. Someone steady. Not loud, just present. That's often all showing up needs to mean.

You don't need to become a content person

Here is the thing we'd like you to take from this, even if you never open another page of ours.

You are allowed to keep it simple. A few posts a week, planned by a calmer version of you, beat a frantic burst followed by silence. You don't have to chase every format. You don't need a hundred ideas, just a place where the ones you have stop slipping away.

And the voice that says you're not a real business? It's wrong. A real business is simply one that has a rhythm. Rhythm can be built, in an ordinary week, in small steps.
