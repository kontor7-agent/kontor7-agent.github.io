Dreading a Difficult Client Email? The Fitting Room

2026-10-17

The curtain is a heavy green velvet, and behind it your trousers are being pinned. You stand in your socks on a small wooden stool, holding your phone in both hands.

The email arrived four minutes ago. The subject line is just: Re: Final designs.

You haven't opened it. You can see the first line in the preview: Honestly, this isn't what we discussed, and I'm a bit surprised that…

Your stomach does the thing it does.

Let's call you Mara. You're a freelance designer, six years in, and this is a Thursday you thought was going to be easy.

The preview you can't unread

The tailor mumbles something around a mouthful of pins. You nod without hearing it.

You open the email. It isn't cruel. It's worse than cruel, because it's vague. Not what we discussed. Expected more. Can we revisit the fee?

You start composing replies in your head, right there on the stool.

The first one is apologetic. So sorry, I'll fix everything straight away, no extra charge. You can feel the money leaving as you think it.

The second one is defensive. As stated in the brief on the 3rd… You can feel the relationship leaving as you think that one.

The third one is a long, careful paragraph that explains everything and is really a plea.

None of them feel like you. All of them feel like something you'd regret at 3 a.m.

What the dread actually costs

Here is the part nobody says out loud: the email itself is maybe a ten-minute problem. The dread around it is a three-day problem.

You put the phone in your pocket and tell yourself you'll answer "properly" this evening. But this evening you'll be tired. So you'll answer tomorrow, when you're fresh. Tomorrow there will be a call, and the day after is the weekend, and the email will sit in your inbox like a stone in a shoe.

In the meantime you'll check it at the supermarket queue. You'll check it over dinner. You'll half-listen to a friend and rehearse a sentence instead.

And quietly, the cost adds up. A reply that goes out three days late sounds less sure than one that goes out the same afternoon. A reply written while anxious tends to give away more than you meant to. And the thought underneath it all, the one you'd never say to a colleague, because you don't have one: a real business would know what to say.

You do know your work. What you don't have is the right words, ready, at the moment your pulse is up.

The curtain moment

"Step down, please," says the tailor. "Let me look at the hem."

You step down. You stand there in the pinned trousers, and for a second, because you're not looking at a screen, a quieter thought arrives.

You've written this kind of email before. Not this exact one, but its cousins. The one where a client wants more than was agreed. The one where you needed to say no without slamming a door. The one about the invoice. Each time, you started from a blank box and a racing heart, and each time you eventually found words that were fine. Calm, even. They just cost you an afternoon of feeling sick first.

What if the blank box wasn't blank?

Not a stiff template that makes you sound like a form letter. Something closer to a friend who has seen this situation a hundred times, sits next to you, and says: start with what you heard, then say what you can do, then offer one next step. A friend who hands you a rough first draft, so all you have to do is make it sound like you.

That's the whole idea. Not a new personality. Just a starting line that isn't zero.

A different Thursday

Picture the same email arriving, same preview, same slightly sour first line.

Your stomach still notices. You're human. But the next move is different. You don't spiral through three imaginary replies. You open a small collection you keep for exactly this, find the situation that's closest, and fill in the names and the details: the project, the date, what was agreed.

You read the draft out loud, quietly. You soften one sentence and firm up another. It sounds like you on a good day: warm, clear, not afraid.

You hit send before the tailor has finished the second hem.

Then you put the phone away, and for the first time that afternoon you actually look at your own reflection in the mirror, and the trousers fit.

The rest of the day is just a day. No stone in the shoe. The client may still be unhappy, and you may still have a real conversation ahead. But you've answered like someone running a real business, and you did it while the feeling was small.
