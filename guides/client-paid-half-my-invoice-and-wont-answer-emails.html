Client Paid Half My Invoice, Now Silent? The Jetty at Noon

2026-10-17

The boat rental has a jetty with peeling green paint and a hand-written price list. You are standing on it at noon on a Sunday, holding two paddles and a phone with one bar of signal.

The day was supposed to be easy. Your sister's birthday, a picnic basket, a lake. You did not plan to open your banking app next to a bucket of life jackets.

But you did. And there it is again: the same single line you have looked at nine times this week.

Half. Not the whole amount. Half.

The half that arrived

Let's call you Jonas. You edit videos for small companies. Three weeks ago you delivered the final cut of a product film, and you sent an invoice for 4,800. Ten days later, 2,400 landed in your account, with no note and no explanation.

You were relieved for about an hour. Then you started doing the maths in your head. Half is not nothing, but half is also not rent plus the software subscriptions plus the hard drive you bought for this project.

So you wrote a friendly email. Hi, I noticed only part of the invoice arrived. Could you let me know about the rest?

No answer. Four days later you wrote a second one, a little shorter. Still nothing.

Now you are on a jetty, and the person who rents out the boats is asking whether you want the red one or the blue one. You say "blue" without hearing yourself.

The questions nobody hears

The silence is loud in a specific way. It is not just the money. It is the questions that start running underneath everything else.

Did they think the film was only worth half? Did I miss something in the contract? Did the email land in spam? Is this the moment I become the annoying freelancer who keeps writing?

And under those, the quieter one: Am I even allowed to ask again?

You row out onto the lake and your sister talks about her new flat. You nod in the right places. In your pocket, the phone is a small warm weight. You catch yourself composing the third email between strokes. Too polite, and it sounds like you don't mind. Too firm, and it sounds like a threat you'll regret on Monday.

By the time you reach the middle of the lake, you have rewritten it six times in your head and sent it zero times.

What it costs when you wait

Nobody sees this part. The birthday photos will show you smiling. They won't show that you checked your bank balance twice during the cake, or that you quietly decided not to take the Tuesday off you had been looking forward to.

The unpaid half does not only sit in someone else's account. It sits in your shoulders. It makes you take a smaller job you don't want. It makes you answer other clients faster, just in case. It makes the weekend feel like borrowed time.

And the worst of it is that the problem is not really the client. It is that you have no plan for this moment. Every time it happens, you start from zero, with a blinking cursor and a stomach that has already decided how this ends.

The moment on the water

Your sister stops talking and trails her hand in the water. "You've gone somewhere else," she says.

So you tell her. Half paid, two emails, nothing back. You expect advice, maybe a dramatic suggestion involving phone calls and lawyers. Instead she asks a plain question.

"What would you tell a friend to do?"

You know exactly. You would tell a friend that there is no shame in a second, clear message. That a missing half is a fact, not an insult. That the next step should already be written down, so that nobody has to invent it on a Sunday while rowing.

The realisation is almost boring. You don't need courage on the day it happens. You need the calm part decided in advance: what you say first, what you say a week later, what you say if nothing changes, and when. Then the silence stops being a verdict on you. It becomes one more row with a next step beside it.

You put the phone away. For the first time all week, the lake is just a lake.

A different Monday

Picture the same situation, a few weeks on, with that plan already in place.

Monday morning, coffee, laptop open. One list shows what is open and what is partly paid. Beside the 2,400 there is a date, and next to the date, a short, kind, clear message that is ready to go. You read it once, change the client's name, and send it. It takes three minutes.

It doesn't beg. It doesn't apologise for existing. It says what was agreed, what arrived, what is still open, and by when you would like it settled.

Then you close the tab and start the day's actual work. The knot has not vanished, but it has a shape now, and a shape can be handled. You know what happens next if there's still no reply, and you know it will be just as calm as this email.

That is all a simple system really changes. It moves the hard decision from the worst moment to a quiet one.
