Freelance Receipts Everywhere? The Spare Room on the 28th

2026-10-22

The carrier bag has been in the boot of the car since October.

It's the 28th of December, a little after eleven in the morning, and Mara is sitting on the single bed in her parents' spare room. Her laptop balances on a pillow. Downstairs, someone is running the dishwasher and someone else is laughing at a quiz show. It sounds like a holiday.

She is not on holiday. She has the bag open on the duvet.

What was in the bag

A crumpled train ticket with the ink almost gone. A receipt for a graphics tablet pen, folded into quarters. Two coffee slips from a client meeting, which may or may not count for anything. A card reader statement. A photo on her phone of something she bought in March, and she can't remember what.

In the inbox there are three more: a software subscription, a font licence, a co-working day pass. Each one is sitting in a different folder, or in no folder at all.

She tells herself it's only an hour of sorting. She has told herself that every December for four years.

The thought she doesn't say out loud

Real businesses don't do this.

That's the sentence underneath all the others. Real businesses have a drawer, a system, someone who deals with it. Mara has a bag, a vague feeling and a deadline her accountant mentioned in an email she has read twice and answered never.

She isn't lazy. She illustrated nine book covers this year, delivered on time, and answered every client within a day. But the little paper trail of it all, the part that nobody sees, slid off her desk one week at a time. Always on a day when a client needed something right now.

And now it's all arriving at once, in a room with a poster of a band she liked at sixteen.

The quiet cost

Her mum puts her head around the door. "Tea? You've been up here ages."

"Just finishing something," Mara says.

She isn't. She's squinting at a receipt and trying to work out whether it's from this year or last. She's thinking about the tea going cold, the quiz she's missing, and the January evenings that will go the same way. The sorting is hard enough. The dread that arrives before the sorting is worse.

Nobody talks about what that costs. It isn't only the hours. It's the low hum of I should really deal with that, which follows you into a walk, a dinner, a night's sleep. It's the way a pile of paper can make a capable person feel like she's winging it.

The turning point

Mara closes the laptop lid halfway and sits still for a moment.

The problem, she realises, isn't this one bag. If it were, she'd have fixed it by now. The problem is that every receipt in the year landed somewhere different, with nowhere obvious to go. A pocket. A drawer. An email. A photo. There was never one place where things simply went.

So she doesn't sort the bag. She takes a pen and a sheet of paper and writes down a plain list of what "done" would look like for her, just a few lines. Where do receipts go? What's left to gather? Which dates are coming up? She puts a small tick next to the first line. It's absurdly small, and it feels like opening a window.

A list isn't a solution. But it's the first time in weeks that the pile has had an edge.

A different kind of January

Imagine the same room a year from now, in a different December.

There's no bag. There's a single list, and it has been filling up quietly all year: a line here when the font licence arrived, a line there after the co-working day. Nothing dramatic. Ten tidy minutes on a Friday, now and then, with a mug of something warm.

On the 28th, Mara sits on the same bed. Downstairs, the quiz show is on again. She glances at her progress, sees most of the year already accounted for, and spends twenty minutes on the last few gaps. Then she closes the laptop and goes down for tea while it's still hot.

When her accountant writes in January, she doesn't flinch at the subject line. She attaches what's asked for, and the reply takes four minutes.

The money and the work are exactly the same as they were. What changes is the knot in her stomach. It simply isn't there.

A small system, not a big project

You don't need a new accounting life. You need one calm place where receipts land, a short list of what's left for the year, and the dates in front of you instead of in the back of your mind. That's all. It's a way of seeing the year, not a lecture about doing it better.

And if your bag is already in the boot of the car, it isn't too late. Start with the list.
