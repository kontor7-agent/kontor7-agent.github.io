Hours Lost to Data and File Chores? The Dentist Waiting Room

2026-10-13

The chair in the waiting room is slightly too low. The magazine on the table is from March. You are holding your phone and, instead of reading the article you saved, you are doing arithmetic.

Monday: an hour renaming exports. Tuesday: forty minutes fixing a date column that turned into text. Wednesday: the same two files merged by hand, again, because the supplier changed one header. Thursday: a folder of PDFs sorted into client folders, one drag at a time.

None of it was hard. That is the odd part. Nothing you did this week would impress anyone. And yet you can't say where the week went.

"Mr. Keller?" the receptionist says to someone else. You exhale and keep counting.

The chores that never make it onto a ticket

Nobody assigns this work. It doesn't have a story point or a sprint. It lives in the gaps between the real work: a CSV that arrives with semicolons instead of commas, a folder called final_v2_NEW, a spreadsheet someone sends back with merged cells and a note saying "just quickly tidy this?"

You say yes, because it is quick. Each time. That is how it hides.

The cost is not only the hours. It is the way your attention gets chewed into small pieces. You sit down to do the interesting thing, the one you actually enjoy, and by the time the chores are done, the good part of the day has gone. You write the clever bit at 17:30, tired, and wonder why it feels like work.

There is also the quiet embarrassment. You are a developer. You know that repeating something by hand is the thing you are supposed to be allergic to. Yet here you are, dragging files, for the ninth Thursday in a row.

You don't mention it to anyone. It feels too small to complain about and too large to ignore.

The thought you keep pushing away

In the waiting room, a child is tapping the fish tank. You notice that your jaw is tight, and not because of the dentist.

The thought that keeps surfacing is simple: I have done this before. I will do it again next week.

You have, of course, written little scripts. Everyone has. There is a folder of them somewhere, with names like fix.py and clean2.py. Half of them only work on one machine, with one file, if you remember what you meant. When you need one, you open it, squint, and decide it's faster to do the job by hand.

So the chores stay. Not because you can't fix them, but because fixing them properly always looks like a project, and you never have a free afternoon for a project.

That is the trap. The thing that would give you time back needs time you don't have.

The small shift

What changes it is rarely a grand decision. It is more like a Tuesday where you notice you've just done the same job for the fourth time, and instead of sighing you think: this one shouldn't be mine anymore.

Not a rewrite of everything. Not a new framework. Just the plain relief of a few boring jobs being handled by something tested, tidy and already finished, so you aren't rebuilding it from memory each time.

The messy export goes in, a clean file comes out. The downloads folder sorts itself into places that make sense. The things you'd want to double-check before sending an API into the world have a calm, ordered list rather than a feeling in your stomach.

It doesn't make you a different person. It just removes a layer of friction you had stopped noticing.

A Monday that feels different

Imagine the same week, a bit later.

Monday morning. Coffee, still too hot. The supplier's file arrives with its usual quirks, and you look at it the way you'd look at a parcel you know how to open. A few minutes, and it's done. You don't think about it again.

By ten, you are already in the part of the work you actually like. The thing with the interesting problem in it. Nobody is waiting on a stray spreadsheet, and you aren't quietly dreading Thursday's folder.

At lunch you eat away from the screen. In the afternoon you finish early enough to walk home without carrying the day with you.

Nothing dramatic happened. You just stopped paying a small tax, over and over, that you never agreed to pay.

Back in the waiting room

"Your turn," the receptionist says, and you stand up, a little stiff.

On the way in you make a decision that costs nothing: this week, you will notice every chore you repeat. Not fix them. Just notice. Write them on a note. Because the first step out of wasted hours is seeing them clearly, and you have already started.
