Saturday at the Garden Centre: Freelance Invoices and Hours, Sorted

2026-10-12

The tomato seedlings are two for five euros, and you are holding four of them when your phone buzzes.

It's a client. "Hi, just checking: did you send the invoice for the March project? Our accounts person can't find it."

You stand there between the herbs and the watering cans, and your stomach does that small drop. Did you send it? You think so. Or you wrote it and meant to send it. Or the number was 2024-017, or maybe 018, and there's a file called Rechnung_neu_final2 somewhere on your laptop at home.

You type "Of course, I'll resend it today!" and put the tomatoes down. Suddenly you're not that interested in tomatoes.

The Weekend That Isn't One

Nobody warns you about this part of working for yourself. The work itself is fine. You're good at it. It's the thin layer of admin around it that never quite goes away.

There's a notes app with hours in it, half of them rounded in your head. A folder of invoices named by whichever mood you were in. A vague mental list of who has paid and who probably has. And a small, steady worry underneath it all that you can't quite put a name to.

You drive home with the seedlings on the passenger seat and the sentence from the client looping in your head. Our accounts person can't find it.

It isn't a disaster. It's just embarrassing in a quiet way. It makes you feel like a hobbyist who happens to have clients, instead of someone running a proper business.

What It Quietly Costs

The invoice you can't find is the visible part. The rest hides better.

There's the Sunday afternoon you spend reconstructing last week's hours from calendar entries and memory. There's the follow-up you postpone because you're not sure whether the payment is late or just feels late, and asking wrongly would be awkward. There's the number you invent when a client says, "Roughly how many hours did that take?"

And there's the money. Not dramatic amounts, usually. A half hour that never made it onto a bill. A small job that slipped between two months. It adds up in the background, and you only sense it as a vague feeling that you work a lot and the numbers don't quite show it.

Mostly, though, it costs you ease. You can't fully switch off, because a part of you is always holding the whole picture in your head. And your head was never meant to be a filing cabinet.

The Moment It Shifted

Back at home, you find the invoice. It was sitting in a folder, finished, never sent. Eleven days ago.

You send it, apologise briefly, and the client answers with a cheerful thumbs-up. Nothing bad happens. That's almost the strangest part. All that dread, and the world carries on.

But you sit there for a minute with the tomato plants still in the car, and a quiet thought arrives: this doesn't have to be this way.

Not a grand reinvention. Not new software with a monthly fee and forty menus. Just one place. One calm place where the hours go in as you work, where an invoice gets its number without you thinking about it, where you can see at a glance what's open, what's paid, and what's gone past its date.

A place where the question "who owes me what?" has an answer you can read, instead of one you have to remember.

A Different Monday

Picture the same week, a little later.

It's Monday, 8:30. Coffee is next to the keyboard. You open one file, and there it is: this month, laid out plainly. The hours from last week are already in, because you added them on Friday in about a minute each. One invoice is marked overdue, so you write a polite, relaxed reminder. It's not awkward anymore, because you know it's overdue.

A client asks how long the project took. You answer in ten seconds, and the number is true.

Then you close the file and get on with the actual work, the thing you're good at. No low hum of worry. No weekend reconstruction. The admin has become a small, quiet part of the week instead of a cloud over it.

That's all, really. Not more money overnight, not a new life. Just the feeling of being someone whose business is in order.
