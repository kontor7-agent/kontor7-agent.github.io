Forgot to Charge for Screws on Your Quote? The Coffee Tin

2026-11-08

It's Thursday, a quarter past noon, and you're sitting in the van with a sandwich you haven't unwrapped.

On your knee is the quote you sent three weeks ago. A built-in wardrobe, two alcoves, a client who was friendly and punctual with the tea. The quote has a line for the boards. A line for the doors. A line for your days.

There is no line for the rest.

You look at the dashboard. The old coffee tin is rattling there again, full of whatever was left over: brass screws, wall plugs, a half-used tube of sealant, hinge covers, a strip of edging tape. Next to it is a crumpled receipt from the fixings counter. You don't need to smooth it out to remember the total. You paid it without thinking, the way you always do.

It wasn't the boards that made the job thin. It was the little things, all of them.

The kind of loss that never announces itself

Nobody ever sends you an angry email about screws.

That's the strange part. A big mistake has a moment: the wrong measurement, the missed delivery, the phone call. But a few euros here and a few euros there never gets a moment. It just slides out of the margin while you're busy being good at your craft.

You bought extra plugs because running out halfway up the wall costs you an hour. You grabbed the better sealant because the cheap one yellows. You topped up the tape, the sandpaper, the blades, the fixings you'll need next week anyway. All of it sensible. None of it written down anywhere your client could see.

So you ate it. Quietly. Again.

And there's a small voice that comes with it, the one that says: it's only screws, don't be petty. You've heard it before. You heard it when you drove back for one forgotten box. You heard it when you stood at the counter and thought, I'll sort the quote properly next time.

Next time has been a while coming.

What it actually costs

It's not really about the money in one job. It's the feeling underneath.

It's finishing a week where the client is happy, the work is good, the invoice is paid, and still thinking, why does it feel like I barely made anything? It's quoting the next job from memory, nudging the number down because you don't want to lose it, and promising yourself you'll watch the extras this time.

It's the little flinch when the client says, "Oh, can you just add a shelf while you're here?" Because you know a shelf comes with brackets and screws and edging and you won't say so, because saying so feels like nickel-and-diming.

Here's the thing you've half-known for a while. Small materials aren't petty. They are part of the job, exactly like the boards. A client who hires a professional expects the professional's price to include the things the work needs. Charging for them isn't greedy. It's just completing the sentence.

The moment it clicks

You tip the coffee tin out onto the passenger seat. Not to count anything, just to look.

There it all is. The leftovers of four different jobs, mixed together. You can't say which job paid for which screw, and that's exactly the point: nobody could. It was never written anywhere. Every job just borrowed from the tin and the tin was refilled out of your own pocket.

And you think: it doesn't have to work like this. Not with a thick binder. Not with an evening of bookkeeping. Just one place where the job lives, where the materials go in as you buy them, where the small stuff has a home next to the big stuff, so that by the time you quote, it's already there. Calm and visible. A real line on a real quote.

No guilt attached. Just a number that matches the job.

A different Thursday

Picture the same van, a few weeks on.

You're parked outside a new client's house, 8:10 in the morning, a coffee going lukewarm in the holder. You've just measured the alcoves. You open the quote on your phone and build it standing up, right there on the pavement: boards, doors, your days, and a sensible allowance for fixings and consumables, already sitting in its own line, as ordinary as the rest.

The client reads it and nods. Nobody blinks. Of course nobody blinks. It's a quote for a job that has screws in it.

The following Thursday, you're eating that sandwich at noon, unwrapped, because there's nothing nagging at you. The receipts are in. The margin on the last job is a number you can see instead of a feeling you were avoiding. The coffee tin is still on the dashboard, but now it's just a tin.

You've stopped quietly subsidising your own clients.

And on Friday at four, when you pull onto your own drive, you notice you still have the energy to look at your own garden.
