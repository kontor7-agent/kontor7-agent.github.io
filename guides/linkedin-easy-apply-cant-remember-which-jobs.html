Easy Apply, Can't Recall Which Jobs? The Optician's Chair

2026-11-04

Your phone buzzes on your knee in the optician's waiting room. It's 10:20 on a Tuesday. The screen says Unknown number.

You almost let it ring out. But you've been waiting for a call like this for weeks, so you step toward the window and answer in your quietest voice.

"Hi, is this Mara? I'm calling about your application for the Operations Coordinator role."

Your mind goes white.

Which Operations Coordinator role? Which company? You press "Easy Apply" so often that the whole month has blurred into one long afternoon of blue buttons. Was it the logistics firm? The software company with the friendly logo? The one that wanted a cover letter, which you skipped because there was no field for it?

The pause you can hear

"Yes, of course," you say. Your voice sounds bright and fake.

"Great! Can you tell me a little about what drew you to us?"

Behind you, a child is reading eye-chart letters out loud to his mother. E. F. P. You stare at a poster of a smiling woman in new glasses and say something about "growth" and "team culture". You could be describing any company on earth.

The recruiter is kind. She says she'll send an email with some times. You say thank you three times, which is two too many.

When you hang up, your hands are slightly damp.

What the scramble costs

The optician calls your name, and you follow her in. Chin on the rest, forehead against the cold plastic. "Better one, or better two?"

You can't answer. You're running back through your inbox in your head, hunting for a confirmation email. Did LinkedIn send one? Did you save the posting? Postings disappear. You know that. The page you applied to three weeks ago may simply not exist anymore.

That's the part nobody mentions about Easy Apply. It takes thirty seconds to apply. It takes nothing at all to forget. Each click felt like progress, and it was. But progress with no record behind it turns into a vague, low hum of worry: did I miss something? Did someone write back? Am I about to look unprepared again?

It costs you more than the odd awkward phone call. It costs the small, steady feeling that you're a person who doesn't have this under control. You are doing a hard thing, looking for work while life carries on, and you're holding it all in your head.

"Better one," you finally say. "Or two. Sorry. Can you go back?"

The optician smiles. She's patient. She goes back.

The turning point on the pavement

Outside, with a pair of dilating drops stinging your eyes and the street a little too bright, you sit on a low wall and open your phone. The recruiter's email is already there. The subject line has a company name in it.

It's the logistics firm. Of course it is. You applied on a Sunday, between laundry and a call with your sister, and never thought about it again.

You sit there for a minute. Not upset, just tired in a clear way. The thought arrives quietly: it doesn't have to be like this.

You don't need a better memory. You don't need to apply to fewer jobs. You need one calm place where each application lands the moment you send it. The company. The role. The date. A note on what the posting said. Somewhere that shows you, without effort, who you haven't heard from and who is worth a polite nudge.

A place that also holds the other half of the story: what you'd say if someone asked, tell me about a time you handled a difficult situation.

You realise that's the real fear under the forgetting. It isn't only "which job was that?" It's "will I sound like I know what I'm doing?"

A different Tuesday

Picture the same call, a few weeks from now.

The phone buzzes. Unknown number. You glance at it, take a breath, and answer. While the recruiter says her name, you already have the right row open. You see the role, the date you applied, the two lines you jotted about what the team does. A small reminder has been sitting there since last Thursday, saying the follow-up was due.

"Yes, hi," you say. "Thanks for calling. I was hoping to hear from you."

And you mean it.

You're not performing certainty. You simply know where you are. When she asks what drew you to the company, you answer in your own words, because the details are in front of you and not lost somewhere behind a hundred blue buttons.

Afterwards you add one line: call went well, interview Thursday. Then you go back to your day, your coffee, your actual life. Your head is quieter. The search still takes effort, but it no longer takes the kind of effort that leaves you feeling foolish.
