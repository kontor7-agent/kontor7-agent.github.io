Project Stuck Waiting on Client Approval? The Held Door

2026-11-08

Ines is standing in the doorway of a co-working space at 11:20 on a Tuesday, holding the door with her hip. A man with a flat-pack wardrobe is coming through it. Slowly. One corner at a time.

Her phone is in her other hand. She is not looking at the wardrobe. She is looking at a message thread that ends with her own words, sent nineteen days ago:

"Hi Karl, the full draft is attached. Let me know your thoughts whenever suits."

Below it, nothing. Not even a thumbs-up.

"Thanks," says the man, and the door finally lets go of her.

She goes back to her desk and does what she has done every morning this month. She opens the thread. She closes it. She opens her calendar, where the delivery date still sits in cheerful blue, as though it had any say in the matter.

The kind of waiting that doesn't look like work

Nobody would call this a busy week. Ines has nothing to do on the project. The draft is done. The next stage can't start until Karl approves it. Her hours are not being used, and they are not free either.

That is the strange part. She can't start anything big, because the feedback might land tomorrow and change everything. She can't relax, because it might land today. So she answers small emails, tidies her files, rewrites her about page for the fourth time, and feels vaguely guilty about all of it.

She has two other clients. Both are fine. Both are also, she suspects, noticing that she has more time than usual. She keeps that thought quiet.

What she does not say, even to her sister, is the other worry. Is this her fault? Did she ask the wrong question? Should she have said "please approve by Friday" instead of "whenever suits"? Does a real business let a project sit for nineteen days?

She doesn't know. That is what makes it heavy.

The email she keeps almost sending

At lunch she sits on the low wall outside, eating a sandwich she doesn't taste, and drafts a follow-up.

"Just checking in!" She deletes the exclamation mark. It sounds cheerful in a way she doesn't feel.

"Just a gentle reminder." Gentle for whom? She deletes that too.

"I'm still waiting on your feedback." Too sharp. Karl is not a villain. He runs a small company with a leaking roof of his own, a new hire, and a board that, he once mentioned, "has opinions about fonts." He isn't ignoring her. He's just not thinking about her. Which, in a way, is worse.

She puts the phone face-down on the wall. A pigeon looks at her sandwich with open ambition.

And then, because there is nothing else to do, she thinks back to the beginning. Not the draft. The start. The first week with Karl.

What the first week didn't say

There had been a good call. Warm, even. Karl said, "Send me a proposal and let's go." She did. He said yes. She started.

What there had not been was a single sentence about what happens after the draft. Who looks at it. Whether it's only Karl or also the board with the opinions. How long they'd need. What happens if the answer takes a while.

She hadn't asked, because asking felt like doubting him. Like handing a new friend a contract at dinner.

So the project had a start date and a finish date and, in between, a long unmarked stretch where everything depended on a person who didn't know he was holding anything up.

The delay wasn't rudeness. It was a door nobody had agreed who should open.

It was a quiet little click of an idea, sitting on that wall. It doesn't have to be a negotiation after the fact. It can just be part of how things begin.

The same Tuesday, differently

Imagine the version where Ines had a simple kickoff in place. Nothing grand. One short call with a few clear questions, a page that says who approves what and roughly by when, and a friendly line in the welcome email: "Here is what I'll need from you, and here is what you can expect from me."

In that version, day nineteen doesn't exist. Around day five, she sends a short note that says: "As agreed, feedback is due Thursday; if the board needs a bit longer, just tell me and I'll plan around it." No begging. No gentle reminder. Just the thing everyone already nodded to.

She would still wait sometimes. Clients are people. But the waiting would have edges. She'd know whether to start the next job or hold her week open. She could close the thread and mean it.

And on a normal Tuesday at 11:20, she'd hold the door for a stranger, smile, and think about her sandwich.

Monday, later

A few projects down the line, Monday morning looks different. A new client has said yes. Ines opens one folder, sends one welcome email, and books one kickoff call. The questions are already written. The access she'll need, the people who decide, the way feedback arrives: all of it is in the open, politely, before any work begins.

Nobody feels interrogated. If anything, new clients seem relieved. People like knowing what's expected of them. It makes them look organised too.

She finishes her coffee while it's still warm. At six she closes the laptop. The calendar says Feedback due Thursday, and for once she isn't refreshing anything.
