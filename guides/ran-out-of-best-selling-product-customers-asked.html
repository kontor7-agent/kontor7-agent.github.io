Ran Out of Your Best Seller Again? The Video Call at 11

2026-10-24

It is 10:58 on a Tuesday, and Anselm is straightening the camera so the shelf of jars behind him looks tidy.

He makes small-batch chilli oil in a rented unit behind a bike repair shop. Today is the call he has been quietly proud of for weeks: a gift-shop owner two towns over wants to talk about stocking his range.

The call starts well. She likes the labels. She likes the story about the first batch made in his flat.

Then she says, "I tried to order the smoked garlic one last week. Your shop page let me add it to my basket, and then your email said it was unavailable. A couple of my regulars have asked me about it, too."

There is a pause. Anselm hears his own breath.

The thing nobody asks you about

He says the polite thing. "Ah, that one's been very popular. I'm just finishing a new batch."

It is not quite true. There is no new batch. He ran out ten days ago and did not notice. The jars on the shelf behind him are the other flavours, the ones nobody gets excited about.

The smoked garlic is his best seller. It is the one people message him about. And he found out it was gone because a stranger told him, on camera, in a nice voice.

After the call he sits there with the laptop half closed. Nothing dramatic has happened. She said she would think about it. She was kind.

But the knot is there, the familiar one. It is not really about the oil. It is the feeling of not being quite a real business, the kind that knows what is on its own shelf.

The notebook, the memory and the guess

Here is what Anselm's system looks like, if you can call it that.

A notebook by the packing table with some numbers in pencil. A rough sense in his head that "there should be about thirty left." A spreadsheet he started in spring that stopped matching reality in June. Orders arrive from the shop, from two markets and from a few shops that text him directly. Each one takes a little from the stock, and none of them tell the others.

So the stock is always a feeling, never a number. And a feeling is fine right up until the week it is wrong.

What it costs is not only the missed order. It is the quiet apology emails. It is the customer who does not come back. It is the way he now checks the shop page every morning with a small flinch, hoping nothing has gone wrong in the night. And it is the Sunday evening he spends counting jars in the unit instead of being anywhere else.

He has told himself it is just what happens when you are busy. Busy people run out of things.

That is the lie. Running out is not the problem. Running out without knowing is.

What he noticed on the walk home

He locks the unit early and walks instead of cycling. Somewhere between the roundabout and the bakery he thinks about the gift-shop owner. She did not ask for a discount or a miracle. She asked, essentially, "Can I count on you to have the thing?"

That is a very small question. It is also the whole business.

He realises he does not need to be better at remembering. He needs the shelf to tell him, plainly, before a customer does: this one is getting low, this is how long the last batch took, order the oil and the jars now. One quiet place that holds the number, so his head does not have to.

It is a modest thought. It does not feel like a breakthrough. It feels like putting a heavy bag down.

The same Tuesday, a few weeks later

Picture the other version of that call.

It is 10:58. Anselm has already glanced at one page that morning, over coffee, and it said what he expected: the smoked garlic is healthy, the chilli-lime is getting low, and the next batch of jars arrives Thursday. There were no surprises, so there is nothing to brace for.

When the buyer says her customers ask about the smoked garlic, he smiles. "It's my best seller. I can send you a first order next week, and I'd suggest a case of twelve so you don't run low either."

He says it without checking, without stalling, without the small lie. He is just a person who knows what he has.

After the call he does not stare at the wall. He eats lunch. He answers two emails. On Monday evenings he is not in the unit counting jars, because he does not need to be.

Nothing about his business changed except that the numbers stopped living in his head.

If you recognise the flinch

Maybe you do not sell chilli oil. Maybe it is candles, prints, tea, yarn or leather cases. The scene is the same: a customer tells you before your own records do, and you feel a little smaller for it.

It does not mean you are disorganised by nature. It usually means the stock lives in too many places, and nobody has given you one calm spot to look.
