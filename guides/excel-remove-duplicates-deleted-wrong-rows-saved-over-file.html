Excel Remove Duplicates Deleted Wrong Rows? Counter 31

2026-11-06

Ticket number 31

It is Saturday, 10:40, and you are standing in the butcher's queue with a paper ticket in your hand. The counter display says 27.

Your phone buzzes. It is a client, the one with the autumn event. Hi, could you send me the phone number of the caterer again? Mine seems to have vanished.

You open your contact sheet on the phone. You wanted to be helpful. You type the caterer's name into the search box.

The number is there. But it is the old one. The one that stopped working in the spring.

You scroll. Row after row of people you know. Some look right. Some look strangely thin. A florist with no email. A venue manager who had two entries last week and now has none.

Your stomach does a small, cold turn.

What you did on Thursday

You remember it now, in pieces.

Thursday afternoon. Fourteen hundred rows, exported from three places, full of the same people appearing two and three times. You wanted one clean list before the busy season. You selected the columns, clicked Remove Duplicates, and watched the little box say it had removed a few hundred rows.

It felt like sweeping a floor. You pressed Ctrl+S. You closed the laptop. You made tea.

Nobody told you the tool keeps whichever row comes first and quietly drops the rest. Nobody told you the first row was often the old one, the one with the dead phone number and the missing email, while the newer, better row sat below it. Not until now.

"Number 28," calls the butcher.

The part nobody says out loud

You would like to say you stay calm. You do not.

You do the quick maths of the damage. Which rows are gone? You cannot know. You saved over the file, so there is no "before" to compare against. There might be an old export in a download folder somewhere. There might not.

The real cost is not the file. It is the small, creeping doubt that follows you into every message now. Is this number right? Did I lose someone? Will I find out in front of a client?

You feel a bit foolish, too. It was a button in a menu. Everyone uses it. Surely a real professional would not have got this wrong.

But it is not foolish. You did a sensible thing with a tool that gave you no preview, no warning, and no way back.

"Number 29."

The turning point, somewhere near the sausages

Here is a thought that arrives while the woman ahead of you orders six chops.

What if the problem was never you? What if cleaning a list should work more like trying on a coat? You look first. You see what would change. You decide. And only then does anything touch the real thing.

You are not after magic. You just want to see which rows would go and why. You want to see which version of a person it would keep. You want a short list of every change, written down, so that if something looks off you can say "ah, that one" instead of "what happened?"

And you want the original to stay exactly where it is, untouched, while all of this happens.

That is not a luxury. It is how this should have felt from the start.

"Number 31!"

That is you. You order two chicken breasts and a piece of beef for Sunday, and you hear yourself sounding almost normal.

Monday, a different kind of quiet

Picture the week after.

It is 9:00 on a Monday. The export arrives from the booking system, as it does every Monday, with the same people appearing twice under slightly different spellings. You open nothing. You run one small tool.

It does not change your file. It shows you a preview: these rows would merge, these would stay, this is why. You skim it with your coffee. Two entries look wrong, so you fix them in a minute. Then you let it write a fresh, clean copy next to your original, along with a plain report of everything that changed.

The old file is still there, untouched. The new one is tidy. The report sits beside it like a receipt.

When the client messages on Tuesday asking for the caterer's number, you find it in four seconds, and it is the right one. You reply with a smiley. You do not think about your contact list again until the following Monday.

That is the whole difference. Not a smarter you. Just a safer way to do the same chore.
