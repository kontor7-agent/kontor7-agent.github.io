Keep Running Out of Your Best Seller? The Dog Park Shout-Out

2026-10-14

It's 7:40 on a Wednesday, and you're standing in a dog park with a lead in one hand and your phone in the other.

The phone won't stop buzzing. Order. Order. Order. A local newsletter mentioned your shop this morning. It was one kind sentence about your chilli oil, "the one everyone at the Sunday market asks for."

You should be thrilled. Instead your stomach does a small, cold drop.

Because you know what's on the shelf in the spare room. You think it's about eleven jars. Maybe nine. You haven't counted since last week, and last week you were guessing too.

The number you carry in your head

The dog tugs at the lead. You walk, but you aren't seeing the trees. You're running the maths in your head, the way you always do.

Eleven jars on the shelf. Two boxes at the post office waiting to be collected. Three reserved for the Saturday stall. Did you promise those? You're fairly sure you did.

The orders keep coming. The shop page still says In stock, cheerfully, to everyone.

This is the part nobody sees. From outside, a bestseller looks like a gift. From inside, it's a small, constant worry. You're always one good week away from letting people down.

You've done it before. A customer wrote to ask where her jar was, and you typed three apologies and deleted two. A café owner who wanted a regular order got "back in about three weeks, so sorry." You never heard from her again.

Nobody called you unprofessional. You just felt it anyway.

Why it keeps happening

It isn't that you don't care. It's that the whole picture lives in your head, next to the dentist appointment and the birthday you mustn't forget.

The supplier takes three weeks. You know that. But "three weeks" only helps if you know today that you're running low. Usually you find out when the shelf looks thin, and by then the three weeks have already started, quietly, without you.

So you reorder in a panic, pay for faster shipping, eat the margin, and promise yourself you'll get organised. Then the next busy week arrives and the promise slides away again.

Standing there in the wet grass, you do the honest sum. The mention was free. The reorder is going to be expensive. And some of the people ordering right now are going to get an apology instead of a jar.

That's the real cost. It isn't only the sales you can't make. It's the first impression you didn't want to give.

Eleven jars, forty orders

Back home, jar by jar, you count. Nine. Not eleven.

You switch the product to sold out, then to back soon, then to a waiting list, and you write one honest note to everyone who's already ordered. It's a decent note. It's also a lot of typing at 8:15, and your coffee has gone cold beside you.

The supplier says the earliest delivery is in three weeks. Of course it is.

Then, late morning, you do something small. You open a blank page and write down every product you sell, how many you have, and how long each one takes to come back. You don't make it pretty. You just see it all in one place for the first time.

And something shifts, quietly.

It turns out most of your shelf is fine. Two items are drifting low, and you hadn't noticed. One thing you reordered twice last spring is sitting there, barely moving, with money tied up in it.

The problem was never that you're bad at this. The problem was that you were carrying it by memory, and memory doesn't send you a message on a Tuesday to say now, not later.

It doesn't have to work like that. A simple list that knows your supplier's three weeks and knows what's left can tap you on the shoulder while there's still time. That's all it needs to do.

A different Wednesday

Picture the same newsletter, the same kind sentence, the same buzzing phone at 7:40.

You're still in the dog park. The lead is still in your hand. But your shoulders aren't up around your ears.

You glance at the numbers. You already ordered the next batch two weeks ago, because the list told you to when the shelf still looked full. The jars arrive on Friday. A few people may wait a couple of days, and you can say so honestly and calmly, because you know what you're talking about.

You put the phone back in your pocket and throw the ball.

That's what a normal Monday looks like when stock is under control. You open one sheet, once a week, with your tea. You see what's low, what's healthy and what's sitting around. You place an order or you don't. Then you get on with the part you actually love, which is making the thing people want.

The shelf stops surprising you. That's the whole change. It's not dramatic, but it's the difference between being grateful for a busy week and dreading one.
