Who Owes Me, What Do I Charge, Where Did My Time Go?

2026-10-13

The ferry is halfway across the water when you do it again.

You take out your phone. One bar of signal. You open your banking app, refresh it, and look at the list of incoming payments as if it might have changed in the last eleven minutes.

It hasn't.

Your partner is pointing at a lighthouse. The kids are eating crisps. You nod at the lighthouse and think: Did the Hartmann invoice go out in August or September? Or did I only mean to send it?

This is the holiday that isn't one. Your body is on the deck. Your head is in a spreadsheet you can't quite remember saving.

The three questions that follow you everywhere

It is rarely one big problem. It's three small questions that never stay answered.

Who still owes me money? You have a rough idea. A feeling, really. Two, maybe three clients. Possibly four. The feeling is not a number, and it sits in your stomach like a stone.

What should I actually charge? A new enquiry arrived on Thursday. You wrote a price, deleted it, wrote a lower one, and sent that. It felt polite. It also felt a little like apologising.

Where does my time go? You worked all week. You were busy, properly busy. But when someone asks which project pays best, you shrug. You genuinely don't know.

None of this is laziness. You are good at your craft. Nobody ever taught you to run the small business around it, and you don't have a back office to catch what slips.

What it quietly costs

Nobody puts this on an invoice, so it's easy to miss.

It costs the first ten minutes of every Monday, spent hunting for last month's figures. It costs the awkward email you rewrite before you send it, because chasing money feels like begging. It costs the quote you undercharged and then resented for six weeks.

And it costs moments like this one. A lighthouse, a ferry, a good day, half watched.

You start to wonder whether other freelancers have this sorted, and you're the only one winging it. Mostly, they're winging it too. The calm ones just stopped carrying everything in their heads.

The small moment on the pier

The ferry docks. While the kids race ahead, you sit on a bench with your bag between your feet and finally say it, quietly, to yourself.

I don't want to spend my whole life with this feeling.

Not a dramatic decision. No new business plan, no six-week course. Just a sentence, and under it a gentler one: It probably doesn't have to be this loose.

That's the turning point, and it's smaller than people expect. You don't need to become a different person. You need the money, the prices and the hours to live somewhere other than your memory, somewhere you can look at them in a few minutes and then close the lid.

A simple, steady system does that. It doesn't make decisions for you. It just puts the answers where you can see them.

A different Monday

Picture it a few weeks on.

It's Monday, 8:40. Coffee is still hot. You open one file, and ten minutes later you know three things.

You know who owes you money and since when. There's no stone in the stomach, just a short list and a calm, polite message ready to send.

You know what a new enquiry should cost, because you worked out your number once, on a quiet day, and it's written down. When the next client asks, you say your price in a normal voice and don't add an apology.

You know which project actually earned its hours and which one quietly ate your week. That isn't a verdict on you. It's information, and you can use it.

Then you close the laptop and get on with the work you're good at.

The next time you're on a ferry, you look at the lighthouse. The phone stays in the bag. Not because everything is perfect, but because you know where things stand.
