CRM Export Full of Duplicate Contacts? The Lobby at 8:40

2026-11-04

Mira is standing in a hotel lobby at 8:40 on a Thursday, holding a paper cup of coffee that has already gone lukewarm. Behind her, two staff members are unrolling a banner. In front of her is her laptop, balanced on the arm of a leather sofa.

On the screen: a CSV file with 6,412 rows.

She is a freelance event organiser. Today is the day she moves her whole client and attendee list into a new CRM, because the old one stops renewing at midnight. The new tool wants the file by noon so the reminder emails for tomorrow's conference can go out from the right place.

She scrolls. Anna Keller. Then Anna Keller again. Then A. Keller, with a different email. Then keller, anna in lowercase.

The thought arrives quietly and sits down in her chest: if I import this as it is, people will get the same email three times.

The thing nobody tells you about exports

The export looked fine when she downloaded it. Of course it did. A file always looks fine at the top.

It is only when you scroll that the history of your own business shows up. The sign-up form she changed two years ago. The spreadsheet a friend sent over after a workshop. The week she typed contacts in from business cards, in a hurry, with a pen in her other hand.

Every shortcut she ever took is in there, politely waiting.

She tries the obvious thing. Sort by name. Scan. Delete a row. Scan again. After twenty minutes she has cleared a few dozen duplicates and the row count has barely moved. Worse, she is no longer sure she deleted the right Anna. One of them had a phone number. Did she keep that one?

What it quietly costs

The deadline is not really the problem. Deadlines are survivable.

The problem is the small private dread underneath. A client receives two identical invitations and wonders whether she runs a tidy operation. A speaker's contact vanishes because the wrong twin got deleted. A person who unsubscribed last spring gets mailed again, and Mira has to write an apology she doesn't want to write.

And under that, the voice she tries not to listen to: a real business would have this sorted by now.

She is not careless. She is one person with six hundred things to do, who has been using the same list for years because it was always "fine for now."

A staff member asks where the registration desk should go. She points without looking up. Her coffee is cold. It's 9:15.

The pause on the sofa

What changes things is not a clever trick. It is a small sentence she reads later that morning, in a message from a developer friend who has heard her sigh over the phone:

"You don't have to fix it by hand. You can let something tidy the file first, look at what it would change, and only then decide."

Look at what it would change. Before anything is touched.

She reads that line twice. Not because it is complicated, but because it is the opposite of what she has been doing. For two hours she has been making irreversible little decisions on a single copy of the only file she has, hoping nothing slips.

What she wanted all along was not speed. It was being able to see. A list of "these three rows look like the same person, here is why," and the original left alone, safe, in the corner.

Something in her shoulders drops, about a centimetre.

The same Thursday, differently

Picture the other version of this morning.

Mira still stands in the lobby at 8:40. The banner still goes up crooked. But the CSV is not an ambush. She runs it through a small, boring tool she trusts, one that merges the obvious duplicates, tidies the phone numbers and fixes the stray capital letters. It gives her a plain report of what it changed and what it left for her to decide.

She reads the report like a receipt. Fourteen pairs that need a human glance. She takes those fourteen, not six thousand.

By 10:00 the file goes into the new CRM. By 10:20 she has a test email in her inbox, addressed to one Anna Keller, once.

Then she does something she hasn't done on an event morning in years. She walks over, takes a cup of hot coffee from the lobby counter, and watches the first delegates arrive with her hands free.

And the Monday after

The real gift is not that one Thursday. It is the Monday after.

The next export arrives with its usual mess, because exports always do. But now there is a routine for it, ten quiet minutes with a clear before and after, and then she closes the laptop. Nobody gets mailed twice. Nothing is lost. The list is something she can trust again, instead of something she apologises for.

It is a small thing. It also changes how a week feels. A tidy contact list is not glamorous, but it is one fewer reason to feel like you're winging it.
