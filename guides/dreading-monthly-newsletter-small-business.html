Dreading the Monthly Newsletter? The Bike Shop Bell

2026-11-09

It is Thursday, 14:10, and the bike shop is quiet.

Imagine a man called Tobias. He runs a small repair shop on his own. The bell over the door hasn't rung for forty minutes. A wheel is clamped in the stand, a half-drunk coffee has gone cold beside the till, and on the laptop there is a document called Newsletter November.

It contains two words: "Hi everyone,"

He typed them at 9:30. It is now 14:10. The cursor has been blinking at him for most of the day.

The thing you promised yourself you'd do monthly

Someone told him a newsletter was a good idea. A customer, maybe, or an article, or that quiet voice that says real businesses have one. So he set up the sign-up form. Eighty-odd people joined. Then came the first issue, which was lovely, and the second, which was fine.

By the fourth it had turned into a chore with a deadline he had made up himself.

Now the month ends and the guilt starts. Every time he opens his inbox he sees the little reminder that he hasn't sent anything since September. People signed up to hear from him, and he has gone silent.

He doesn't think of this as a marketing problem. It feels like a small personal failing: I can fix a derailleur in eleven minutes, but I can't write three paragraphs.

What it quietly costs

The writing itself would take an hour. The dread takes much more.

It is the tab he keeps open all week. It is the "I'll do it after lunch" that becomes "I'll do it on Sunday". It is Sunday afternoon, when he is meant to be out on his own bike, and instead he is half-writing sentences in his head about winter tyres.

There is a second cost, one that is harder to see. When he does write, he tries to sound like a company. We are delighted to announce. He is not delighted to announce anything. He is a man in an apron who wants to tell you that your brakes will be better if you come in before the first frost.

So he deletes it and starts again, and the bell stays quiet.

The blank page is not the real problem

Around 15:00 a customer finally comes in, a woman with a flat tyre and a toddler in a trailer. While Tobias works, she says, "I keep meaning to get the bike serviced. I only remembered because you posted last spring."

He nods and smiles. Then he realises something while his hands are covered in grease.

He has never lacked things to say. He says them all day, over the counter: why chains stretch, what to check before a long ride, why that creak is nothing to worry about. People nod and thank him and walk out a little less worried.

What he lacks is a way to start. Everything he says at the counter is easy because someone is standing in front of him asking a question. The newsletter has no one standing there. It has only a white rectangle.

A different Thursday

So picture the same Thursday, a little differently.

The bell is still quiet at 14:10. The coffee is still cold. But this time the document isn't blank. Tobias has opened a few lines with gaps in them, things like the one thing customers ask me most this month is [question], and he fills in the brackets in his own words. Then he asks an AI helper to turn his rough notes into a friendly draft. He reads it, cuts the line that sounds like a brochure, adds a sentence about the trailer-and-toddler customer (no name, no details), and changes "delighted" to "glad".

It takes twenty-five minutes.

He presses send before the school-run rush. He closes the laptop and picks the wheel back up. He doesn't think about the newsletter again until Sunday, when he is on his bike.

That is what the after feels like. It isn't dramatic. The tab is closed, Sunday is yours, and the "I haven't written in weeks" feeling is gone. Showing up regularly stops being a test of willpower and becomes something small you do on a Thursday.

It was never about being a writer

Most owners who dread the newsletter aren't bad at it. They are doing the hard part alone, which is deciding what to say, in what voice, with nothing to react to. The part that should be easy, saying what you already know to people who want to hear it, gets buried under that.

A fill-in-the-brackets starting point doesn't make you sound like someone else. It gives your own words somewhere to land. You still decide what goes out. You still read every line before it leaves.

And the same trick works for the other writing that piles up in a small business: the supplier email you've been putting off, the product description you've rewritten three times, the reply to a customer who is a bit cross. The blank page is the same one each time.
