Client Wants All the Photos, Won't Pay the Deposit?

2026-10-20

The kayak hut smells of wet rubber and sunscreen. Mira stands in the queue behind a family arguing about paddles, her phone in one hand and a paper cup of coffee in the other.

The message is from Jonas, the client from last Sunday's brand shoot.

"Hi Mira! Just realised I need all the photos by Monday for the website relaunch. Can you send the full gallery? I'll sort the payment this week."

She reads it twice. The sun is out, her friends are already on the jetty, and there is a small cold stone in her stomach.

The deposit that was always "about to arrive"

She remembers the deposit. She had written it into the quote: a third up front to hold the date. Jonas had said "of course, I'll do it tomorrow." Then tomorrow turned into the shoot itself, and the shoot was lovely. He laughed, the light was good, and she didn't want to spoil the mood by mentioning money in the car park.

So she shot the whole thing without it. She told herself it was fine. He seemed reliable. He was friendly. He had a real business, after all.

Now he wants everything. All 340 frames, the outtakes, the ones where he's mid-blink. And the deposit, which is the smaller problem, has slipped into the same fog as the balance, the gallery link and the invoice she meant to write.

"Single or double?" the man at the hut asks.

"Sorry, what?"

"The kayak."

"Single," she says, and steps aside to let the next person in.

What it quietly costs

On the jetty she sits with her feet over the water and does not look at the lake.

The worst part isn't the money. It's the way her mind starts auditing herself. Did she actually send the quote? Was it signed? Which version did he see? Had she said the gallery would be online for fourteen days or thirty? Where is that email?

She opens her inbox and starts scrolling. Quote, reply, a thread about location, a thread about the second outfit. A tab with the booking calendar. A notes app with a half-line that says "Jonas – deposit?"

Ten minutes of her Saturday are gone and she still can't say what was agreed, what was paid and what was promised. She only knows how it feels: like she is about to ask a friend for a favour, when all she wants to do is run her business.

That is what the unpaid deposit really takes. Not a few hundred euros. It takes the clean, professional feeling of the whole job, and it turns every message from the client into a small flinch.

The pause

She doesn't answer straight away. She finishes the coffee. A kayak slides past with a child in the front, shrieking happily.

And a thought arrives that is quieter than panic: This isn't about Jonas. I just don't have it all in one place.

If she could see, in a single glance, what was quoted, what was due before the shoot, what had landed and what was still open, she wouldn't be scrolling. She wouldn't be wondering whether she was being unreasonable. She would simply know. And knowing makes a calm sentence easy to write.

She thinks about the photographers she admires. They aren't tougher than she is. They just have a rhythm. A booked date means a deposit received. A gallery goes out when the balance is settled. Nobody has to feel awkward, because the order of things was never a surprise.

She types a reply on the jetty with the sun on her knees:

"Hi Jonas, glad the Monday deadline is clear. Your deposit is still open on my side, so as soon as that is in, I'll send over the full gallery. Let me know if you need the invoice again."

It is friendly. It is short. It doesn't apologise for existing.

She sends it and puts the phone face down on the planks.

A different kind of Monday

Picture it a few weeks later.

Monday, 9:05. A laptop on the kitchen table, a window open, coffee that is still hot. One sheet in front of her. Every client in a row: shoot date, deposit, gallery deadline, invoice status. Green where it's settled. Amber where something is waiting. Red where she needs to say a polite word today.

She doesn't hunt through threads. She doesn't rehearse sentences in the shower. She glances at the amber line, sends one friendly nudge, and moves on to editing.

When a new enquiry comes in, the deposit is part of the booking from the first message, the same way the date is. It isn't a favour she has to ask for. It's simply how the day gets reserved.

And when a client says, "Can I just have everything now?", she doesn't feel her stomach drop. She knows exactly where things stand, so she can answer kindly and clearly, and then close the laptop and go outside.

The work is still hers to decide. How flexible to be, which clients to trust, what to put in her terms: those stay her choices, and for anything legal she'd ask a professional. But the picture is always in front of her, and that changes how everything else feels.

Back on the water

Three minutes after Mira sends the message, her phone buzzes.

"Oh, of course, sorry! Sending it now. Can you share the invoice again?"

She breathes out. Not every story ends that smoothly, and she knows it. But this time she didn't spend her Saturday in a fog of maybes.

She pushes the single kayak off the shore. The water is cold and bright, and for the first time in two days her shoulders come down from around her ears.

That evening she makes herself a promise: the next shoot gets its own line from the very first email. Client, date, deposit, gallery deadline, invoice. Everything in one place, nothing living in her head.
