Bathroom Quote, Tiles Ate Your Profit? The Wet Saw Pause

2026-10-24

It is Thursday, 2:15 in the afternoon. The wet saw has just gone quiet, and you are kneeling on a half-tiled bathroom floor with a cut tile in your hand.

The driver is in the hallway. He wants a signature for the second delivery of tiles. Nobody told you there would be a second delivery. You told yourself that, because it is easier than the truth.

The truth is that you quoted this job standing in the doorway, eleven days ago. The client held the tile sample up to the light and said, "This one, I think." You looked at the room, did the sums in your head and wrote a number on the back of your hand, then in a text message. It felt fair. It felt like a good, confident number.

Now you are doing the sums again, on your knee, with a pencil stub.

The number you can't unsee

The tile you quoted for was not the tile they chose. The one they chose is the nicer one, which costs more per box. Then there is the cutting waste, because the room has corners and a niche and a window reveal. Then the adhesive, the grout, the trim strips and the waterproofing. Somewhere in there is the petrol for three extra trips to the supplier.

None of it is dramatic. It is a small amount here and a small amount there. Added up, it has quietly taken your whole margin and started on your wages.

You sign for the delivery. You say thanks to the driver, and you mean it, because it isn't his fault.

Then you sit on the edge of the bath and do the thing you always do. You count the days left on the job and the pounds left in the quote, and you try not to divide one by the other.

The call you don't want to make

At 3:40 the client rings. She is lovely. She wants to know if the tiles look right and whether you think a little shelf in the niche is possible. "Not a big thing," she says.

You say yes. Of course you say yes.

You hear your own voice, warm and easy, and underneath it a small tight knot. It is the same knot you get when a quote goes out too fast. You are a good tradesperson, and your work is the kind people recommend over a garden fence. But you are also someone who priced the job from a feeling, and the feeling was wrong.

Nobody talks about this part. You can lay a floor flat to within a millimetre and still lose money on it, because the money was decided in a doorway, before a single tile was cut.

What you quietly pay for

The cost isn't only the pounds. It is the drive home with the radio off. It is the weekend, when you open the receipts folder and it turns out to be a carrier bag on the passenger seat. It is the next quote, which you now write a bit higher out of fear, then lower out of guilt, until it is just a guess with a worried face.

It is also the small thought that you are not a real business. A real business would know. A real business would have looked at the tile, the waste, the time and the trips, and arrived at a number it could say out loud without flinching.

You are a real business. You just never had a place where all of that sat together before the number went out.

The pause that changes the next job

Here is what you notice, sitting on the edge of that bath. This job is not going to be fixed. The quote is sent, the tiles are bought, the shelf is already promised. You will finish it properly, because that is who you are.

But the next one doesn't have to start in a doorway.

Imagine the next quote. You stand in the same kind of bathroom with the same sample in the client's hand. This time you have one simple sheet on your phone or laptop. You put in the room, the tile, the waste, the trim, the hours and the trips. You see your margin appear next to the price, quietly, before you say anything out loud.

So when she says, "Could we do the other tile?", you don't flinch. You say, "Sure, that changes the number a little. Let me update it." You say it calmly, because the real figure is right there in front of you.

That is all the sheet changes. It doesn't make you a different tradesperson, it just moves the sums to before the job instead of after.

A Monday that feels different

Picture a Monday a few weeks on. The van is loaded, and the receipts from last week are already in one place, not in a carrier bag. You know which jobs are paid and which one still owes you. You know, roughly, what each job actually earned.

There is no knot when the phone rings. When someone asks for a price, you can say "I'll send it this afternoon" and mean it, because the afternoon is calm and the number is solid.

And when Friday comes, the evening is yours.
