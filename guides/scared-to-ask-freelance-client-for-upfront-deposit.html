Scared to Ask a Client for a Deposit? The Revolving Door

2026-11-05

It's 8:52 on a Tuesday, and you're standing outside a glass office building with your laptop bag cutting into your shoulder.

The kickoff is at nine. The client is lovely. The project is a full brand refresh, the biggest job you've had this year.

And you still haven't asked for a deposit.

You watch the revolving door turn. A man in a lanyard walks through it. Then a woman with two coffees. The door keeps going round, and so do your thoughts.

I should have asked. I meant to ask. I wrote the sentence in an email on Friday and deleted it.

The sentence you keep deleting

You know the sentence by heart. "Before I begin, I'll need 30% upfront." It looks perfectly reasonable on the screen. Then you imagine the pause on the other end of the line, and it turns into something else.

What if they think you don't trust them?

What if they say, "Oh. We usually pay on completion," and you hear yourself say, "Of course, no problem," because that's what your mouth does when it panics?

The awkward part is that you know all this. You've read the posts that say "just ask." They don't help, because the problem was never information. It's the small hot feeling in your chest, the one that says: a real business wouldn't have to ask. A real business would have it all sorted.

So you tell yourself it'll be fine. They seem nice. The scope is clear enough. You'll bring it up after the first call, once the mood is good.

You've told yourself that before.

What the quiet version costs

You remember the last project that started this way. Nothing dramatic happened. It just got uncomfortable, slowly.

The first draft went out. The feedback came back late. Then the scope grew by an inch, then another, and each time you thought, I can't push back now, I haven't even been paid anything.

By week five you were working on a Saturday morning with a cold cup of tea beside you, feeling a small, specific resentment. Not at the client. At yourself, for starting without a footing.

That's the cost nobody puts in a quote. It isn't only the money that arrives late. It's the way you start to shrink inside the project. You say yes more. You apologise more. You check your bank app in the supermarket queue and put the phone away quickly, as if the number might notice.

All of it traces back to a sentence you didn't say on day one.

The door stops turning

At 8:56 you step into the lobby and sit on a low sofa that is far too soft. Your laptop is open on your knees. You are not preparing a speech. You're just looking at the notes you made last night.

And you notice something odd. The deposit isn't really the scary part. The scary part is that you are about to improvise it. You'll have to invent the wording, the timing and the tone, live, while someone smiles at you across a table.

If the question were simply one line on a page the client had already seen, you wouldn't be asking anything. You'd be walking through a step that was always part of the way you work.

That thought lands more gently than any advice has. It isn't "be braver." It's "don't be alone with this moment."

You close the laptop. You'll go in, you'll be warm and honest, and you'll tell them you'd like to sort out the practical bits properly after today. You also decide that this is the last project you start like this.

A different Tuesday

Picture the same morning another way.

A week earlier, a welcome email went out. It was friendly, short and clear. It said what happens first, what you'll need from them, and how the first payment works. The client replied with a single line: "Great, all makes sense."

Now it's 8:52 again, and you're in the lobby with a coffee. Nothing is hanging over you. The kickoff agenda is already agreed. The access details are ready, and the scope is confirmed in writing. When someone asks "So, how does this work?", you smile, because the answer already exists, and it isn't a confession.

The call is easy. You leave at ten past ten, and you walk to the station without composing apologies in your head.

The project still has its bumps. But you're standing on solid ground, which changes how you hear every later request. When the client asks for "one tiny extra thing", you can answer calmly, because the start was calm.

That's the quiet gift of a proper beginning. It makes you feel like what you are: someone running a real business.
