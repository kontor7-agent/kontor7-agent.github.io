Tutoring Parents Always Pay Late? The Phone Repair Kiosk

2026-10-20

The man behind the kiosk counter takes your phone, turns it over and says, "Twenty minutes, maybe thirty."

So you stand there between a shop selling phone cases and a stall selling pretzels, with nothing in your hands. No screen to hide in. That is the problem. Without the phone, your head fills the silence with the one thing it has been circling all week.

The Hartmann family. Three weeks late. Maybe four.

The question you can't answer on the spot

Imagine Leila. She tutors maths and exam prep, mostly teenagers, three afternoons a week and Saturday mornings. She is good at it. Students leave her kitchen table a little less afraid of numbers than when they arrived.

What she is not good at is the part after the lesson.

Standing at the kiosk, she tries to work out how much the Hartmanns owe. Was it four lessons or five? Did Jonas cancel on the Tuesday, and was that more than a day before? Did she say it was fine, because he had a fever, and then forget she'd said it?

She doesn't know. The answers are scattered across a notes app, a calendar, a few chat messages and her own memory, which at 4 pm on a Thursday is not a reliable witness.

And that is exactly why she hasn't written to them. Not because she's shy. Because she can't state a number with a straight face. Asking for money you can't quite name feels worse than asking for money you can.

The message you keep rewriting

She has drafted it before. Several times, in fact.

Hi Mrs Hartmann, just a quick reminder about the last invoice…

Delete. Too stiff.

Hope you're well! No stress at all, but whenever you get a chance…

Delete. Now it sounds like she's apologising for existing.

The truth is that Mrs Hartmann is kind. She always says thank you. She remembers Leila's birthday. She is also, somehow, always "about to do the transfer." Nobody is behaving badly. That makes it harder, because there is no villain to be annoyed at. Just a small, soft knot in Leila's stomach every time the family's name appears on her screen.

And the knot has a cost, which she rarely says out loud. It costs her a little warmth at the next lesson. It costs her the clean feeling of a good session, because somewhere underneath she's thinking, I'm teaching for free again. It costs a Sunday afternoon, spent half-reading old messages to piece together who owes what.

It also costs the quiet thought she doesn't like: Maybe I'm not a real business. Maybe I'm just a person who helps kids and hopes for the best.

Thirty minutes at a kiosk

The man calls her name. Screen replaced, phone warm from the machine.

She doesn't open the draft. Instead, something else surfaces. She remembers a conversation with another tutor, a casual chat after a workshop. The woman had said, almost in passing, that she never chases anyone anymore. "I just look at the page on Monday," she'd said. "It tells me who's behind. Then it's not a feeling. It's a line."

Leila hadn't thought much of it then. Now, walking past the pretzel stall, it lands differently.

The awkwardness was never really about the asking. It was about not knowing. If she could see the lessons taught on one side and the lessons paid on the other, the gap between them would just be a fact. Not a judgement of anyone. Not a confrontation. A fact she could mention in two calm sentences.

It doesn't have to be a ritual of dread. It could be a ten-minute habit.

A different Monday

Picture it a few weeks on.

Monday morning, coffee, the kitchen table still empty of students. Leila opens one file. Last week's lessons are already in it, because she added each one right after the session while the pencils were still warm. Payments sit next to them. One family shows a small gap, plainly visible. Another shows a late cancellation, with the amount it cost her written next to it, not as a grudge, just as a number.

She writes one short message. Friendly, specific, no apology. "Hi, a quick note: two lessons from the 3rd and the 10th are still open. Here's the total. Thank you!"

Send. Done. The knot never gets a chance to form.

Later in the day, something else turns up in the same file, something she hadn't expected: a clear picture of what her hours add up to. Whether her rate still fits the work she does. Whether it might be time to raise it, gently, at the start of the next term, and say so without a speech.

She closes the laptop at six. The evening is hers.

If you recognise the kiosk

Maybe your version isn't a phone repair stall. Maybe it's the bus, or the car outside, or the hallway after a lesson where a parent says, "Oh, I'll sort that this week."

The feeling is the same. You didn't go into teaching to become a collector of small debts. You went in because you like the moment a student gets it.
