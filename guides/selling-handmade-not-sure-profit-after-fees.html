Handmade Profit After Fees? The Payout at the Checkout

2026-10-16

The notification in the checkout queue

It's Thursday, a quarter past five, and you are third in line at the supermarket. One basket, pasta and oat milk. Your phone buzzes.

Your payout has been sent.

You open the banking app, the way you always do, with a small flicker of hope. The number is lower than you expected. Not terrible. Just lower. Lower than the month felt.

The month felt busy. Twenty-three orders, a market weekend, and that long Sunday folding linen tea towels into tissue paper while a podcast played in the background. Your wrists still remember it.

The woman ahead of you is packing her bags slowly. You do the sum in your head, the way you always do. Orders, minus something, minus something else. You lose track somewhere around the shipping labels.

The cashier says, "Next, please." You tap your card and realise you have no idea whether you are paying for these groceries with profit or with savings.

The question nobody asks out loud

Let's call you Hanna. You block-print tea towels and tote bags, and you sell them online and at a market stall twice a month. Nobody told you that you'd become a bookkeeper, a packer, a photographer and a customer service desk. You just wanted to make beautiful things with your hands.

The question you don't say out loud is this: Am I actually earning anything?

You say "it's going well" when people ask. You post the happy photos. Your sister says, "You should do this full time!" and you nod and smile and change the subject.

Because here is what you know. There are listing fees. There are payment fees. There is the shipping you sometimes absorb because "free delivery" sounded friendly. There's the ink, the linen, the packaging, the stall fee, the petrol to the market. And somewhere in all of that is a price tag you chose one afternoon because it felt about right.

Felt about right. You've been running a business on about right.

The kitchen table, the next morning

Friday, you try to settle it. Coffee, laptop, a pile of printed order summaries. Two notebooks sit beside you: the market one with its cash-box scribbles, and the online one with its half-finished columns.

You start with the bestseller, the mustard-yellow towel with the little fern pattern. Everyone loves it. It sells out at markets. It is, in your mind, the one that keeps you afloat.

You try to work out what it really earns. You find the linen receipt, but the linen was bought in a bulk roll. You remember the ink, but not the price. The fee on the last sale is somewhere in a PDF you'd have to hunt for. After twenty minutes you have three different answers and a cold coffee.

The worst part isn't the maths. It's the feeling underneath it. That you're not quite a real business. That a real business would simply know.

The turning point is quieter than you think

Nothing dramatic happens. No bad month, no scary letter.

It happens at the next market, on a grey Saturday morning, when a customer picks up the fern towel, turns it over, and asks, "Is this your best price?"

You open your mouth to say yes. Then, to your own surprise, you hear yourself think: I don't know what my lowest price is.

Not because you're shy about money. Because you honestly don't know where it stops being worth it. You say, "That's the price, I'm afraid," a bit too quickly. She buys it anyway. But on the drive home you keep thinking about that gap, the gap between I made something people want and I know what it earns me.

And then a simpler thought arrives. Maybe the problem was never your product. Maybe it was that you've never been able to see it: cost, fees and what's left, one product at a time, in one calm place.

It doesn't have to be guesswork. It could be a page where you put in what the thing costs you, and it tells you what's left. That's all. That's the whole idea.

A different Thursday

Picture the same supermarket queue a few weeks on.

The phone buzzes. Your payout has been sent. You glance at the number and, this time, it doesn't surprise you. You already know roughly what to expect, because you know which products carry the month and which ones quietly cost you more than they bring in.

You know the fern towel earns a decent amount, and you know the small tote bag barely covers its own fees. You decide, calmly, over a cup of tea, to nudge one price up and let another go. No drama. No guilt.

At the next market, when someone asks, "Is this your best price?" you smile and say, "It is." And you mean it, because you've seen the numbers and they hold.

The evenings change too. Orders and market takings end up in one place instead of two notebooks. You close the laptop earlier. The knot you carried around without noticing, the one that tightened every time someone said "you should do this full time," starts to loosen. Not because you earn more overnight. Because you finally know where you stand.
