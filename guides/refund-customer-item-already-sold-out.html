Had to Refund an Order Because It Was Sold Out? The Birthday Lunch

2026-10-14

The soup has just been served when your phone buzzes against your knee.

You are at your mother-in-law's birthday lunch. The good tablecloth is out. Someone is passing the bread. You glance down, as discreetly as you can manage.

New order: Hand-dyed merino, "Dusk", 4 skeins.

Your stomach does a small, familiar drop. You are almost sure you only have three.

The shelf you thought you remembered

Let's call you Mara. You dye wool in your spare room, and on Saturdays you stand at a market stall with a cash box and a thermos. Orders come in online the rest of the week. It is a real little business, and it runs on a good memory.

Your memory said: plenty of Dusk.

But on Saturday morning, a woman at the stall bought two skeins for a cardigan. A friend took one more. You wrote none of it down, because the queue was long and the coffee was hot and you thought, I'll sort it tonight. Then there was the drive home, the dinner, the tiredness.

Now it is Sunday. Somebody is waiting for four skeins you do not have.

"Everything alright?" asks your sister-in-law.

"Just a customer," you say, and smile with your mouth only.

The refund nobody sees

After the soup, you slip out to the hallway, between the coats and a framed photo of a wedding from 1994. You open the shop and count in your head, then check the shelf in your mind's eye. Three. Definitely three.

So you write the message you have written before. I'm so sorry, one of the skeins sold at a market, and I didn't catch it in time. I've refunded your order in full.

It is polite, and honest. It also feels like standing in a doorway with your hat in your hands.

The refund itself is small. What stays with you is quieter than that. The customer picked a colour, imagined a scarf, maybe told someone about it. You took that, and you gave an apology back.

And there is the other thing, the one you don't say out loud: a real shop would know what it has.

What it quietly costs

The money is only part of it. There is the payment fee that doesn't come back. There is the review you now half-expect. There is the afternoon at the lunch table where you laugh a beat too late, because part of you is still in the hallway.

There is also the habit it creates. You start checking the shop on your phone at odd moments. You hold back from listing things you could sell, because you aren't sure they're there. You overbuy a fibre you don't need, just in case. Another time you underbuy, and the bestseller runs out in the week it matters most.

You call it "keeping an eye on things." Really, you are carrying the whole shelf in your head, all day, every day, and your head was never meant to be a warehouse.

The sentence you didn't expect to think

Back at the table there is cake. Your nephew is explaining something about dinosaurs. You nod, and somewhere between the candles and the second coffee, a plain thought arrives.

It doesn't have to live in my head.

Not a grand plan. Just a small, almost embarrassing idea: one place where every skein is written down. A number that goes down when you sell and up when the new batch dries. Something that taps you on the shoulder when Dusk is getting low, long before a customer finds out for you.

Nothing clever. Just the shelf, written down, so you can stop guarding it.

You finish your cake. For the first time since the buzz under the table, your shoulders come down.

An ordinary Monday, later

Picture a Monday a few weeks on.

The kettle clicks off. You open one sheet while the tea cools. You can see what you have, what is running low, and what you ought to order from your dye supplier this week rather than panic-ordering in a fortnight. It takes a few minutes. You close it.

On Saturday, the market is the market again. A woman asks if you have more Dusk, and you answer from knowing, not from hoping. "Two left. I'm dyeing more on Wednesday." She buys one. You write it down in ten seconds, between customers, and hand over the change.

No hallway. No hat in your hands. When an order arrives on Sunday, the phone buzzes and you let it. It's lunch.

The odd thing is how professional it feels. Not because anything got bigger, but because the surprises got smaller. You are still one person, with a spare room and a thermos. You just stopped finding out about your own stock from your customers.

If you saw yourself in the hallway

If you have ever refunded someone because the thing they paid for was already gone, and you only found out afterwards, you know this particular knot. It isn't carelessness. It's what happens when a small business outgrows memory and a scrap of paper, quietly and without asking.
