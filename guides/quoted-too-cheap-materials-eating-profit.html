Quoted Too Cheap, Materials Eating Profit? The Pickup Line

2026-11-01

It is 15:05, and you are third in the queue outside the primary school, engine off, windows down a crack.

The cup holder is full of paper. Timber merchant, garden centre, the fuel station near the ring road. A crumpled slip from the Tuesday you had to go back for more screws. You smooth them out on your knee and start adding on your phone.

The job was a raised bed, a short fence and a gravel path for a couple on Alder Road. You priced it standing in their garden, with a cold coffee in your hand and a nice view of their hydrangeas. You said a number. They said "lovely, when can you start?" You felt the little glow of being wanted.

Now the phone says the materials alone are close to what you quoted for the whole job.

You add it again, as if the numbers might have changed in the last ninety seconds. They haven't.

The number you said out loud

Here is the thing nobody tells you about quoting on site. The number comes out of your mouth before it has been anywhere else. It comes from the last similar job, from how much you hope they'll say yes, from the fact that August was thin and you would like September to be better.

You didn't forget about timber. You just remembered last year's timber. The path edging was a different price. The delivery fee was something you meant to mention and then didn't, because the client was smiling.

And your own time? That went into the quote as a vague feeling. "A few days, probably." It is now day six.

The car behind you taps its horn, politely. The queue has moved. You start the engine, roll forward two lengths, and turn it off again.

What it quietly costs

You are not going to lose your house over this one job. That isn't the point.

The point is the small, sour feeling that sits under everything else this week. You smile at the client over the fence and think, I'm working for almost nothing here. You say "no problem" when they ask for the gate to be a little wider. You hear yourself say it and you hate it.

You also know the part you rarely say out loud: you've done this before. Another job, another spring. You promised yourself you'd price properly next time, and then next time came and you were standing in someone's garden again, and the number came out of your mouth again.

It feels a bit like not being a real business. Real businesses, you tell yourself, know their margins. You know roughly where your van is and roughly what's in your wallet.

Then the school door opens, and your daughter comes out with her bag half open and a drawing flapping in her hand. For a few minutes, none of it matters.

The kitchen table, a week later

The job finishes. It isn't a disaster. It isn't a good job either, and you know it. You tell yourself you'll make a proper system, and you remember the last three times you told yourself that.

But this time, on a Sunday morning with the kettle on and nobody needing anything, you do one small thing. You write down the next job's materials before you say any number. Not on the back of a receipt. In one place, with a line for your own days, a line for the delivery, and a line for the screws you always forget.

It takes fewer minutes than you expected. And something odd happens. The number you end up with is higher than the one in your head.

You look at it for a while. It feels strange, and then it feels fair. It isn't greedy. It's simply what the job costs, with you included.

A different kind of Monday

Three weeks on, you're standing in another garden, this time a patio and two planters for a man called Dev who keeps apologising for the mess. He asks what it'll come to.

You don't say a number from memory. You say, "Give me until this afternoon, and I'll send you something clear."

He says, "Of course."

That's it. That's the whole thing. Nobody gasps. Nobody walks away.

In the van, you enter what you know. You see the materials, your time, a margin that you chose on purpose rather than discovered by accident. When the quote goes out that afternoon it feels steady, like a handshake you meant.

A week later, the receipts from the merchant don't go in the cup holder. They go into the same place as the quote, so you can see the job as it actually unfolds: what you planned, what you spent, what's left. If the timber goes up halfway through, you notice on Wednesday and not in a school queue on Friday.

And on Monday morning, you also know who still owes you. No scrolling through bank messages. You just look, and you know.

The knot in your stomach, the one that lived there since spring, isn't gone completely. But it's much quieter. You can hear yourself think again.
