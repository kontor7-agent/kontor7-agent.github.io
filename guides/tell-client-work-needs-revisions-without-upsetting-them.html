Need to Tell a Client Their Work Needs Revisions? The Lift Doors

2026-10-14

It's 11:52 on a Wednesday, and you're standing in the lift of a glass office block, going down to the ground floor.

You have forty minutes before a call. In your bag, a laptop. On the laptop, a file from a client. The file is the thing you were hired to build on, and it isn't right.

Not a disaster. Just wrong in a few places. The logo files are low resolution. Two pages of copy contradict each other. A section of the brief changed last week, and the draft hasn't caught up.

She sent it with a sentence you've read four times: "So excited about this! Let me know what you think!"

The lift doors open. You don't get out. You press the button again and go back up.

The sentence that won't write itself

Let's call you Mara. A freelance brand designer, six years in, works from a rented desk and her kitchen table.

She isn't afraid of the work. She's afraid of the message.

In her head, she's already drafted it eleven times. "Thanks so much, a few things need changing." Too blunt. "I think maybe we could look at…" Too soft, and it sounds like she's the one who made the mistake. "As per the brief…" That one has a little edge, and she deletes it fast.

The client is lovely. That's part of the problem. Lovely clients are the hardest to disappoint.

What it quietly costs

This is the part nobody talks about. The email is three sentences long. The worry around it takes up half a day.

It costs you the lunch you eat standing up. It costs you the evening where you're technically off, but your mind is still turning the words over. It costs you a little confidence too, because somewhere underneath you start to wonder whether a real professional would find this easy.

And sometimes it costs you money. You say nothing. You quietly fix the problems yourself, on your own time, then send the polished version as if the first round had been fine. The client never knows. The hours are never billed. The next round arrives, and you do it again.

Most people in this spot aren't bad at their job. They're just doing a very human thing: avoiding a small moment of friction by paying for it in a much bigger, slower way.

The coffee cart

Mara gets out of the lift on the second try. There's a coffee cart by the revolving door, and she stands in the short queue holding her phone.

A friend of hers, a copywriter, once said something offhand over a long lunch. She didn't say it as a tip. She said it like a fact of her week. "I don't write those emails from scratch anymore. I start from something that already sounds like me, and I change the details."

Mara hadn't thought much of it at the time. Now she does.

Because the problem has never been her ability to say things. In the room, on a call, she is clear and kind. What stops her is the blank box with the blinking cursor, where every word feels like it has to be perfect and permanent.

A blank box is a heavy thing. A first draft is light.

What if the hard part, the tone, the order of the sentences, the thank-you before the but, was already sitting there? What if she only had to put in the names, the three specific issues, and the date she'd like the next version by?

She pays for her coffee. She doesn't write anything perfect. She writes something plain and honest in about four minutes: what's working, what needs to change, what happens next. She reads it out loud once, under her breath, in the street.

It sounds like her. It sounds like a professional who isn't scared. She sends it.

What the reply looks like

The reply comes before her call starts. We won't pretend it always goes this smoothly. Sometimes a client pushes back, sometimes they need a day. But this time it's short:

"Oh, of course, thanks for catching that. Can you send the updated version Friday?"

That's it. No drama. No hurt silence. The thing she'd carried up and down in a lift all morning weighed almost nothing once it was out of her head and on the screen.

An ordinary Monday, later

Imagine a different version of the week.

It's Monday, 9:15. You open your inbox with tea beside you. A client has sent feedback on a draft, and one part of it needs a polite no, because it's outside the original scope. Another needs a gentle "this will need another round."

A few months ago, either of those would have eaten your morning. Now you know the shape of the reply. You open something that sounds like you on a good day, fill in the details, change a sentence so it fits this particular person, and send it. Six minutes, maybe less.

Then you get on with the work you actually enjoy.

Nothing about the clients has changed. They're still people with opinions and deadlines. What's changed is that you're no longer afraid of the blank box, and that fear was the expensive part.
