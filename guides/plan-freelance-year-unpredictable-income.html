Planning a Freelance Year on Unpredictable Income: The Paint Counter

2026-10-14

The machine at the hardware store shakes your tin of paint for four minutes. Four minutes is a long time to stand still.

So you do what you always do when you stand still. You take out your phone and open the calendar. It is Wednesday, mid-October, and you are looking at next year.

January is half full. February has one booking and a question mark. After March there is just white space, and the white space has a sound. It sounds like your own heartbeat.

Let's call you Marta. You write copy for small companies, on your own, from the box room that is now going to be a pale sage green. You are painting it because you told yourself that a real business deserves a proper office. You are also painting it because it is cheaper than thinking about the thing you are actually avoiding.

The question nobody asks out loud

The thing is simple, and it is hard. How much will I earn next year?

You don't know. Nobody who works for themselves knows. One client might double their budget, another might vanish after a reorganisation, and a third has been "about to confirm" since August.

So you have a habit. You hold the whole year in your head as a vague fog. Some months the fog is warm and nothing bad is in it. Other months it is cold, and you lie awake doing sums with numbers that aren't real.

The cost of that habit is quiet. You say yes to a project you don't want because the white space scares you. You say no to a week off because you can't tell whether you've earned it. You don't book the train to your sister's because you're not sure about May.

And there is a smaller cost. Whenever someone asks, "So how's business?", you say "Good, busy!" in the same bright voice you use for everything. It is not quite a lie. It is not quite true either.

What the tin doesn't know

The machine stops. The man behind the counter lifts the tin, wipes the lid, and presses it shut with the heel of his hand.

"Sage," he says. "Same as the sample. It'll dry a bit lighter."

You nod. Then he says something offhand, the way people at counters do. He mentions that the shop orders its paint by the season, not by guessing. Spring is busy. August is slow. January is a mess of people who got a gift voucher. They don't know exactly how many tins they'll sell, but they know the shape of the year.

The shape of the year.

You carry the tin to the car, and the phrase comes with you.

You have been treating the year as a number you must predict correctly. A single number, right or wrong, pass or fail. But a shop doesn't predict. A shop knows its slow months and its busy months, and plans around them. It doesn't pretend August is March.

You could do that. You could stop asking "How much will I earn?" and start asking smaller questions that have honest answers. What do I need each month? What is already booked? What is only hoped for? When do I want to be away, and what would that week actually cost me?

None of these need a crystal ball. They only need a place to put the answers, so they stop living in the fog.

A different Monday in January

Picture it. It is the first Monday of the new year, and the kettle is on.

The room is sage green and smells faintly of paint that has finally dried. You open one file. Not six, just one. You can see the year as a whole: the months that are comfortable, the months that are thin, and the two weeks in August that you marked as yours before anyone else could claim them.

A prospect emails and asks if you can start in March. You don't feel your stomach drop, and you don't feel a rush of relief that tricks you into saying yes to anything. You look at March, see there's room, and answer in a calm voice.

In April, one client goes quiet. It stings, but it is not a catastrophe, because you can see it in the picture and you can see what to do next. You have a place for the hoped-for work and a place for the confirmed work, and you know which is which. When a quarter ends, you spend twenty minutes looking at what really happened against what you thought would happen. You don't judge it. You just adjust, and the next quarter starts a little wiser.

Nothing about this makes your income certain. A freelance year is still a freelance year. What changes is that you are no longer alone with the fog. You are looking at something you can name.

And when someone asks, "So how's business?", you can answer honestly, in a normal voice.

Before the paint dries

You don't have to solve the whole of next year this week. You only have to stop carrying it in your head.
