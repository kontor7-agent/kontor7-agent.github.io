Students Paying Late for Lessons? The Staff Room Printer

2026-10-19

Wednesday, 8:50 a.m. The staff room printer at the language school is jamming again, and you are standing next to it with a lukewarm coffee and your phone in your hand.

You are Mira, an illustrative tutor. Three mornings a week you teach a group class here. The rest of your week is private lessons: English for a nurse, Spanish for a retired couple, exam prep for a man who changed careers at forty-five. Adults, all of them. Good people.

The printer whirrs and gives up. You open your banking app instead, because a thought has been sitting in your chest since last night.

Did Daniel pay for October?

You scroll. There is a transfer from someone with a similar name. Is that Daniel, or his wife? Is it for October, or the two lessons from September that he moved? You cannot tell. You close the app and open your notes. A line says "D. – 3x?? check." You wrote that yourself, and you have no idea what it means.

The question you can't answer

The printer starts up again, and someone from the front desk walks in for the stapler. You smile, and you wait until the door closes.

Here is what nobody tells you about teaching privately. The teaching is the easy part. You like the Tuesday when the penny drops. You like the small victory when a student says a sentence without translating it first.

The hard part is the in-between. The lesson that was moved. The lesson that was cancelled two hours before, which you meant to charge for and then didn't, because the student was sorry and it felt mean. The lesson that you taught and never wrote down because you ran to the next one.

So the money lives in your head, in a notes app, in a calendar, and in a bank statement. Four places, and none of them agree.

And the worst part is the feeling. Asking a student whether they've paid feels like checking their pockets. You don't want to be that person. So you wait. You tell yourself it will arrive. Sometimes it does. Sometimes you find out three weeks later that it never left.

What it quietly costs

The cost is not only the money, though that is real. It's the small tax on every lesson.

You sit across from a student who is lovely and funny, and underneath the conversation a small voice is counting. Is she behind? Is it two lessons or three? Do I say something today?

You don't say anything. You teach the subjunctive with a smile. Then you walk to the bus stop and rewrite the polite message in your head for the fourth time.

It also costs you a certain kind of dignity. You know what a business looks like, and you are quietly afraid yours looks like a favour you do for people.

The printer finally coughs out your worksheets, warm and slightly crooked. You take them to the classroom. For ninety minutes, you are fully there. Then the class ends, the room empties, and the question comes back, because it was never really gone.

The sentence you didn't expect to think

On the way out, you pass the notice board, covered in flyers. A guitar teacher. A tutor for school exams. A choir. Each of them is a person like you, with a calendar and a bank app and the same quiet question.

And it occurs to you, standing there with the worksheets under your arm: I don't need to be braver about asking. I need to be able to see.

That is a different problem. Brave is hard. Seeing is only a matter of having one place where every lesson is written down once, with a date, a rate and a yes or no for paid. A place that shows, without you doing arithmetic, who is a lesson or two behind. Where a late cancellation is a line you can look at, not a guilty feeling you carry around.

If the picture is already in front of you, the message to a student isn't a confrontation. It is just a friendly note with a date in it.

The Monday after

Picture a different Monday.

You make tea. You open one file and spend ten minutes there, no more. The week's lessons are logged. Two students show as paid up. One shows a gentle amber, a lesson behind. You send a short, warm message: "Hi Daniel, quick note – the October lessons are still open on my side. Here are the dates. Thanks!" No dread, no rewriting.

You can also see, at a glance, what the last-minute cancellations added up to this month. It's a number you decide what to do with. Maybe you keep waiving them for some students. Maybe you change your policy. Maybe you notice it's time to raise your rate, and you can say so calmly, because you can see what your hours are worth.

Then you close the laptop. The morning is still yours. The printer, somewhere across town, can jam without you.
