OneDrive Sync Deleted Files, No Backup? The Seed Stall

2026-11-06

Tobias is standing at a seed stall on a Tuesday morning, 9:20, holding a paper bag of sweet pea packets in one hand and his phone in the other.

He is a garden designer. Two-person-sized dreams, one-person-sized business. He has a client walk-through at eleven, and he only wanted to check one thing: the planting plan for the Hartmann terrace.

The OneDrive app on his phone says: This folder is empty.

He refreshes. He closes the app. He opens it again. Still empty.

The seed seller asks if he wants a bag for the packets. Tobias hears himself say "yes, thanks" in a normal voice, as if his stomach hasn't just left his body.

The tidy-up that spread

He knows what happened, or he thinks he does. Last night he cleaned up an old laptop he was about to sell. He deleted a pile of folders from it, because they were "just copies". Everything was in the cloud anyway. That's what the cloud is for, right?

Except the cloud wasn't a second copy. It was the same copy, wearing a different coat. Delete in one place, and the other places obediently agree. Quietly, politely, everywhere at once.

Three years of projects. Plans, plant lists, site photos, quotes, the spreadsheet with his real costs. No external drive in the drawer. No folder he had zipped "just in case". He had always meant to. It was on the list, somewhere below invoicing and above learning to say no.

The walk back to the car

He sits in the car and doesn't start it.

There is a particular kind of silence that comes with this. It isn't dramatic. It is the quiet of someone running through every client in their head and asking: what do I still have?

The Hartmann plan is gone. The photos of the muddy before-state, gone. The quote he sent in March, the one he can't quite remember the numbers of. He thinks about the eleven o'clock walk-through and the sentence he doesn't want to say: "I'll need to send you that later."

He also thinks something less polite. A real business wouldn't do this. He thinks it in the voice he usually keeps for the evenings when he feels like he's winging it.

That voice is wrong, by the way. Nearly every self-employed person has a version of this morning. Sync feels like safety. It is convenience. Those are different things, and the difference is invisible until the day it isn't.

What he does with the next hour

He does the sensible, unglamorous things. He looks through what the service itself might still be holding. He emails the Hartmanns' assistant and asks for the PDF he sent them, which is, humbly, sitting in their inbox. He digs through his sent mail for attachments.

Some of it comes back. Not all of it. He will spend the rest of the week piecing together what he can, and some things will simply be rebuilt from scratch.

At eleven, he stands on the terrace with a printed plan from the client's own copy and a pencil. The walk-through goes fine. Clients mostly care about the garden, not your filing system. That's the one kind thing about the whole morning.

But driving home, he makes himself a promise. Not a heroic one. A small, dull, useful one: never again will the only copy of his work depend on one drive and one afternoon of tidying.

The Monday it stopped being scary

Picture a different Monday, a few months later.

Tobias makes tea. The laptop wakes up. Somewhere, without any fuss, a copy of his work was taken overnight, a snapshot as it looked on Sunday, and one from the week before, and one from the week before that. Older ones tidy themselves away on a sensible schedule. A small logfile sits next to each run, saying what happened, in plain words.

He doesn't look at it. That's the point. He knows he could.

If he deletes a folder by mistake, or the laptop goes in a canal, or he tidies the wrong machine again, the copy is somewhere that sync doesn't reach. It doesn't care what he did last night. It simply holds Sunday.

That's the whole feeling. Not cleverness. Not a dashboard. Just the ordinary calm of knowing a bad hour has a floor under it.

And the voice that says "a real business wouldn't do this"? It has less to say. Because a real business has a copy. Yours can too.
