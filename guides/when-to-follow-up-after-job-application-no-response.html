No Response After Applying? When to Follow Up: The Jigsaw

2026-10-31

It is Tuesday afternoon, and you are at your parents' kitchen table with a thousand-piece jigsaw of a harbour. The box says fishing boats at dusk. So far you have found the edges and a lot of sky.

Your phone lies face down next to the lid. You turn it over, check the mail app, and turn it back. Nothing. Again.

You are here for a week, officially to rest between jobs. Your mum calls it a holiday. It doesn't feel like one, because every hour has a small question running underneath it.

Is it time to follow up yet?

The question under the sky pieces

You have sent eleven applications in three weeks. Two got an automatic "thanks for applying". The rest went quiet.

Now your mind keeps doing the same maths. One company, you think, you applied to ten days ago. Or was it twelve? Or was it the other one, the one with the nice careers page, that you sent on the ninth? You can't remember whether you wrote to a named person or to the general address.

So you can't decide whether a follow-up would be sensible or a little desperate. And it's worse than that. A message sent too early might look impatient. A message sent too late might look like you've lost interest. And a message sent to the wrong company, about the wrong role, is the one that makes you wince at 3 a.m.

So you do nothing. You place a piece of sky and look at your phone.

What the quiet costs

It isn't dramatic. That is why nobody talks about it.

It costs you the walk you were going to take, because you might miss a call. It costs you the film, because half your head is back in the inbox. Your dad asks, kindly, "Any news?" and you say "Not yet" in a voice that closes the subject.

It also costs you something quieter: the feeling that you are on top of your own life. You are a capable person. You have run projects, met deadlines and talked to difficult people. Yet you can't say with confidence which company you wrote to last Wednesday.

The waiting is hard enough. The not-knowing what you are waiting for makes it heavier.

The envelope

On Wednesday it rains, properly, the kind that keeps everyone indoors. Your mum is doing the crossword. Your dad is asleep in his chair with the newspaper on his chest.

You find an old envelope in the drawer and turn it over. You write the company names down the left side. Next to each one you write the day you sent the application, as best you can reconstruct it from your sent folder.

It takes eleven minutes.

And something shifts. Not because the envelope is clever, but because the fog has edges now. Two of the roles are clearly still fresh and need nothing from you. Three have been quiet long enough that a short, friendly note would be perfectly normal. One you will probably let go, and that is allowed too.

None of it is a verdict on you. It is just a list of dates, and a list of dates is something you can look at without your stomach tightening.

You realise you were never really asking "when should I follow up?" You were asking "where do I even stand?" The first question has no single answer that fits everyone. The second one you can answer in an afternoon, once it's written down.

The Monday after

A week later you are home again. It is Monday, 8:40, and the kettle is just clicking off.

You open one page. Every application is a row: the company, the role, the date you sent it, the date you'd like to look at it again, and a short note on what happened. One row says follow up Thursday. You see it and feel nothing alarming at all.

On Thursday you write two friendly lines to a real person, thanking them and asking whether there is an update on timing. You press send and close the laptop.

Then you do something unremarkable. You go for the walk. You don't take your phone out of your pocket until you're back at your front door.

The replies will come or they won't. That part was never fully in your hands. But the part that was in your hands, knowing what you sent and when, now sits quietly on a page instead of circling in your head. When a recruiter does ring, you'll know within seconds which job it is.

The jigsaw is still on your parents' table, mostly sky. That is fine too.
