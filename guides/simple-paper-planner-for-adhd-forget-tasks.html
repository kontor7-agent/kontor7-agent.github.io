Forget Tasks When You Look Away? The Kitchen Doorway

2026-10-23

It is 10:40 on a Thursday morning, and Mara is standing in her kitchen doorway holding an empty mug.

She has no idea why she came in here. Coffee? Maybe. But the mug is already in her hand, so it can't have been that. Something about an email. Or a invoice. Or the thing the client said at the end of the call.

Thirty seconds ago it was perfectly clear. She had even thought, good, I'll do that right now. Then she stood up, took four steps, and the thought slid out of her head like a coin through a hole in a pocket.

(Mara is made up. But you may know her.)

The tasks that vanish when you look away

Mara works alone as an illustrator. No office, no colleague who says "didn't you promise Jonas that file?" Just her, a laptop and a long list of things that feel important for exactly as long as she is looking at them.

That is the strange part. She is not lazy. She is not careless. When a task is in front of her, she cares a great deal. It is only when she looks away, at a notification, at the kettle, at a bird on the fence, that it is simply gone. Not postponed. Gone, as if it had never been said.

So she has tried the clever things. A notes app with eleven lists. A task manager with colour-coded labels she set up on a hopeful Sunday. Sticky notes on the monitor, which by Wednesday formed a small yellow beard around the screen and which she no longer saw at all.

Everything lived on the device where the distractions also live. Opening the list meant opening the very thing that pulls her away.

What it quietly costs

The cost is not dramatic. That is why nobody talks about it.

It is the reply she meant to send on Tuesday, which she remembers on Friday in the supermarket queue, with a small hot flush of embarrassment. It is the "sorry, slipped my mind" she has written more often than she would like to admit. It is the afternoon she spends not working but scanning, trying to feel whether she has forgotten something, a low hum of suspicion underneath every hour.

And it is a quieter thought, the one that arrives when the hum gets loud: a real professional wouldn't need this much effort just to remember things.

That thought is unfair. But it is persistent.

The doorway, again

Back in the kitchen, Mara puts the mug down. She does not remember. She walks back to the desk, sits, and stares at the screen as if it might confess.

On the corner of the desk lies a sheet of paper she printed last week and half ignored. Plain. A date at the top. Three empty boxes under a heading that says what matters today. A small space for a few other things. Nothing else.

She had printed it mostly out of tiredness with apps. It was not a plan to change her life.

Now, almost out of spite, she picks up a pen and writes one word in the first box: Jonas file.

Then she remembers the rest. The thing the client said. The invoice. She writes those down too, two more boxes, and stops. Three. That is all the page asks for. It does not want eleven.

The pen scratches. The paper does not ping. It does not offer her a notification or a news headline. It just sits there, in the same spot, holding the thought she would otherwise have lost.

A different Monday

Now picture a Monday a few weeks on.

Mara sits down with her tea at 8:30. Before she opens anything on the laptop, she pulls the page toward her and writes the day's three things in pen. It takes about two minutes. Some mornings it takes less.

When she gets up to refill the kettle, the page stays where it is. When she comes back, her eyes find it first. She does not need to remember. She only needs to glance.

On Friday she doesn't scan her own mind for what she might have dropped. She looks at the week, sees what got done, and what slid to next week, and moves it on without drama. A tick is a small, honest pleasure. A task that slid is just a task that slid, not a verdict on her character.

Nothing about her has been fixed or replaced. Her mind still wanders to the bird on the fence. But now the things she cares about have a place to wait for her, somewhere that isn't her head and isn't a screen.

At six, she closes the laptop and the hum is quieter than it used to be.

Why paper, why simple

It isn't magic, and it won't suit everybody. Some people do well with apps and good for them. Whether or not you have a diagnosis, if your tasks tend to evaporate the moment your attention shifts, a very plain page you can see may be kinder than another clever system you have to remember to open.

The trick, if there is one, is how little it asks. Three things for today. A look back at the week. Room for the bigger projects without turning them into a wall of text. Nothing to maintain, nothing to catch up on when you miss a day. You can skip Tuesday and just start again on Wednesday, with no streak to feel guilty about.

This is a gentle tool for organising your day. It isn't medical advice or treatment, and it doesn't replace support from a professional if you want that.
