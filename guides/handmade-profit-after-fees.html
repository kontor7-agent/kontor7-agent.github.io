Handmade Profit After Fees? The Book Club Question

2026-10-21

It is the second Thursday of the month, and the book club has moved to Hanne's living room. Somebody has put out a plate of shortbread. Somebody else is holding your tote bag.

"Tilda, this is gorgeous," says Priya, turning it over to look at the stitching. "So how is the shop going? Are you actually making money with it?"

Everyone looks up, kindly. Nobody means anything by it.

"Oh, it's going really well," you say. "Busy. Lots of orders."

All of that is true. And you have no idea whether the answer to her question is yes.

The sentence you've said a hundred times

You make crocheted plant hangers and canvas totes. You sell them on Etsy, and on the first Saturday of the month you stand behind a trestle table at the town market with a flask of tea and a cash tin.

The orders keep coming. The notifications still make you smile. Some weeks you wrap parcels at the kitchen table until your wrists ache, and you think: this must be working.

But "busy" and "profitable" are not the same word. Somewhere underneath, you know it.

There is the listing fee, and the transaction fee. There is the payment fee, and the little extra for the offsite ad you can't remember agreeing to. There is the postage you absorbed because "free shipping" converts better. There is the cotton cord you buy in bulk, the tissue paper, the stickers, the cardboard, and the stall fee at the market.

Each of those is small. You've never seen them all in one place, side by side with the price on the tag.

What you do instead of looking

You look at the payout. A number lands in your bank account, and it feels like money. You look at the stall tin on Saturday night, count the notes, and feel rich for about four minutes.

Then you put the tin in the cupboard and don't think about what the cord cost, or the Tuesday you spent crocheting eleven hangers for a bulk order.

You've heard other makers say, "Just price it properly." It sounds simple. But to price it properly you would have to know your costs, and to know your costs you'd have to open a drawer you've been quietly avoiding.

Because what if the bestseller, the one everyone loves, barely pays you anything? What if you've been working for free and calling it a small business?

That is the worry nobody says out loud at book club.

The walk home

You carry the empty shortbread tin to the car, because Hanne pressed it into your hands. The street is quiet. A bin lorry rattles somewhere behind the houses.

"Are you actually making money?"

You realise the question didn't bother you because it was rude. It bothered you because you couldn't answer it. And you notice that it is not a big, frightening question. It is a small one, with an answer sitting in your own receipts, your own order history, your own hands.

It doesn't have to be a verdict on you. It is only a number you haven't looked at yet.

There is a strange relief in that. You don't have to be good at finance. You just have to see it once, laid out plainly, product by product. After that you can decide what to do about it. Maybe nothing. Maybe a small price change. Maybe the tote that nobody notices turns out to be the one that actually pays.

A different Monday

Picture it a few weeks on. Monday morning, a mug of tea, the laptop open at the kitchen table.

You don't dread the sheet. For each thing you make, you can see what it costs, what the fees take, what is left, and roughly what you earn for an hour of crochet. Nothing is hidden in a drawer.

The plant hanger you always felt a bit guilty about charging for? It's fine. Better than fine. The tote you've been shipping for free as a favour needs a slightly different price, and now you know exactly how much.

On Saturday, the market tin and the online orders are not two separate worlds. They're one picture. You close the laptop by ten and go outside, because the thing you were avoiding took twenty minutes, not a lifetime.

And the next time somebody asks how the shop is going, you smile and say, "Actually, I know exactly how it's going."
