Post-Editing Pay Too Low? The Parents' Evening Corridor

2026-10-30

It is 18:20 on a Tuesday, and you are sitting on a chair built for a nine-year-old.

Parents' evening. The corridor smells of floor polish and wet coats. Three families are ahead of you, and the teacher's door stays shut. You pull out your phone, mostly so you don't have to make small talk.

There is an email from the agency you have worked with for four years. The subject line says: Light post-editing project, fast turnaround.

You open it. The machine translation is already done, they write. You only need to "review and polish." The rate is a little over half your usual per-word price. A friendly line at the end says they know you will be able to work through it quickly.

You read the line twice.

The word "light"

You know that word. You have met it before.

Last spring you took a job like this. The sample page looked fine. Then you opened the full file and found the same small errors in every paragraph. A product name translated three different ways. A joke turned into a warning. Sentences that were grammatical and still wrong.

Fixing them is not light. You cannot skim a text that sounds confident and is quietly mistaken. Every line needs your whole attention. Sometimes it is slower than starting from a blank page, because you first have to un-believe what is on the screen.

You finished that job on a Sunday. You remember the invoice arriving at a number that looked fine, until you tried to divide it by your hours. You closed the spreadsheet you had half-opened and went to put the kettle on instead.

You never did the sum properly. Part of you did not want to know.

The sum you avoid

That is the quiet part of this work, the thing translators rarely say out loud. The rate on the email feels like a decision about the word. But you do not live on words. You live on hours: the ones at the screen, the ones spent reading the brief, the unpaid ones before and after.

So a low rate does not just feel low. It feels vaguely insulting, and you cannot say exactly why. Is it the percentage? Is it the "quickly"? Is it that you might be wrong, and say no to something that was fine, and then have a slow month?

That last fear is the one that wins. It usually does. You say yes, a bit tight in the chest, and tell yourself you will be fast this time.

The classroom door opens. A father comes out looking relieved. It is not your turn yet.

Four minutes in a corridor

You do something small. You open the notes app and type three lines.

The word count from the email. How long you think this really takes, if you are honest and not hopeful. The amount they are offering.

You do not use a clever formula. You just divide. The number that comes out is lower than what you would accept from any client who spoke to you face to face. It is also lower than the number you give yourself when someone asks what you earn and you want to sound fine.

Nothing dramatic happens. No alarm. You sit there with a number on a small screen and a very ordinary feeling: oh. So that is what it is.

The strange part is the relief. The knot does not loosen because the number is good. It loosens because it is known. A fog you have been walking through for years suddenly has edges.

And a second thought arrives, quieter. If you can do this sum in a corridor in four minutes, why have you been doing it only when something already hurts?

What else is hiding in the same fog? The agency that always says thirty days and means sixty. The translation-memory discount that was applied "automatically" and that you accepted without checking. The rush job that cost you a weekend and paid like a Wednesday.

A different kind of Monday

Picture a Monday a few weeks from now.

You sit down with coffee. A new offer is in your inbox, again from a machine-translation workflow, again with the friendly word "light." You open a simple sheet. You enter the words, your honest time guess, the offered rate. The real hourly figure appears on its own.

This time you are not guessing. You are looking at it, the way you would look at a price tag.

Maybe you reply and propose a different rate, in a calm voice, with a reason. Maybe you accept, because the text is clean and the number works. Maybe you say no, and the no costs you nothing but a pause, because you know your month is not hanging on this one email.

The point is that it is your decision again, with the facts on the table. Not a reflex. Not a flinch.

Later, in the same sheet, you can see which agencies pay when they said they would, and which ones you quietly chase every time. It is not a lecture. It is just a picture, and it makes the next quote easier to write.

You close the laptop at six.

Back in the corridor

The door opens again. "Come in, please," the teacher says, smiling, and you stand up with your phone still warm in your hand.

You have not replied to the agency. You will do that tomorrow, with a clearer head. For tonight you have your number, and the feeling that you are, after all, running a real business, one that is allowed to look at its own figures.
