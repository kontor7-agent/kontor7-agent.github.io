Keep Running Out of Stock? The Post Office Queue Reorder Moment

2026-10-13

You are third from the front in the post office queue, six parcels stacked in your arms, when your phone buzzes.

It's an order notification. Then a second one. You smile, because a Wednesday lunchtime with two orders is a good Wednesday.

Then the thought arrives, quietly and without permission: Do I actually still have that?

You try to picture the shelf in the spare room. The row of jars, the box on the left, the gap you noticed on Sunday and told yourself you'd look into. You can see the gap. You can't see the number.

The queue shuffles forward. You do the sum in your head, and it doesn't come out.

The sentence you've typed too many times

Back home, you open the shelf and count. Two jars short of what you just sold.

So you write the email you have written before. Hi, so sorry, there's been a delay with your order. The item is temporarily out of stock and I'm reordering as we speak.

You read it back. "As we speak" is generous. You haven't even found the supplier's number yet.

Nobody is angry. That's almost the worst part. One customer replies with a friendly shrug. Another quietly asks for a refund. A third says nothing at all, and you wonder if she'll ever come back.

The shop looks fine from the outside. Inside, you feel a little like someone running to keep up with their own business.

What it costs when no one is shouting

It's not a disaster. It's a hundred small leaks.

The bestseller that sat empty for nine days last month, right when a blogger mentioned you. The slow-moving boxes you ordered "just in case" that still sit in the corner, taking up space and cash you could have used elsewhere. The rush shipping fee you paid last spring because you noticed too late.

And there's the Sunday-evening feeling. Not panic, more a low hum. A mental list of things that might be running low, which you carry around like a stone in your coat pocket. At the supermarket you find yourself thinking about packaging tape. In bed you wonder about labels.

You don't tell anyone, because it sounds small. I just keep running out of things. It doesn't sound like a real problem. It sounds like a personal flaw, like you should simply be more organised.

But you are organised. You pack every order carefully. You answer every message. What you lack is a way to see.

The call that changed the question

A few days later, you phone your supplier about the missing jars.

"Three weeks, probably," she says. "Maybe a bit less if the lids arrive on time."

Three weeks. You stand in the hallway holding the phone, and the real question lands, one you've never asked properly.

It was never how do I stop running out? It was how early do I need to know?

If the wait is three weeks and you sell a certain amount in a week, then the moment to reorder isn't when the shelf looks thin. It was already a while ago. You were always going to be late, because you were watching the shelf instead of the calendar.

It's a strangely gentle realisation. It means you weren't careless. You were just looking at the wrong thing.

And it means this can be fixed, quietly, without becoming a different person.

A Monday where the shelf doesn't surprise you

Picture a Monday a few weeks from now.

You make coffee. You open one file, the same one as always, and your eyes go to a short list. Two items are marked as "time to order." Not urgent, not alarming. Just a calm nudge, weeks before it would have hurt.

You send two emails to your supplier. It takes six minutes. You close the laptop, and the stone in your pocket is gone.

The spare room is the same room, with the same shelves. But now you know what's on them, roughly what they're worth, and which boxes have been sitting still for too long. When a bigger order comes in, you say yes without the tiny flinch.

On Wednesday lunchtime you're back in the post office queue. Your phone buzzes. You smile, and you let it buzz.

That's the whole difference. Not more work, but less carrying around in your head.
