Sick as a Freelancer, No Income? The Thermometer Reading

2026-11-08

It is 8:10 on a Thursday and the thermometer says 38.9.

Jonas stands in the kitchen in yesterday's T-shirt, holding it up to the window as if the light might change the number. It doesn't. His head feels packed with wet wool. The kettle is on, but he isn't sure why.

The first thought is not I should rest. It is the rent goes out on the 1st.

The second thought is the landing page for the bakery client, due Monday. The third is a number he can't quite name: how much money is actually coming in this month, and from whom.

There is no sick pay for this. There is no HR person to email. There is only a laptop on the counter, glowing politely.

The Arithmetic of Being Ill

If you work for yourself, you know this particular sum. It runs quietly under the fever.

Every day in bed is a day you didn't bill. You can feel it, a little like a tap left running somewhere in the flat. You lie on the sofa with a blanket up to your chin and the sofa doesn't feel like rest. It feels like a decision you are paying for.

So Jonas does what many of us do. He opens the laptop "just to check."

There is an email thread from a client with the subject line Re: Re: Invoice. There is a bank app that he opens, closes, and opens again, as if the balance might have grown while he wasn't looking. There is a folder with the word Invoices on it, and inside, files with names like final_v2 and final_ACTUAL.

He tries to remember: did the March project get paid? Is the agency invoice from last month still open, or did that land? He scrolls. He squints. His eyes water from the fever and from the screen.

He can't tell. That is the worst part. It isn't that the answer is bad. It is that he can't find the answer at all.

The Cost Nobody Counts

Here is what sick days cost a freelancer, beyond the missing hours.

They cost the rest you can't take, because your head is doing sums. They cost the sleep that doesn't come properly because the knot in your stomach has opinions about the 1st. They cost a slightly shaky message to a client that begins So sorry, I'm a bit under the weather, but I'll still try to... when what you actually need to say is I need two days.

And they cost the feeling underneath: that you are not a real business. A real business would have a cushion, a plan, a clear picture. You have a sofa and a balance you are afraid to look at.

That feeling is not true. But it is loud when your temperature is 38.9.

The Phone Call With the Pharmacist

Around noon Jonas gets dressed and walks to the pharmacy because his sister has told him to, twice. The pharmacist is brisk and kind and says the plain thing: fluids, sleep, no screens if you can manage it.

On the way back he stops at the zebra crossing and notices something small. He has been so busy fearing the unknown that he has never actually looked at it. The dread isn't made of a number. It's made of not knowing the number.

A known number, even a small one, can be planned around. You can say to yourself: I have this much coming, I have this much going out, and two days off fits in there. Or: it doesn't fit, and here is the one client I should write to. Either way, it's a plan, and a plan is a lot lighter to carry than a fog.

He doesn't solve anything that day. He goes home and sleeps.

A Different Kind of Sick Day

Imagine the same Thursday, with one thing changed.

The thermometer still says 38.9. The head is still wool. But before you lie down, you open one page and glance at it, the way you glance at a weather forecast. Who owes you, and since when. What is arriving this month. How many weeks your usual costs are covered. It takes under a minute, and nothing on the page is a surprise.

You write one calm line to the bakery client: I'm ill and need until Wednesday. The page will be with you then. You press send without rewriting it nine times.

Then you put the laptop in another room. The sofa becomes, for the first time in two days, just a sofa.

That is all. It isn't a miracle. It is the difference between being ill and being ill and afraid.
