Craft Stock and Orders in a Mess? The Courier Slot

2026-10-15

It is 7:50 on a Monday morning and the courier is booked for nine.

You are standing in the spare room in your socks, a mug going cold on the windowsill. Hand-dyed skeins hang from the airer like a slow rainbow. On the desk: a notebook with Etsy orders, a second notebook with market sales, and a yellow sticky note that says order more base yarn?? in your own handwriting.

One customer wrote on Friday. She asked whether her order would arrive before her sister's birthday. You said yes. You said it quickly, because you wanted to be the kind of seller who says yes.

Now you cannot find her order.

The three notebooks

Let's call you Ines. You are an illustration, not a real person, but you may know her well.

Ines started dyeing yarn at the kitchen table two years ago. It was a hobby that began to pay for itself, and then, quietly, a bit more than that. Now there are around forty colourways, two marketplaces, one market stall every second Saturday, and a system that grew the way weeds grow: wherever there was room.

Orders live in one notebook. Stock lives in her head, mostly, and on a shelf she counts when she is worried. Costs live in a folder of receipts and a vague feeling that the merino got more expensive in spring.

She finds the birthday order on page nine of the wrong notebook. The ship-by date is Wednesday. Fine. Just fine. But her stomach has already done the sprint, and she is not even properly awake.

The question under the question

The courier arrives at 9:04 and takes four parcels. Ines waves, closes the door, and stands in the hallway with that small hollow feeling.

Because here is what she doesn't say out loud, not even to her partner, who asks every Sunday how the shop is going.

She does not know which colourway actually earns money. The bestseller, a deep teal she calls Harbour, sells so well that she assumes it must be her best product. But the dye is pricey, the skeins take twice the time, and the last time she tried to work out the margin she gave up halfway through and made tea.

She does not know how many skeins of base yarn are left until she goes and counts them. Twice this year she has run out in the middle of a busy week and told customers restocking soon, which is a polite way of saying I didn't see it coming.

And she does not know, with any certainty, whether the shop is making her money or just keeping her busy. That is the one that wakes her at 4 am.

Being busy feels like a business. It isn't always one.

The Thursday that changed the shape of it

Nothing dramatic happens. No disaster, no one-star review.

On Thursday she sits down with a friend who sells ceramics, both of them on the bench outside a café, both holding something warm. The friend mentions, almost in passing, that she stopped keeping her shop in her head.

"I just look at one page in the morning," she says. "It tells me what's running low, what's due out, and which things are actually worth making."

Ines laughs a little, the way you laugh at something that sounds too good. But on the walk home the sentence stays with her. One page in the morning.

It hadn't occurred to her that the knot in her stomach was information she could have been given instead of feeling. That it didn't have to be carried. That a shop could tell you things.

She wasn't missing talent. She wasn't missing hard work. She was missing a single place where stock, costs and orders could sit side by side and be honest with each other.

A different Monday

Picture the same room, a few weeks later, same airer, same cold-ish light.

It is 7:50 on a Monday. Ines has her coffee, still hot this time. She opens one screen and reads it the way you read a weather forecast.

Two orders are due out this week, and she can see which ones. The base yarn is getting low, and she knows it with days to spare instead of on the day she runs out. Harbour, it turns out, is lovely and not as profitable as she thought. A quieter colourway she almost stopped making is quietly carrying more than its share.

None of this is frightening. It's just clear, and clear is a different feeling from busy.

She packs the parcels without the sprint in her chest. When the courier arrives at nine she is already dressed, already smiling, already thinking about what to dye next, instead of what she might have forgotten.

The afternoon is hers. She dyes a batch because she wants to, not because she is behind. At six she closes the laptop and the spare room becomes just a room again.

That is the quiet gift of seeing your own shop. It doesn't make you work less. It lets you stop bracing.
