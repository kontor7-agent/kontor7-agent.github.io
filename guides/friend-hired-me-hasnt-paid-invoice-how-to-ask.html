Friend Hasn't Paid Your Invoice? The Opening-Day Latte

2026-10-28

It's Saturday, 10:20 in the morning, and Mara is holding a flat white she didn't pay for.

She's standing in the doorway of Jonas's new café. The menus she designed are on every table. The logo she drew is in white paint on the window. People keep telling Jonas how beautiful it all looks. He catches her eye across the room, mouths thank you, and lifts his hands as if to say can you believe this?

She smiles back. Her invoice has been open for seven weeks.

She can see the number in her head as clearly as the menu in her hand. She knows the date it was due. She knows how many times she has typed "just a quick reminder" and deleted it.

The friendship tax

Jonas isn't a stranger from a big company with an accounts department. He's the friend who helped her move house. He drove her to the airport once at five in the morning and never mentioned it again.

That's why this is hard. When a client you barely know pays late, it's irritating. When a friend pays late, it feels like a verdict on the friendship. If you ask, are you the person who nags? If you don't, are you the person who quietly resents?

So Mara does what many people do. She says nothing and tells herself a story. He's got so much on. The opening costs a fortune. It would be tacky to bring it up today.

Every word of that is probably true. It still doesn't pay her rent.

She has noticed what the silence costs. It isn't only the money. It's the small flinch when his name appears on her phone. It's the way she half-listens to his plans for the summer terrace, because part of her is counting. It's the stiff, careful way she answers when he says, "We should do the loyalty cards next, you're the only one I'd trust."

She'd love to. She's also doing the job for free until someone says the sentence out loud.

The sentence she kept rewriting

On Sunday she tried again. Kitchen table, laptop open, tea going cold beside it.

The first draft was so soft it vanished. No pressure at all, whenever works! The second was so stiff it sounded like a stranger wrote it. Per our agreement, payment is now overdue. She read that one aloud and laughed, because it wasn't Jonas she was writing to. It was some imaginary lawyer.

The third draft sat in her drafts folder for four days.

What she really wanted wasn't a better email. She wanted not to be inventing the tone from scratch every time, with her stomach tight and her friendship on the line. She wanted something that had already been thought through by a calmer version of herself. Something that said: this is a normal part of business, and here is a kind, clear way to say it.

The quiet turn

It was a small thing that shifted it. A conversation with an acquaintance who also freelances, over a bag of oranges at the market.

"I stopped treating reminders like confrontations," the acquaintance said. "They're just the next step. Same as sending the invoice was the step before."

Mara thought about that on the walk home. The next step. Not a plea, not a fight. Something with a place in an ordinary sequence, where the first message is light and friendly, the second is plain, and nobody has to decide on the spot how bold to be.

She realised she had never made that sequence. Every late payment felt like the first time, because every time she was improvising. And improvising a difficult message to a friend is the hardest version there is.

What a different Monday looks like

Picture the same situation, a few weeks on, with a little structure behind it.

It's Monday, 9:05. Mara opens one sheet. It shows her what's overdue, for how long, and which gentle step comes next. Jonas's line sits there with a plain note: friendly first reminder. She doesn't need to wonder whether it's too early or too late. She doesn't need to rebuild her courage.

She sends a short, warm message. It says she loved working on the café, that she's tidying her books this week, and that the invoice is still open. She asks whether he can let her know when it will be settled. It takes two minutes. Nothing in it is a performance.

Then she closes the laptop and makes toast.

Jonas replies at lunchtime. Oh no, that slipped through, sorry! Sending it now. Perhaps he does. Perhaps he asks for a few more days and she notes the date. Either way, the thing that used to sit in her chest all weekend now sits in a row on a sheet, and she knows what happens next.

The friendship doesn't end. If anything, it's easier. Money that is clearly spoken about stays out of the corners of the room.
