Customer Ordered Something Sold Out? The Theatre Interval

2026-10-24

The bell rings for the interval, and everyone stands up at once.

Mira shuffles along the row with her coat over her arm. The bar queue is already ten people deep. She has just reached the front of the row when her phone buzzes in her bag. She tells herself not to look. She looks.

New order: 6 × Smoked Chilli Sauce. Gift note: "For my brother's 40th, needs to arrive by Friday!"

Her stomach drops before her brain catches up.

She makes hot sauce in small batches in a rented kitchen unit and sells it online. Smoked Chilli is the one people reorder. She tries to picture the shelf. Two jars? None? She remembers a weekend market, a good one, and a feeling of great, that went well. She doesn't remember counting afterwards.

The queue that doesn't move

The bar queue shuffles forward. Someone behind her laughs at something. The lights in the foyer are warm and golden, and she is standing in the middle of them with a knot in her chest.

She could open the spreadsheet on her phone. She knows exactly what it will look like: three tabs, one of them called "Stock NEW", and a column she stopped updating sometime in September. It might say 14. It might say 0. It has never once felt like something she could trust.

The worst part isn't the order. It's the email she will have to write. I'm so sorry, it turns out we're out of stock. She has written that sentence before. Every time she writes it, a small voice says: a real business would have known.

She thinks about the customer, too. Someone is planning a birthday, picking a gift, typing a note with an exclamation mark. That person trusted a product page that said "in stock" in cheerful green letters.

What it quietly costs

Mira gets her drink. She stands by a pillar and checks. The sauce is gone. She sold the last jar at the market and never took it off the shop.

It isn't a disaster. Still, it costs more than one order.

It costs the second half of the play, which she watches without taking in a single line. She is rewriting the apology in her head instead. It costs the refund, and the small embarrassment of the apology. It costs the next hour, when she wonders what else the shop is showing as available that isn't. A batch of shallots and peppers takes an afternoon to prep, but nobody told her to start one, because nobody was watching the number going down.

And it costs something harder to name. The feeling that you are always one step behind your own shop. That you built something people want, and you can't quite keep up with it.

On the way home she doesn't say much. Her friend asks if everything is okay. "Just a stock thing," she says, and it sounds so small.

The turning point, on a very ordinary Saturday

The repair comes later, and it isn't dramatic.

On Saturday morning she sits at the kitchen table with tea and, for once, doesn't open the old spreadsheet. She asks herself a different question. What if I didn't have to remember?

Not how do I get better at remembering. She has tried that. She has tried sticky notes on the unit door and a reminder on her phone titled "COUNT!!". What if the list simply knew what was running low, and said so, before a customer did?

It turns out to be a small idea. One place where every product lives. What you have, what you ordered, what you'd like to have left before it gets tight. A quiet flag next to anything that needs reordering. Nothing clever. Just the shelf, written down, in a way that talks back.

She enters her eight products. She counts the jars on the real shelf, one by one, with the odd satisfaction of holding the true number in her hand. It takes less than an hour. Smoked Chilli: zero, as she suspected. Two others are lower than she'd have guessed.

A Monday that feels different

Three weeks later it's Monday morning, grey and drizzly, and Mira is standing in the supermarket queue with a basket of lemons.

Her phone buzzes. A new order.

She looks at it. And nothing in her chest tightens.

She already knows what's on the shelf, because on Sunday evening she spent ten minutes updating the numbers over a cup of tea. She knows the Smoked Chilli batch is due on Thursday, because the list told her a week ago it was getting low. The jars she needs are already in the post. The order on her screen is for something she has.

She pays for the lemons and walks out into the drizzle, almost smiling.

Nothing about her business has become bigger or flashier. The change is smaller and better than that. The shelf has stopped surprising her. When she opens the laptop, she isn't braced for something. She just looks, sees where things stand, and gets on with her day.

The next time she sits in a theatre, the phone stays in her bag. Not out of discipline. She just isn't afraid of what it might say.
