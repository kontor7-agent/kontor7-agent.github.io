Double Booked Two Photo Clients? The Chalkboard Menu

2026-11-02

Wednesday, 9:15, a flat white and a notification

You are standing at the café counter, reading the chalkboard menu without taking in a single word. Your phone is in your hand. On the screen is a calendar reminder for Saturday: Reyes wedding, getting-ready shots, 11:00.

That is fine. You remember the Reyes wedding.

Then you swipe down. Another entry sits on the same Saturday, same hour, with a different name. Lindqvist family portraits, 11:00, park by the lake.

You read it twice. You check the date, as if the date might have changed since the last glance.

The barista says your name. You don't hear it the first time.

How it happened

Let's call you Mara. Mara is an illustrative photographer, not a real person, but you may know her well.

The Reyes booking came in by email, six weeks ago, while she was in the middle of editing. She said yes, wrote the date on a sticky note, and meant to add it to the calendar after lunch.

The Lindqvist family wrote on Instagram. She replied from her phone on a bus. The date went into the calendar. The sticky note went somewhere under a lens cloth.

Nobody was careless in the way people imagine. Mara was just working alone, with her business spread across an inbox, a direct-message thread, a notes app and a few pieces of paper. No single place held the whole picture.

She carries the coffee to a table by the window and sits down very straight.

The quiet maths of the next ten minutes

The thoughts come in a rush, and none of them are kind.

Which client do you cancel? One of them has already paid a deposit. Did the other? You think so. You can't remember. Which email was it in?

Could you do both? The wedding prep is at 11:00. The park is forty minutes away. You can already hear how that sentence would sound out loud, and you know it wouldn't work.

Then the bigger thought arrives, the one you rarely say out loud: a real professional would never have done this.

That is the part that costs the most. The mistake is fixable. The feeling of being someone who is winging it takes longer to shake off.

Her coffee goes cold while she scrolls through three apps to find out who owes her what.

The phone calls

She makes both calls from the car park, because it feels wrong to do it inside.

The first one goes better than she feared. The family is relaxed, a little disappointed, and happy to move to the following weekend. The second call is shorter, and the apology she gives is honest.

Nobody shouts. Nobody asks for a discount. But Mara notices something on the drive home: she is more tired than the problem deserved. Most of the tiredness wasn't about Saturday. It was about the week she'd just spent not knowing: not knowing who had paid a deposit, not knowing which galleries were still due, not knowing whether anything else was hiding in a corner of her inbox.

It turns out the double booking was only the loudest thing in a quiet pile.

The moment it doesn't have to be like this

That evening she doesn't open her editing software. She makes tea, opens a blank sheet, and writes one line per client: name, shoot date, deposit paid or not, gallery due, invoice sent or not.

It takes forty minutes. It is the first time she has seen her whole business on one screen.

Two things happen. She finds one more date that sits very close to another, close enough to deserve a second look. And she feels her shoulders drop, a little, for the first time since Wednesday morning.

The answer was never to be more careful in her head. Her head had been doing the work of a system, and it had been doing it alone.

A normal Monday, afterwards

Picture the same photographer a few weeks later.

It is Monday, 8:40. She opens one file with her coffee. The dashboard shows what is coming this week, which galleries are due, and which deposits have arrived. A new enquiry comes in from a couple wanting the second Saturday in June. She looks at the screen before she types a word. That Saturday is free. She writes back, warm and sure, and the booking goes into the sheet before she has finished her coffee.

No scrolling through messages. No sticky notes under the lens cloth. No quiet dread when the phone buzzes.

She still works alone. She just doesn't carry everything alone anymore.

By ten she is out of the house with her camera bag, thinking about light rather than logistics. That is the part she'd forgotten she liked.
