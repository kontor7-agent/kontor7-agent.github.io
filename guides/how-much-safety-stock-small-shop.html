How Much Safety Stock Is Enough? The Recital Row

2026-10-31

It's 4:10 on a Saturday afternoon, and you are in the third row of a village hall. The folding chair creaks. Your niece is about to play her first piece on the piano, and you are thinking about wax.

Not the recital. Wax. Specifically, whether the 40 kilos in the back room will last until the supplier delivers.

You sell small-batch candles from a spare room and a market stall. Last month you ran out of the cedar one on a Friday. This month you ordered extra "just in case" and now a shelf of lavender sits there like a quiet reproach.

So you do the maths on the back of the programme. Two weeks of buffer? Three? Is that too much? Is that too little?

You don't know. You never really have. You just pick a number that feels safe and hope.

The number that is really a feeling

People ask the question in search bars all the time: how much safety stock should I keep? It sounds like it needs a clever answer.

But for most small shops, the honest truth is that the number is a mood. After a stock-out, you order too much. After an overstock, you order too little. Your buffer swings with your last bad week, not with how your shop actually sells.

It isn't carelessness. You're running the whole shop alone, and nobody ever showed you a calmer way. You make the candles, answer the messages, pack the parcels and do the photos. The stock level lives in your head, between the school-run reminder and the invoice you must send.

The piano starts. A wobbly first bar, then a steadier second one. You smile at your niece and, underneath the smile, you are still counting jars.

What the guessing quietly costs

Nobody sees this cost on a receipt, but it is there.

There is the customer who wanted six of the cedar for a wedding and got an apologetic message instead. There is the cash sitting in boxes of lavender that moves slowly, money you could have used for something that sells.

There is the small flinch whenever a big order comes in. Do I have enough? Let me check. And then the walk to the back room, mid-sentence, to count with your finger on each box.

And there is the thing you rarely say aloud: that you don't feel like a real shop. Real shops know what they have. You just feel your way through.

It also follows you to places it shouldn't, like a Saturday recital, a Sunday walk, a week of holiday that isn't quite a holiday because you keep checking your phone for orders.

The moment it shifts

Afterwards there are sandwiches in the hall and warm squash in paper cups. Your sister hugs the pianist, who is now very proud and a little pink.

Your sister's friend runs a bakery stall, and while you queue she says something in passing. She doesn't guess her flour any more. She looks at how fast it goes, how long the delivery takes, and she lets that set the cushion. Nothing grand, she says. Just numbers she can see instead of numbers she feels.

You nod, and say nothing.

But on the drive home, one thought sits next to you on the passenger seat. What if the buffer wasn't a mood at all? What if it were simply something you could look at?

Not a bigger brain. Not a fancier system. Just a quiet place where each product knows how fast it sells, how long it takes to come back, and tells you when it's time to order before the shelf starts to look thin.

The worry doesn't vanish. But it loosens by a notch, the way a tight shoulder does when someone finally says, "you can put that down."

An ordinary Monday, afterwards

Picture it a few weeks on.

Monday, 8:30. The kettle is on. You open one sheet, and it is calm. A few items are marked as running low. The rest are fine, and you can see why.

The cedar candle is on the list, because it is selling faster than last month. You place the order with your supplier before breakfast is cold. No back-room count, no finger on the boxes.

The lavender shelf is shrinking. You know it's no longer the safe choice, so you stop reordering it by habit.

When a big wedding enquiry arrives, you answer in two minutes. Yes, you can do six. Yes, you can do twelve. You know, because you can see it.

At six you close the laptop. On the next recital, if there is one, you watch the pianist and only the pianist.
