Admin Drowning Your Small Business? The Van Lunch in the Rain

2026-10-13

It is 12:40 on a Thursday and the rain is drumming on the roof of the van.

You have twenty minutes before the next job. The sandwich is on your knee. The phone is propped against the steering wheel, and a client has just written: "Hi, could you send the quote you mentioned? And did we ever get the invoice for the hedge work?"

You read it twice. The quote is somewhere. You said the number out loud on the doorstep, and you think you wrote it down. The invoice is either sent, drafted, or still in your head.

So you do what you always do. You open the laptop on your knees and start looking.

One question, five places to look

There is the spreadsheet called Jobs 2026. There is Jobs 2026 NEW, which you made in March when the first one got messy. There is a notes app with half-sentences like "Meier, +2h?" There is a folder of receipt photos with names like IMG_4471. There is also the inbox, where the real answers hide.

You are not lazy. You are good at your actual work. People recommend you, and that is why the phone keeps buzzing.

But the work behind the work has grown quietly, one small file at a time. Each spreadsheet made sense on the day you created it. Together they are a house with no hallway.

The sandwich goes cold. Eleven minutes later you find the invoice. It was drafted, never sent. It has been sitting there for nine days.

What it costs when nobody sees it

The money part is obvious. Late invoices mean late payments, and late payments mean that tight feeling when the supplier bill lands.

The other costs are quieter.

There is the small flinch every time a client asks a simple question. You can't answer with confidence, so you say, "Let me check and come back to you." You say it so often that you start to hear how it sounds.

There is Sunday evening, when you are not working but not resting either. You are doing a slow mental inventory: who owes me, what did I promise, what have I forgotten?

There is the thought you rarely say out loud: a real business would have this sorted by now.

But that thought is wrong. A real business is simply one that someone is running. You are running it. You just haven't given it a single place to live.

The sentence that changed the question

A few weeks later, you are talking to a friend who runs a small print studio. You are not complaining, just chatting, and you mention the thing with the van and the nine-day-old invoice.

She laughs, not unkindly. "I used to have my whole business in four places and my head," she says. "The head was the worst one."

Then she says something that stays with you. "I didn't need better software. I needed one place where the answers sit."

On the drive home you turn it over. You had been asking, How do I get better at admin? Maybe the question was smaller and kinder: Where should each thing live, so I don't have to remember it?

Small things. Who owes you. What you quoted. What a job really cost. Which receipts belong to which month. None of that needs to be clever. It only needs to be in one calm, ordinary spot that you can open without bracing yourself.

A different Thursday

Imagine it is 12:40 again. Same van, same rain, same cold-ish sandwich.

The client's message comes in. This time you take one bite, open one file, and look at one page. The quote is there. The invoice is marked sent on a date you can see. You reply in under a minute, in a warm, steady voice: "Yes, sent on the 4th, here it is again. No rush."

Then you finish the sandwich. You watch the rain for a bit, because you can.

The afternoon feels lighter, and not because the work got smaller. It is the same hedges and the same ladders. What changed is that the part of your brain that used to run a background search for loose ends can finally rest.

On Friday evening you spend about ten minutes looking over the week. Open invoices, what's coming in, what's due. You close the laptop. Nothing is circling at 23:00.

That is all it is. Not a transformation, just a quieter head and a business that feels like yours again.
