Can't Keep Track of Job Applications? The Parkrun Question

2026-10-16

It's 8:40 on a Saturday morning. You're standing in a damp park car park in borrowed running shoes, holding a paper cup of tea you don't really want.

A woman from your neighbour's running group smiles at you. "Oh, Dana mentioned you're looking for something new. How's it going? Anyone got back to you?"

It's a kind question. You open your mouth and nothing useful comes out.

You have applied. Loads. Somewhere around thirty, or maybe forty. A product designer role at a company with a green logo. Something in logistics that sounded better than it looked. A startup you found at 23:50 on a Tuesday and applied to before you could talk yourself out of it.

But "anyone got back to you?" Honestly? You don't know. And that is the strange part.

The answer you didn't have

"A couple of bits in the pipeline," you say. "Still early."

It isn't a lie. It just isn't an answer either. You couldn't say which company, which stage, or whether you owe somebody a reply. The whole thing lives in a fog made of browser tabs, a half-sorted inbox and one note on your phone that stopped making sense in September.

The race starts. You run. Somewhere around the second kilometre your head does what it always does now: it replays the inbox.

There was an email from a recruiter on Wednesday. Was that the green logo company or the logistics one? There was a missed call last week. You never wrote down who. And somewhere there was a message saying we'd love to schedule a conversation, and you can't remember if you answered it.

You finish the run. You don't enjoy the finish.

What it quietly costs

Nobody warns you that a job search is mostly a filing job.

The applications themselves are the visible part. The invisible part is remembering. Who you wrote to, which version of your CV went where, who said "we'll be in touch by Friday," and which Friday that was.

So you carry it all in your head. Every unanswered application becomes a small, low hum of unease. Every unknown number makes your stomach tighten. Every free evening turns into an hour of scrolling your own sent folder, trying to reconstruct your own life.

It also makes you feel a bit unprofessional, which is unfair. You are careful at work. You are the person who remembers the details for everyone else. But here you are, unable to say whether you replied to someone who wanted to talk to you.

And the cost isn't only the muddle. It's the follow-up you didn't send because you weren't sure it was time. It's the interview invitation that sat for four days because it hid between two newsletters. It's showing up to a call and thinking, wait, which role was this again?

The walk back to the car

After the run, the woman from the group is stretching by the fence. She laughs and says she once kept her whole house-hunting process on a single sheet of paper stuck to the fridge. "Boring," she says. "But I never once wondered where I was."

You drive home with that sentence rattling around.

Never once wondered where I was.

You realise you haven't been missing effort. You've been missing a place. One quiet place where every application has a line, every reply has a date, and the next small step is written down in plain words. A place that remembers, so your head doesn't have to.

It doesn't need to be clever. It only needs to be there, and to be yours.

A different Monday

Picture it a few weeks later. Monday, 9:05. Coffee, a window, the laptop open.

You don't scroll through your inbox looking for clues. You open one page. It shows what's waiting on a reply, what needs a nudge today, and what's coming up this week. Thursday, an interview at 14:00. Beside it, the three stories you want to tell, ready to go. Nothing is hidden.

Two applications are marked as a week old. A follow-up is due, so you write it, kindly and briefly, and send it. It takes four minutes. The email asking for availability gets answered at once, because you saw it at once.

Then you close the laptop and go for a walk. The hum is gone. Not the uncertainty, because a job search always has some of that. But the mess is gone, and the mess was the louder part.

The next time somebody asks, "Anyone got back to you?", you can say, "Yes, two replied. One interview Thursday, one waiting on me for a call time. The rest I'll check in with next week." It's a calm, specific answer. It's also true, and you can say it without flinching.
