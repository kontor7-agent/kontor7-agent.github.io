Everything in Your Head, Can't Delegate? The Pool Lounger

2026-10-28

Day two of the week off. The sun is on your knees, the pool is a very clean blue, and your phone is face-down on the towel like something you're not allowed to look at.

You look at it anyway.

It's 11:20. Lukas, your part-timer, has sent a message: "Quick question, a customer wants to know if the lavender ones can ship with the gift wrap, and what do we do when the courier label fails?"

You know the answer. You've always known the answer. That's exactly the problem.

You type it out with your thumbs, sunscreen smearing the screen. Three messages. Then a fourth, because you remembered the thing about the wrap paper. Then you put the phone down and stare at the water.

This was supposed to be a holiday.

The business that lives in one skull

Let's call you Marta. You make candles and small-batch soaps. You started at your kitchen table, and now there's a shelf of stock, a handful of regular wholesale customers, and one person helping three mornings a week.

You wanted help. You hired help. And still, nothing moves unless you are in the room, or at least in the chat.

Because the rules were never written down. They live in you:

  • Which customers get a free sample, and which ones just ask.
  • How you word the reply when a parcel is late.
  • What you say to the wax supplier when a delivery is short.
  • The order you do things on a Thursday, so Friday isn't a disaster.

None of it is complicated. All of it is invisible. When Lukas asks, you don't hand him a page. You give him a half-remembered speech, and he writes it on a sticky note that gets lost by Wednesday.

What it quietly costs

Nobody tells you this part when you start working for yourself. Being the only one who knows things feels like being important. For a while, it is.

Then it turns into a leash.

You check your phone at the dinner table. You answer "quick questions" at 7:40 on a Sunday. You say "it's easier if I just do it" so often that you've stopped hearing it. And somewhere underneath, there's a small, tired thought: if I got ill for two weeks, this would stop.

That thought doesn't sound dramatic. It sounds like a grey Tuesday. But it sits there.

There's a second cost, quieter still. Lukas is capable. He'd like to be trusted with more. Every time you answer for him, you take something small from him, and from yourself.

The sentence that stuck

On the third evening, you're on the hotel balcony with a glass of something cold. You call your sister, mostly to complain.

You tell her about the lavender message, the courier labels, the sticky notes.

She listens, then says something simple. "You don't need to explain it better. You need to get it out of your head once, so you don't have to carry it every time."

You laugh, because it sounds too easy. But it lands.

Because the truth is, you've never not delegated because you couldn't explain. You haven't delegated because the explaining felt like a whole project. A blank page, a Sunday, a long list you'd never finish. So you kept it in your head and called it efficiency.

What if the first draft didn't need to be good? What if you only needed a starting point, something to react to, a gap to fill in with your own details?

That's a smaller job. A job you could do with a coffee, in twenty minutes, on an ordinary Wednesday.

A different Monday

Picture it, later, back at home.

It's Monday, 8:50. The workshop smells of vanilla and warm wax. You're not rushing.

On the shelf above the packing table, there's a folder. Inside are a few plain pages in your own voice: how to answer a late-parcel email, what to say to the supplier when something's short, how to handle the gift wrap question. Nothing fancy. Just how we do it here.

Lukas comes in, reads the top page, and gets on with it. He doesn't ask. He doesn't need to. And when something truly new comes up, he messages you once, and the answer goes into the folder, so it never has to be asked again.

You drink your tea while it's still hot.

On the first day of your next week off, your phone stays face-down. Not because you're being disciplined, but because there's nothing waiting on you. The business can breathe without you in it for a few days. And you feel, quietly, like someone who runs a real business rather than someone holding it together with both hands.
