Client Sent No Brief but Wants It Tomorrow? Farm Shop Box

2026-10-18

It is 16:50 on a Thursday, and Lena is standing at a farm shop honesty box with a bag of apples in one hand and her phone in the other.

She was only going to buy apples. Then the email arrived.

"Hi Lena, great to be working together! Please get started. We'd love to see first results by tomorrow noon. Thanks!"

That is the whole message. There is no brief and no attachment. There is no logo file, no login, and no mention of who decides what "good" looks like.

She reads it twice. The coins in her palm are warm. A hen wanders past the crate of pumpkins, entirely unbothered.

The knot you know

You might be a designer, a developer, a writer or a photographer. Either way, you know this feeling.

The client is friendly. The deposit has not arrived yet, but they said it would. The call last week was warm and fast, and everyone nodded a lot. You left it believing you understood the project. Now you realise you only understood the mood of it.

So a small voice starts bargaining.

Just start. Guess. Make something decent and fix it later.

Another voice answers.

If you guess wrong, it's your evening. And your weekend. And your "no, I can't just redo it for free."

A third voice, the quietest, says the thing nobody likes to hear.

You don't look unprofessional because you asked questions. You look unprofessional because you have no way to ask them without feeling like you're being difficult.

Lena drops the coins in the box. She forgets to take a bag.

Sitting in the car with the engine off

She sits in the car with the apples on the passenger seat and opens her laptop on the steering wheel.

She starts three replies.

The first is too apologetic: "So sorry to ask, I know you're busy, but..."

The second is too sharp: "I can't begin without a brief." She deletes it before her finger leaves the key.

The third is just a question mark and a blinking cursor.

The real cost is not tomorrow's deadline. Tomorrow's deadline is a symptom. The cost is that every new client relationship begins with Lena improvising: a bit of notes on her phone, a half-remembered call, a folder with a name she will not find again in three weeks. Each one costs her a small piece of calm before the work has even started.

It also costs her a small piece of dignity. She quietly doubts whether she is allowed to say "here is how I begin."

What she remembers

While the engine ticks and cools, she remembers a conversation from months ago with a friend who builds websites.

He told her about a bad start and a worse middle. The client had assumed one thing and he had assumed another. Neither of them had written anything down, and nobody was exactly wrong. They just had different pictures in their heads.

"The strange thing," he said, "is that it got better when I stopped treating the start as a favour I was doing the client. I started treating it as part of the job. A normal part. Like a handshake."

She had nodded and forgotten it.

Now she thinks about it differently. A first step that is just how things begin here, calm and the same every time, is not rude. The client would not take it as pushing back. It would feel like being in capable hands.

The reply she sends

She writes it slowly, then sends it.

It is friendly and short. She is excited to begin. She needs a few things from the client first, and she lists them plainly. She suggests a short call tomorrow morning to agree what "first results" means and by when. She adds that she will send a one-page summary after the call so that everyone is looking at the same thing.

She does not apologise once.

Then she drives home with the apples rolling gently in the passenger footwell.

The client answers within the hour: "Of course! 10 works. Sorry, we're a bit all over the place this week."

That is the line that stays with her. People are usually not trying to be difficult. They are all over the place, and they quietly hope someone will bring a little order.

A Monday that starts differently

Imagine a different version of that Thursday. It is not a miracle, just a quieter one.

The new client says yes. Within the hour, they receive a short welcome and a simple set of questions. What is this for? Who signs off? What do you already have? Which logins will I need? What does done look like?

When the kickoff call arrives, you are not scrambling for notes. There is an agenda. After the call there is a summary with the scope in plain words, so nobody is surprised in week three.

You still have your Monday coffee, and it is still hot when you start the real work.

The client feels looked after. You feel like a person who runs a business, not someone catching it as it falls. Nothing about the project is more complicated. It is just that the beginning no longer depends on your mood, your memory or your courage on a Thursday afternoon.
