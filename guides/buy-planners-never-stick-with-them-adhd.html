Buy Planners, Never Stick With Them? The ADHD Drawer

2026-10-17

The drawer that wouldn't close

It's Thursday, 12:40, and you're looking for a stapler.

You find the planners instead.

There are four of them in the bottom drawer, stacked like a small, expensive apology. One is linen-bound with a gold ribbon. One has a sticker sheet you never opened. One has a "morning routine" page with twelve boxes. One is a lovely dotted notebook you swore you would just use freely.

Every one of them was a fresh start. Every one has about three weeks of handwriting, then a few scribbles, then nothing.

Let's call you Mara. You edit video for small brands, alone, from the spare room. A brain that works in bursts has always been part of your life. You've read enough to wonder whether ADHD is part of it, whether or not you ever put a label on it. Either way, the drawer says what you won't say out loud: I'm someone who buys planners and doesn't stick with them.

The three-week story

You know how it goes, because it goes the same way every time.

Day one feels wonderful. New pen, clean page, the quiet joy of a future that looks organised. You fill in everything. Colours. Little icons. A habit tracker with twenty-one rows.

By day nine you miss a Tuesday. Not a disaster. But the Tuesday page sits there, blank, and it looks at you.

By day fourteen there are two blank pages, and opening the planner starts to feel like opening a letter from someone who is disappointed in you.

By day twenty-one you stop opening it. You tell yourself you'll restart on Monday. Then on the first of the month. Then in January, when everyone restarts.

The planner didn't fail because you don't care. It failed because it asked for a person who shows up identically every day. You are not that person. Most people aren't, but you notice it more, because the gap between the page and your actual week is so visible.

What it quietly costs

The money for the planners is the small part.

The bigger cost is the Monday morning at 9:05, when you open your laptop and three client messages are waiting, and you can't tell which one is urgent. You answer the loudest one. The edit that was due Wednesday slides to Thursday, then quietly to Friday.

It's the Sunday evening when you try to remember what you promised to whom. It's the invoice you meant to send, the call you meant to prepare for, the nagging sense that you're always just slightly behind a version of yourself who has it together.

And it's the shame. Not dramatic shame. Just a low hum. A real business would have this sorted by now.

You close the drawer with your hip. The stapler stays lost.

The question that changed the shape of it

That evening, scrolling past yet another "perfect planner system" video, you stop on a thought that has nothing to do with the video.

What if the planner was never the problem? What if the problem was that every one of them was too much?

Twelve boxes. Twenty-one habits. Colour codes. A page for every hour of a day that never goes to plan anyway.

What would you actually use on your worst day? Not your best day. The day when the inbox is loud, your head is foggy and you have nine minutes before a call.

Probably this: three things. Just the three that matter today. And a place to look back at the end of the week without a verdict attached, just a quiet look at what happened and what comes next.

That's it. A page that is almost embarrassingly light, so that missing a day doesn't turn it into a monument to failure. You can skip Tuesday and open Wednesday like nothing happened, because nothing did.

A different Monday

Picture it a few weeks on.

It's Monday, 8:50. The coffee is still too hot. Next to the laptop lies one printed page, slightly creased from the weekend, with three lines on it in your own messy handwriting.

Deliver the cut for the bakery brand. Reply to the new enquiry. Send last month's invoice.

That's the day. Not a life overhaul. Just three lines.

At 11:30 a client message lands that would normally throw you for the afternoon. You read it, you add it to Thursday, and you go back to the cut. The page didn't make you disciplined. It just gave your attention somewhere to rest.

On Friday you spend five minutes looking at the week. Two of the three lines got done on most days, and one slipped. You don't feel guilty. You move it to next week and close the laptop at six.

And the drawer stays closed, because you're not looking for the next planner anymore.
