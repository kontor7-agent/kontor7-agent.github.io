Student Cancelled Last Minute, Never Paid? The Bike Lock

2026-10-21

15:52, one hand on the bike lock

You are crouched on a wet pavement outside a flat you have visited every Wednesday since September. The lock is half closed. Your bag is heavy with the exam workbook you printed last night.

Your phone buzzes in your jacket.

So sorry, something came up, can we skip today?

The lesson starts in eight minutes. You cycled twenty-five of them to get here.

You type No problem at all! with an exclamation mark. You hate the exclamation mark. You send it anyway.

Then you stand there with the lock in your hand and a small, familiar thought: wasn't there something about last month too?

The question you can't answer on the pavement

You try to remember. Did Jonas pay for the two lessons in September? Or was that the other student, the one preparing for the language certificate? One of them paid in cash. One of them said "I'll transfer it tonight." You think. You are not sure.

This is the part nobody tells you about teaching privately. The teaching is the good bit. You like the moment when a tangled sentence suddenly makes sense to someone. You do not like standing on a pavement doing sums from memory.

There is a notebook at home. There are also some bank statements, a few messages in three different apps, and a note on your phone that says check Jonas? The note is from three weeks ago.

So you do what most of us do. You unlock the bike again, ride to the café on the corner and order a tea you did not plan to buy. You open the banking app and scroll. Payment, payment, a refund for something else, a payment that might be the right person.

You are not angry at Jonas. That is the strange part. He is kind, he works shifts, and his life really is complicated. You are angry at the fuzzy feeling that you are running a small business out of your head.

What it quietly costs

The cancelled hour is gone. That is one loss.

But there are others you rarely count. The slot nobody else could take at that short notice. The tea. The forty minutes you now spend working out who owes what instead of preparing next week's material. And the awkward message you will have to write, the one that starts with "Just a quick note" and takes you three drafts.

When you do not know the numbers, you cannot be calm about them. You soften things. You say "no worries" when you do have worries. You keep your rate where it was two years ago, because raising it means knowing what the work is worth, and you have never really seen it laid out.

So you carry a low background hum: am I being taken advantage of, or am I just disorganised? It is hard to tell which, and that is the whole problem.

The small turn

At the café table, the tea going cold, you do something slightly different. Instead of scrolling the bank app again, you open a blank page and write down every lesson from September, one line each. Date, student, taught or cancelled, paid or not.

It takes ten minutes. It is a mess, but it is a mess you can see.

And something loosens. The knot you have been carrying is not made of one big problem. It is made of nine small unknowns. Written down, they turn into a short list. Two lessons unpaid. Three late cancellations. One student who always pays the same day. Suddenly the picture is not "everyone owes me" or "I am bad at this." It is just a picture.

You think: I should have had this from the start. Not because I'm careless. Because nobody gave me a place to put it.

A normal Wednesday, later

Imagine the same Wednesday a few weeks on.

You lock the bike. The message arrives: so sorry, can we skip today? You read it, and your shoulders stay where they are.

You answer kindly, because you are kind. You offer a new slot. That evening, in about a minute, you mark the lesson as a late cancellation. You do not need to decide how you feel about it. It is simply recorded, with the date, and it sits in the same place as everything else you have taught.

On Sunday you open the log for ten minutes. You see who is a little behind and who is fully paid. You see what the late cancellations added up to this month, in hours and in money, in plain numbers. The reminder to Jonas takes a single friendly sentence, because you know exactly which lessons it is about. There is nothing to apologise for, and nothing to guess.

And some quiet Sunday, looking at a few months of honest lines, you notice that your rate has not moved in two years. You do not panic. You just think: right. Time to talk about that. A decision made with the numbers in front of you feels very different from one made on a pavement.

You close the laptop at six. The evening is yours.
