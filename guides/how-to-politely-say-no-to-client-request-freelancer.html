Can't Politely Say No to a Client Request? The Bus Aisle

2026-10-20

You are standing in the aisle of the 17:40 bus, one hand on the rail, the other holding your phone at an awkward angle. The message from your client is three lines long.

"Hi! Quick one: could you also redo the whole onboarding flow? Should be easy for you. Need it by Friday."

There is a smiley at the end. That makes it harder, somehow.

You already know the answer. Your week is full. Friday is not possible. The work is not "quick". It is not in the agreement either. And yet your thumbs hover over the keyboard, typing "Sure, let me see what I can do", deleting it, typing it again.

The yes that comes out on its own

Here is the thing nobody tells you when you start working for yourself: saying no to a client feels like a small act of disloyalty. You are not an employee who can point at a manager. You are the manager, the department and the person who might lose the client.

So you rehearse. Somewhere between the second and third stop you write a version that is too long and apologetic. Then one that is too curt. Then one that sounds like a lawyer wrote it.

The bus empties a little. Your reply is still a blinking cursor.

What you are really weighing is not the task. It is the relationship. Will they think I'm difficult? Will they go quiet? Will the next project go to someone who just says yes?

Those thoughts are loud. And they are mostly about a person who has not actually reacted to anything yet.

What the small yeses cost

You have said yes before, of course. Last month it was a "tiny" extra round of changes. The month before, a call that turned into a workshop. Each one was reasonable on its own.

But they add up, quietly. A Saturday morning at the laptop. A dinner you ate half-listening. A low hum of resentment toward a client who has never been anything but friendly, because they never knew they were asking for something.

That is the part that stings. The no you avoid does not disappear. It comes back as tiredness, as irritation, as a faint feeling that you are not quite running a real business, just reacting to one.

The reply that was already there

Back home, you put the shopping bag down and open the message again. You are no longer on a moving bus. The kettle is on.

And something shifts, just slightly. You realise that you don't need to be braver. You don't need to be cleverer or more charming. You need the words, in a tone that sounds like you on a good day, and you don't have to invent them from scratch at the worst moment.

A good no is usually short. It thanks them for thinking of you. It says plainly what is possible and what is not. It offers one real alternative: a later date, a separate quote, a smaller version. And it stops. No essay, no grovelling, no long defence of your calendar.

You write it in four sentences. You read it once. It sounds kind and clear, and somehow more professional than the yes you nearly sent.

You press send, and then, strangely, nothing bad happens. You make tea.

A Monday that feels different

Imagine a Monday a few weeks on. The inbox opens, and a request lands that is a little outside what you agreed. You feel the old tug in the stomach, but it's smaller. You know roughly what you want to say, and you have a starting point on the screen within a minute.

You adjust two words, add the client's name, and send it before your coffee has cooled.

The reply comes back: "Of course, thanks for being upfront. Send me the quote."

Maybe it goes like that, maybe it doesn't. Some clients grumble. But the difference is that you answered in the open, on your own terms, in a voice you recognise. And on Friday you are closing the laptop at six, not catching up on a favour nobody paid for.
