API Key Visible in Frontend Code? The Tasting Table Text

2026-10-31

The text arrives at 11:20 on a Wednesday, while you are holding a tiny glass of something red and pretending to have an opinion about tannins.

You are at the wine merchant's tasting table with Marta, your client. You built her little online shop in the spring. Her phone buzzes. She glances at it, then at you.

"My nephew says there's a key visible in the website. In the code. He says anyone can see it." She pronounces the word as if it were something you had left on a bus.

"Is that bad?"

You say, "Let me look at that properly." Your voice sounds fine. Your stomach has dropped through the floor.

The longest walk to the car

You thank her, promise to call by two, and walk out past the crates of Riesling.

In the car you open the laptop on the steering wheel. Browser, the shop, the developer tools your nephew-of-a-client apparently knows how to open. There it is, in a long grey line of code: a string of letters and numbers. It has a name that includes the word key.

Your thoughts arrive in a pile.

Is that the kind of key that is supposed to be there? Or the kind that is not? You half remember that some of them are meant to be public and some are not. You half remember reading it in the documentation. Half is not enough at 11:40 with a client waiting.

Then the quieter thoughts. Did I do this? Is it my fault? Did someone already use it? Will she think I'm not a real developer?

Nobody warns you about that last one. The technical question is only part of it. The rest is the sudden fear that the whole thing, the work and the invoices and the "professional" in your email signature, was held together by luck.

What the doubt actually costs

Here is what it costs, and it is not dramatic.

It costs the next two hours. You do not eat the sandwich. You open the dashboard of the service the key belongs to and stare at menus you have not looked at in months. You search the same sentence in six different ways and read six confident answers that disagree with each other.

It costs the call at two. You will have to say something to Marta, and you do not yet know whether it will be "all fine, that one is meant to be public" or "I need to change something today." You cannot say either one honestly, because you have not checked. You have only worried.

And it costs something slower. The next time you ship anything, a small voice will ask whether you looked at everything, and you will not be sure what "everything" is.

That is the real weight. Not this one key. The absence of a list.

The turn

You do eventually sort it out. Illustratively, in this story, it goes like this: you work out what the key is for, what it can and cannot do, and whether it belongs where it is. You make one calm decision, then another. It takes less time than the worry did, which is the part that annoys you most.

What stays with you is the thought you have while closing the laptop. I was not scared of the key. I was scared of not knowing what I had checked.

Those are different problems. The first one is a single line in a browser. The second is a feeling you can carry into every project, every launch, every Wednesday-morning text.

And the second one can be fixed before the text ever arrives.

A different Wednesday

Picture the same tasting table, a few months on.

Marta's phone buzzes. Someone, maybe the nephew again, has found something curious in the code. She turns the screen toward you with a half-smile.

You read it. You do not feel the floor move.

You know which keys on that site are meant to be seen and which are not, because you wrote it down when you built it. You know when you last tested the routes, because the date is in a tracker you can open in ten seconds. You say, "Good eye. I'll send you a short note this afternoon about exactly what that is."

Then you taste the wine. It is, you decide, rather nice.

The note is easy to write because you have a report you can adapt instead of a blank page. It is plain, honest and in the voice of someone who runs a real business. Because you do.

Nothing about this makes you invincible. Software is software, and someone will always find something. What changes is that surprise stops feeling like an accusation. You have a list. You have a record. You know what "done" looks like.
