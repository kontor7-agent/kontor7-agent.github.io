December and Your Freelance Receipts a Mess? The Tree Lot

2026-10-21

The man at the tree lot is holding a Nordmann fir upright and waiting. Your card is in one hand. In the other is your wallet, which won't close.

You pull out the card and a small paper avalanche follows. A fuel slip. A faded ticket from a print shop. Something from a stationery shop in March, or maybe April. A napkin with a number on it that you wrote for a reason you can no longer remember.

You crouch on the wet gravel and gather it all up. The man says, kindly, "No rush." There is a rush, though. It's just inside your chest.

Let's call you Mara. You're an illustrator, six years on your own. You love the work. You do not love the part where the year ends and a box in your head opens.

The box with no lid

There's a drawer at home. Another in the car. A folder on your laptop called Belege_neu_FINAL, which tells you everything. A few hundred photos on your phone are mixed in with dog pictures and screenshots of train times.

In January, someone will ask for all of it in order. Maybe it's you, maybe your accountant, maybe a form with a deadline. You already know how that week feels. You'll spread paper across the kitchen table and squint at ink that has gone pale. You'll wonder whether that software subscription was paid yearly or monthly.

None of this is dramatic. That's why nobody talks about it. It's just a low hum under December. You walk past the mulled wine stalls and think about the drawer.

It costs you more than time. It costs you the feeling of being a proper business. You send clients tidy files and clear invoices, and then you go home and live out of a wallet.

The call that came at 2:30

Two days later, you're at your desk with a cup of tea going cold. A client call is about to start. It's a small one, a friendly one, about a spring book cover.

She's early and chatty. Near the end she says, "By the way, do you need anything from me for your year-end? Any paperwork on our side?"

You say, "Oh, I think I'm fine."

You are not sure you're fine. You smile at the camera anyway.

After the call you sit still for a moment. The cover project is done and paid. The thing you can't answer is smaller and sharper: where is everything I spent to do this work? The pencils, the scanner repair, the licence, the courier. You know it all happened. You just can't point to it.

That's when the thought arrives, quietly, without any drama. It doesn't have to be a box with no lid.

What if it had edges?

You don't need a grand system. You've bought those before, and they lasted about nine days. What you want is smaller.

You want one place where each receipt lands. You want a short list of what a year-end actually involves, so it stops being one big fog. You want the dates that matter written down where you'll see them, instead of living as a vague dread. And you want to look at it and see how far along you are.

Something that says this much is done, rather than everything is undone.

You spend part of an evening gathering the paper from the drawer, the car and the wallet. You do it with the radio on, not in a panic. It's slower than you'd like, and you find a few receipts you'd given up on. A couple you can't place at all, and you let those be what they are.

But the pile has a beginning and an end now. It has edges. That alone loosens something behind your ribs.

A normal Monday in January

Now imagine the first Monday of the new year.

The tree is long gone and the needles are in the hoover. You make coffee. The light comes through the window thin and white.

You open one file. The receipts are already in it, logged a few at a time through the weeks. The checklist shows most of its boxes ticked. The deadlines are written down, plainly, with room to breathe around them. A progress bar sits near the top, mostly filled.

If your accountant asks for something, you know where it is. You may even send it before lunch.

No kitchen-table avalanche. No napkin with a mystery number. Just a quiet hour, and then you get back to drawing.

That's the whole change. The year-end doesn't get smaller. It just stops being a surprise.
