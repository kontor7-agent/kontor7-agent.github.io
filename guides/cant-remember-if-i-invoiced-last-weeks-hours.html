Not Sure You Invoiced Last Week's Hours? The Record Shop

2026-10-25

You are flipping through a crate of second-hand jazz records on a Saturday morning. The shop smells of dust and warm cardboard. Someone behind the counter is humming along to a trumpet.

Then your phone buzzes in your coat pocket. It's a short message from a client: "Hi, quick one for our budget sheet. Did last week's hours already go out on an invoice?"

You stand very still with a record half out of its sleeve.

Did they? You worked four long days for this client. You remember the Thursday call that ran over. You remember saying to yourself, I'll invoice that Friday. You do not remember Friday.

The little film that plays in your head

You try to reconstruct it. Friday afternoon, you were tired. Maybe you opened the invoice template. Maybe you typed the client's name. Maybe you got as far as the hours and then the doorbell rang.

You can picture the document, but you can't tell whether you're remembering a real one or one you meant to make.

So you do what many self-employed people do. You promise yourself you'll check later, and you put the phone away. You buy a record you don't really need, because you're standing in a shop and it feels rude to leave empty-handed.

But the question follows you out onto the pavement. It sits next to you on the tram. It's there while you chop onions.

What it quietly costs

The money is one part of it. If the hours were never billed, that's a week of your work sitting in nobody's accounts.

But the bigger cost is the feeling. You are a grown professional, and you can't answer a simple question about your own business. You'd rather not reply to the client yet, because any answer you give might be wrong.

If you say "yes, sent it" and you didn't, that's embarrassing. If you say "let me check" on a Saturday, it sounds like you're winging it. And if you quietly send a new invoice and the old one turns up, you've billed them twice. That's a conversation nobody wants.

So the weekend gets a small grey cloud over it. Nothing is on fire. You just carry a question you can't put down.

Saturday afternoon, still digging

Back home, you open your invoice folder. There's a file called Invoice_final.pdf and another called Invoice_final2.pdf. One has a date in March. Your email's sent folder offers several messages with the word "invoice" in them, none of them clearly the right one.

You open your notes app. There's a line that reads Thu: client call + edits ~3h? with a question mark you wrote to yourself and never resolved.

Twenty minutes later you have a rough answer, probably. And that word, probably, is the thing that wears you down. You're not running your business from what you know. You're running it from what you can dig up and guess.

The moment it clicks

Later that evening you're watching the record spin, the one you bought out of politeness. It's actually good. And a thought arrives, quite calmly:

It shouldn't take detective work to know what I've billed.

Nobody should have to piece together their own week from sent emails and half-remembered Fridays. The answer to "did I invoice this?" should be a glance, not an investigation.

You don't need a complicated accounting system for that. You need one quiet place where hours and invoices sit next to each other. Hours go in as you work. An invoice goes out and the hours are marked as covered. Whatever is left over is simply what's still waiting to be billed.

That's all. A small, boring, reliable picture of where things stand.

What a Monday feels like after that

Imagine the same message arriving on an ordinary Monday morning, with a coffee beside you.

"Did last week's hours already go out on an invoice?"

You open one file. You see the client, the week, the hours, and next to them an invoice number and a due date. Or you see a gap, which means those hours haven't been billed yet. Either way you know within seconds.

You reply in one calm line: "Yes, invoice sent Friday, due on the 14th." Or: "Not yet. It goes out this afternoon." Both of those are perfectly professional answers. What matters is that you gave one without your stomach tightening.

The rest of the week feels different too. On Friday afternoon you glance at what hasn't been billed and clear it. You close the laptop at a sensible hour. When a client asks about hours, you don't brace yourself.

This isn't about becoming a more organised person. You were never careless. You were working from memory because nobody gave you a better place to keep the truth.
