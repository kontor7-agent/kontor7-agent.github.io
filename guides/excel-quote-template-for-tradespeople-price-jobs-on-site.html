Quoted on the Doorstep, Lost Money by Friday: Quotes for Tradespeople

2026-10-13

The customer is standing in her hallway with a mug of tea, and you are standing in her bathroom with a tape measure.

"So, roughly what are we looking at?" she asks.

You look at the old tiles. You look at the pipe running behind the basin. You do a quick sum in your head, the way you have done a hundred times, and you hear yourself say a number.

She nods. "That sounds fair."

You smile. You shake hands. And somewhere under your ribs, a small voice says: did I forget something?

The sum you did with your thumb on the tape measure

Nobody teaches you this part. You learned the trade, the craft, the way a wall should be finished. You did not learn how to price a job in ninety seconds while someone watches your face.

So you do what everyone does. You think of the last similar job. You add a bit for luck. You round it to something that feels polite. Then you drive off and the number follows you down the road.

By the time you reach the first set of traffic lights, you are already adding things back in. The extra trip to the merchant. The silicone, the screws, the sandpaper. The day the delivery will be late and you will stand around waiting.

The quote is already gone. You can't un-say it.

The glove box knows

Three weeks later the job is done, and it was good work. She is delighted. She even sends a thank-you message with a little smiley face.

You, meanwhile, are sitting in the van with the engine off, looking at the glove box.

It is full. Receipts from the builders' merchant, curled and fading. A fuel slip. A card payment slip with no name on it. Somewhere in there is the receipt for the tiles, you are fairly sure. Probably.

You tell yourself you will sort it on Sunday. You told yourself that last Sunday too.

What you don't say out loud is the quieter worry: I don't actually know if I made money on that job. You were busy all week. The customer is happy. The bank balance looks okay. But "looks okay" is not the same as knowing, and you can feel the difference.

The part that is harder than the work

Then there is the other question, the one that makes your stomach tighten a little more.

Has she paid?

You sent the invoice. Or you said you would send it. Or you sent it, and she said she'd transfer it "at the end of the week," and that was nine days ago. You don't want to be the person who chases a lovely customer over a bathroom. But you also bought the materials, and you did the work, and your own bills don't wait.

So you do nothing, and carry it around instead. A little weight, not heavy enough to stop you, just heavy enough that you notice it every time you open your phone.

It costs you more than the money. It costs you the clean feeling of finishing a job well. The satisfaction gets smudged by "hold on, was that one even worth it?"

The moment it doesn't have to be like this

Imagine a different version of that hallway.

You are in the same bathroom, with the same tea-holding customer. But this time you don't pull a number out of the air. You have a simple way of working it out. Your hours. Your materials. Your usual margin, already sitting there, so you are not deciding it from scratch while she watches.

You say a number, and you know why it is that number.

That's it. That is the whole shift. Not a bigger business or a fancy system. Just the quiet confidence of a figure you can stand behind, because it was built, not guessed.

A normal Thursday, afterwards

It is Thursday, around lunchtime. You are sitting on the tailgate with a sandwich, and the sun is on the van.

You open one sheet. The bathroom job is there. What you quoted, what the materials really cost, what is left over. You see the real margin, and it is fine. Better than fine. Or, once in a while, it is a bit thin, and you think: right, next time I will price that kind of job differently. Either way, you know. The not-knowing is gone.

The receipts from this week are logged. The glove box holds a few slips, not a season's worth.

And you can see, in one glance, who still owes you. Two names. One is five days over. You send a short, friendly message, the kind that doesn't feel like begging because it is simply a fact. Then you finish your sandwich.

On Sunday you don't sort anything. Sunday is Sunday.
