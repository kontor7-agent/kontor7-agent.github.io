Client Wants Unlimited Revisions? The Tennis Club Bench

2026-10-20

It is Saturday, 10:30, and you are standing at a vending machine in a tennis club corridor.

Your niece has a junior match. You promised to be there. You have a paper cup of lukewarm tea in one hand and your phone in the other, and the phone has just buzzed.

The client's email is friendly. That is what makes it worse.

"Thanks for the second round! We love the direction. Just so we're aligned, we'll keep tweaking until it feels perfect. Unlimited revisions are fine, right? 😊"

You read it twice. The smiley sits at the end like a small, polite hand on your shoulder.

You are an illustrator. Or a web designer, or a copywriter. It doesn't matter which. The question is the same, and your stomach knows it before your head does.

The sentence you can't find

You walk back to the bench and sit down. On court two, someone double-faults. Nobody around you has noticed that you've gone quiet.

In your head you try the answers.

"Of course!" That one is easy to type and expensive to live with.

"Actually, our agreement says two rounds." True, but it sounds like a lawyer wrote it.

"Let me think about it." That one buys you a day and nothing else.

You open a reply and type: "Hi Jonas, thanks so much for the kind words. I wanted to…" Then you stop, because you've reached the part where you have to say something firm and you have no idea what it sounds like in your own voice.

So you delete it. You tell yourself you'll do it properly later, at the desk, with a clear head.

You know how that goes. Later becomes Sunday evening. Sunday evening becomes Monday at 8:40, and by then you've already half-agreed in your own mind, because saying yes is quieter than saying no.

What "unlimited" quietly costs

Here's what nobody puts on the invoice.

Unlimited doesn't arrive as one big request. It arrives as a Tuesday tweak, a Thursday "one more idea", a Sunday "sorry, tiny thing". Each one is small enough to feel petty to refuse. Together they eat the week you had planned for other clients, and they eat the margin you quoted for.

You don't resent the client, not really. You resent that you didn't say it at the start, and you resent that you still can't say it now. That second resentment points inward, and it's the heavier one.

There is also the quiet question underneath: am I a professional, or someone who just does what she's told? A professional would have the words ready. Wouldn't she?

On the court, your niece wins a point. You clap a little late.

The pause that changes the afternoon

Between games, a woman on the next bench is talking to a friend. She runs a small bookkeeping practice, she says, and she used to dread exactly this kind of message. "I stopped trying to be clever," she says. "I just keep the wording somewhere I can find it. Then it's not a mood, it's a paragraph."

You don't join the conversation. But the phrase stays with you: not a mood, a paragraph.

Because maybe that's the whole problem. You've been treating every awkward email as a new test of your character. Every time, you start from zero, with your pulse up and your tea going cold, hunting for words that sound firm but not cold, clear but not cross.

What if the words already existed? Not as a script that makes you sound like a robot, but as a starting point you could bend to your own voice. A warm version for the client who is genuinely lovely. A plainer one for the client who has done this before. One that offers a way forward, an extra round at a fair price, instead of just closing a door.

The no stops being a confrontation and becomes a sentence you edit.

Monday, a different kind

Picture the Monday after that.

The same kind of email lands at 9:15. You feel the old flicker in your chest, but it's shorter now. You open your notes, find the wording you trust, change the client's name, soften one line, sharpen another. It sounds like you on a good day.

You press send. Then you make a coffee, and the coffee stays hot.

The client replies within the hour. They're fine with it. Most people, it turns out, are fine with clear terms when those terms are offered kindly. Maybe they even take the extra round at the price you named. Either way, nothing broke.

The afternoon belongs to the work you actually like. By six, the laptop is closed.

That's all the relief is: not winning an argument, just not carrying it around.
