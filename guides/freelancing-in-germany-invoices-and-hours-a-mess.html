Freelance in Germany: Invoices and Hours a Mess? Aisle 7

2026-10-27

It is 10:20 on a Friday, and Mareike is standing in aisle 7 of the hardware store, holding a box of shelf brackets.

Her phone buzzes. It is the accounts department of a client in Hamburg. She is a freelance copy editor, and this client has been with her for two years.

"Hi Mareike, quick question. Invoice 14 from March. Which hours does it cover? Our project lead says it overlaps with February."

She puts the brackets down on a pallet of potting soil.

The question you can't answer from an aisle

She knows the answer is somewhere. That is the maddening part. It is in a spreadsheet called Zeiten_neu, or in Zeiten_neu_FINAL, or in the notes app, or in the half-filled paper calendar by the kettle. The invoice itself is a Word document. She copies the last one, changes the date, changes the number, and hopes she has not skipped one.

She types: "Of course, I'll check and get back to you this afternoon."

She hits send and feels the small, familiar drop in her stomach. It is not panic. It is quieter than that. It is the feeling of being a professional who cannot, right now, prove what she did.

She buys the brackets anyway. In the car she sits for a minute with the engine off.

What the mess quietly costs

Nobody warns you about this part of working for yourself in Germany. The work is fine. The clients are mostly kind. It is everything around the work.

You are not just the person who edits texts. You are also the person who writes the invoice, remembers the due date, notices when a payment doesn't arrive, and keeps the hours straight. And you are the only one who can do it. There is no back office. There is you, a laptop, and a folder structure that made sense in January.

So the costs are small and constant:

  • A Sunday afternoon spent rebuilding last month from email threads.
  • A reminder you never sent because you couldn't remember if the invoice had gone out.
  • A faint embarrassment when someone asks a simple question and you say "let me check."
  • The sense that other freelancers have it together, and you are somehow winging it.

They don't have it all together, probably. But it does not feel that way in a parking space outside a hardware store.

Three hours of archaeology

That afternoon Mareike does what she always does. She digs.

She opens one file, then another. She finds the March hours in one place and the February hours in another. She finds that two days appear in both. She cannot tell whether the overlap is a typo or a real double entry. She scrolls through her sent mail to find out which PDF actually went to the client.

By 17:30 she has an answer, and it is fine. Nothing was billed twice. But it took three hours, and the answer lives only in her head and one long email.

What stays with her is not the three hours. It is the thought on the drive home. Next month this will happen again, and it will be a different invoice.

The turning point is small

It doesn't arrive as a revelation. A colleague mentions it over coffee the following week, almost as an aside. She keeps hours and invoices in one place, and she just looks at it on Monday morning.

"One place" is the phrase that sticks with Mareike.

Not a new system to learn. Not an app with a monthly fee and forty features. Just one calm sheet where the hours go in, the invoice gets its number on its own, the due date is already there, and every invoice says whether it is open, paid or overdue.

She doesn't need more discipline. She needs fewer places to look.

A different Friday

Picture the same Friday, a month or two later.

The phone buzzes in aisle 7. The same accounts department. The same question about an invoice.

Mareike keeps walking toward the brackets. She opens one sheet on her phone or, if she is at her desk, on her laptop. She sees the invoice, the hours behind it, the dates. She writes back in two lines, with no "let me check" and no dropped stomach.

Then she goes back to choosing between the black brackets and the white ones.

On Monday mornings she takes ten minutes with her coffee. She looks at what is open, what is due this week, and what is overdue and deserves a friendly nudge. It is not exciting. It is just quiet. The knot that used to sit under her ribs on the first of the month has gone somewhere else.

She finishes work at six, and the evening is hers.
