Sorted One Column in Excel, Rows Mixed Up? The Van Call

2026-11-07

Imagine Jonas, a one-man garden maintenance business. It is 8:05 on a Tuesday morning and he is in the van, parked outside a client's gate with the engine ticking as it cools. The laptop is balanced on the steering wheel. In ten minutes he has a call about next season's contract.

He only wanted the customer list in alphabetical order. One column. Two seconds.

Now he is looking at a row that says Mrs. Albrecht, 0171 555 0142, Hedge trimming, 180. Except Mrs. Albrecht has never had a hedge trimmed. The phone number belongs to someone else. The amount belongs to a third person. The names are tidy and alphabetical. Everything beside them is wrong.

The little pop-up you clicked past

He remembers a small grey box appearing. He remembers dismissing it the way you dismiss every box on a Tuesday morning. He had a coffee in one hand.

He saved the file before he sorted it. Or did he? The autosave may have run since then. He isn't sure, and that not-knowing is the worst part.

He scrolls. Row 40. Row 90. Row 140. Every row looks plausible from a distance, and every row might be a small lie.

What it quietly costs

On paper the damage is small. A list went out of order.

But Jonas knows what that list is. It is who has a standing order, who gets a Friday visit, who owes him for last month, and whose gate code he can never remember. It is the whole business, held together in rows.

So his stomach does the thing it does. He wonders whether he can trust any number in the file again. He thinks about the invoice he sent last week and whether it went to the right person. He thinks about the call in nine minutes, where he is supposed to sound like a man with a real business and a clear picture of his own work.

There is a smaller feeling under that one, the one nobody mentions. Real businesses don't do this. He knows it is not true. It still arrives every time.

The call

The call goes fine, mostly. He says "let me send you the figures this afternoon" in the calm voice he has practised. Behind the voice, the laptop is open on the passenger seat, and the screen is still wrong.

He spends his lunch break in the van with a sandwich, comparing the mixed-up file against old invoices, his phone's contacts and a text from March. It works, slowly. It takes most of the hour. Afterwards he doesn't feel relieved. He feels like someone who got away with it this time.

The sentence that changes it

That evening a friend who builds small tools for a living looks at the file over his shoulder. She says something that stays with him.

"The sort wasn't the problem. The file had no safety net. Nothing told you what was about to change."

It had never crossed his mind that a tidy-up could come with a preview. He had always treated it as one click, a leap of faith, and a little prayer.

It does not have to be like that. Cleaning up a list can be a calm, boring step. You see what would change before it changes. You get a plain note afterwards of what was touched. The original stays where it was. The rows keep belonging to their own people.

The same Tuesday, a few weeks later

Picture it again. It is 8:05, same gate, same cooling engine. Jonas has the list open, and he has his coffee in the other hand.

This time the list is sorted, de-duplicated, with the phone numbers all in one format. He didn't wrestle any of it by hand. He ran a clean-up the evening before, glanced at what it would change, and watched it finish. There was a short report on his screen. Nothing surprised him.

Now he is looking out through the windscreen at a hedge instead of a spreadsheet. When the call starts he already knows who is on a standing order and who is behind. He says his price once and does not apologise for it.

Nothing dramatic happened. He just stopped bracing.
