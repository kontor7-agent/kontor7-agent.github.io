Passwords and Tokens in Your API Logs? The Ski Boot Queue

2026-11-02

The queue that wasn't supposed to include work

The boot rental hall smells of wet wool and rubber. Jonas is in line behind a family arguing about shoe sizes. His daughter is hopping on one foot. His partner has the passports. It is Monday, 9:10 a.m., the first morning of a week off that took him four months to book.

He only meant to check one thing. A client's developer had written the night before: "Our log vendor flagged something in the API logs. Can you look when you have a minute?"

So he opens the log viewer on his phone, thumb-scrolling with one glove off. He searches for the endpoint that handles logins.

There it is. A request body, printed in full, plain as a shopping list. An email address. A password. A few lines below, an Authorization: Bearer header with a long token after it.

He wrote that logging line. Eight months ago. It said temporary in a comment.

The second thought is worse than the first

The first thought is simple: oh no, I logged a password.

The second arrives before the queue has moved a single step: what else is in there?

If the login route prints its body, what do the other routes print? What about the error handler? The password-reset flow? The webhook that receives a third party's secret? Where do these logs go, who can read them, and how long are they kept?

He doesn't know. That is the part that sits in his chest. It isn't one mistake. It's not knowing the size of it.

"Sir, what size?" says the boy behind the counter.

"Forty-one," says Jonas. "No. Forty-two. Sorry."

What it quietly costs

He carries the boots to a bench and pretends to be fine. His daughter shows him how the buckles click. He says "wow" at the right moment, but his eyes are on a phone he has turned face-down on his knee.

This is the cost nobody puts on an invoice. Not the fix, which might take twenty minutes. It's the week. It's the lunch on the terrace where he nods at a story and thinks about log retention. It's the sentence he will have to write to the client: "I found it, here is what I'm doing." And the one underneath it, the one he doesn't want to write: "I'm not sure that's everything."

He has done careful work for years. He knows what a secret is. And still, one forgotten debug line made him feel like someone who is winging it.

The turning point, on a plastic bench

He does what he can from a bench in a boot hall. He messages the client: he's seen it, he'll handle it today, and could they restrict access to that log stream in the meantime. Then he puts the phone in his jacket and goes to find skis.

On the lift, somewhere between the first pylon and the second, something shifts. The problem isn't that he found a token in a log. The problem is that he found it by accident, because a vendor happened to flag it. He never had a way of looking on purpose.

No list he could walk through. No fixed set of questions asked of every route, the same way, every time, so that "what else am I missing?" has an answer other than a shrug.

He doesn't need to be cleverer. He needs a calm, boring, repeatable pass over the whole API. Something he can do on a Tuesday, with coffee, and then say honestly: I looked, here's what I checked, here's what I fixed.

What a normal Monday could feel like

Imagine the same morning with that in place.

The message from the vendor comes in. Jonas reads it, nods, and opens the one page where he already tracks his review. He sees which items are done and which are open. He fixes the log line, then checks the neighbours of that mistake instead of imagining them. He writes the client a short, steady note: what he found, what changed, what he checked besides.

Then he goes back to the soup.

Nothing about that is dramatic. That's the point. The knot loosens not because nothing went wrong, but because the next step is obvious. A client who gets a calm, specific report tends to trust you more after a slip, not less.
