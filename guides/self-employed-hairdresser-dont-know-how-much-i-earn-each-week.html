Self-Employed Hairdresser: What Did I Actually Earn This Week?

2026-10-14

It's Tuesday, 1:20 pm. Your 12:30 colour is under the foil and you finally have eleven minutes.

You sit on the stool in the back room with a cheese sandwich in one hand and your phone in the other. Your sister-in-law asked a simple question over the weekend: "So how's business? What do you take home in a week?"

You said, "Oh, it's fine, busy." And it is busy. Your feet know it.

But now you're trying to answer it properly, and you can't.

The number that won't hold still

You scroll through the booking app. Thirty-one appointments last week. You scroll through your banking app. Some card payments arrived, some haven't landed yet. There's a small notebook by the till with cash and tips in your handwriting, some of it in pencil.

Three places, three versions of the week.

You try to add it up in your head. The cut at 9:00 was paid by card. The bridal trial was a deposit, then the rest in cash. Someone tipped you, but was that in the notebook or only in your pocket? The chair rent went out on the first. The colour you ordered last Thursday, was that this week or last?

The sandwich goes dry in your hand.

You don't actually know what you earned. You know you were tired. You know the till felt heavy on Saturday. That's all.

What it quietly costs

Nobody tells you this part of working for yourself: not knowing is its own kind of weight.

It's the small flinch when a friend asks about prices and you say "I should probably put them up" without any idea whether you should. It's the way you hesitate before booking a week off, because you can't tell if you can afford it. It's Sunday evening, sitting with the notebook and a calculator, getting a different total each time and giving up.

And there's the empty slot on Friday at 10:15. Someone didn't show. You felt annoyed for about a minute, then the next client sat down and you forgot. But that gap was real money, and you have no idea how many of those add up in a month.

It isn't laziness. You're good with people, good with hands, good with colour. Nobody trained you to be a bookkeeper in a salon coat.

But a little voice says: a real business would know this.

The foil timer goes off

You put the sandwich down. Your client is waiting and she's telling you about her holiday, and you smile and rinse and listen.

But somewhere between the basin and the blow-dry the thought changes shape. It isn't "I'm bad at this." It's quieter, and kinder:

What if I only needed one place to look?

Not an accountant's program. Not forty tabs. Just somewhere that shows what came in by cash, what came in by card, what the no-shows cost, and which of your services actually earn their time. The balayage that takes four hours, the quick fringe trim you do for free-ish, the early-morning slot nobody wants.

You don't need it to be clever. You need it to be there, and simple enough that you'd use it on a tired Thursday.

A different Monday

Picture the same salon, a few weeks later.

It's Monday, 8:40 am. The kettle is on, the mirrors smell of glass cleaner, and the first client isn't due until nine. You open one file. It takes about ten minutes.

Cash from the weekend goes in. Card payments go in. The two totals sit next to each other and, for once, they agree with what's in the bank. You can see the week as a single number. Not a feeling, a number.

There's a small line for the no-show on Friday. You look at it and nothing in your chest tightens. It's just information. It tells you that gap cost you something you can now name, and that maybe a deposit for long appointments is worth a thought. Your decision, your voice, your timing.

The colour services show up a bit ahead of the quick trims. That doesn't mean you drop anyone. It means you can set your prices on purpose instead of on guilt.

Then you close the file and the day begins. Your sister-in-law asks again at the next family lunch, and you answer in one calm sentence. You even enjoy saying it.

Nothing about your work changed. You still stand all day, still laugh with regulars, still sweep up at six. But you've stopped carrying the week around in your head like a bag of loose change.
