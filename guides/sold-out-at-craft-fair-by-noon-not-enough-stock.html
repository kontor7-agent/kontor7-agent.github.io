Sold Out by Noon at the Craft Fair? The Empty Crate

2026-10-30

It is 12:10 on a Saturday, and the table in front of Mara is almost bare.

Three weeks of evenings went into those hats. Wool from the good supplier, labels sewn in by hand, a price card she rewrote twice. Forty-two hats packed into the car at 6:30 this morning. Now there is a single grey one with a slightly wonky bobble, and a woman holding it up to the light.

"Do you have this in green?"

"I did," Mara says. "They went early."

The woman smiles kindly and walks on. The next person asks the same thing. So does the one after.

The nice kind of bad feeling

Everyone around her says the same thing. "Sold out by noon! Wonderful!" And it is wonderful, in a way. Nobody walks away from a stall that sells out because the work was bad.

But Mara stands there with an empty crate under the table, and what she feels is not joy. It is a small, hot knot behind the ribs.

She knows what she is watching. People with money in their hands, walking past her, because she did not bring enough. Families who came for her. A mother who photographed the green hat an hour ago and said she'd come back after the sausage stand.

She came back at 11:50. The green ones were gone.

Mara folds the cloth, slowly, so it looks like a choice. She tells herself the day was a success. Half of her believes it.

The question under the question

In the car park, while the van next door loads up, she opens the notes app on her phone. Where does she even start?

How many hats should she have brought? Fifty? Eighty? She has no idea. She guessed forty-two because it felt like a lot, and because last year's fair was rainy and she went home with eleven.

That is the strange part. Last year, too many. This year, too few. Both times she was guessing, and both times it felt like a verdict on her.

And under that question sits a quieter one she rarely says out loud: did she even make money today?

The takings are in a biscuit tin. The float was a handful of coins she counted at breakfast. The wool receipts are somewhere in the glovebox, mixed with a parking ticket. She knows roughly what she sold. She does not know what each hat really cost once she counts the stall fee, the petrol and the hours at the needles.

Her evenings are spent knitting. Her Sundays are spent wondering. Neither feels like running a business, and that is the part that stings: the feeling of being a hobby that happens to take cash.

Monday, with the tin on the table

On Monday she empties the biscuit tin onto the kitchen table and counts out the notes. The numbers feel good for about four minutes.

Then the old fog arrives. Was the 28 euro hat actually worth it? Which colour went first, and why does she only remember the green? How many should she make for the Christmas market in five weeks, when she has only so many evenings?

She could guess again. She is good at guessing. It has cost her a lot of empty tables and a lot of quiet resentment.

Or she could do the thing she has been putting off: write it down. Not in a new notebook that will be abandoned by December. Somewhere simple. A place where stock, sales, float and cost sit side by side, so the next fair is a decision and not a hope.

What a calmer market day looks like

Picture the Christmas market, five weeks on.

Mara arrives at 7:15 with the car packed. Not packed to the roof out of panic, and not under-packed out of caution, but packed for a number she actually chose, based on what sold last time and what each hat really earns her.

The float is counted, and it is the right size. Nobody waits while she hunts for a five-euro note. When a woman asks about a custom order for her daughter's birthday, Mara says a price without flinching, because she knows what her time is worth.

At noon there are still hats on the table. A few colours are low, and she knows which, and she isn't surprised. Some of the best stock is still there for the afternoon crowd.

At four, she packs up. The tin is counted once. She closes the laptop that evening before the soup goes cold.

Nothing about it is dramatic. That is the point. A good market day just feels like a good day, not like a lucky escape.
