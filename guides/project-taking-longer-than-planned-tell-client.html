Project Taking Longer Than Planned? The Playground Bench

2026-10-20

Tuesday, 10:35. You are on a playground bench with a lukewarm coffee, watching a small person in a yellow raincoat commit fully to the slide.

Your phone is on your knee. Your laptop is at home, where a video edit sits at "almost there" for the third week running.

The client's message is on the screen: "Hi Tomas, just checking we're still good for Friday? Marketing wants to line things up. Thanks!"

You are not still good for Friday. You haven't been for ten days.

The sentence you keep not writing

Here is what happened, and it is nobody's villain story. The footage was longer than the brief suggested. The first round of feedback arrived as five separate emails. One clip turned out to be unusable, and you rebuilt a whole sequence around it without telling anyone, because you thought you'd catch up.

You didn't catch up. Now it is Tuesday, the deadline is Friday, and the honest answer is "the end of next week".

You have typed that answer four times since breakfast. Every version sounds wrong.

One reads like an excuse: "Unfortunately, due to unforeseen circumstances..." Another grovels: "I'm so sorry, I know this is terrible." A third is too breezy, as if a week were nothing. The last one you deleted because it had the word "honestly" in it twice, and that felt like a confession.

The slide ends. The raincoat runs back to the ladder. You type, delete, type.

What the silence is costing

Nobody warns you that the awkward email is not the expensive part. The waiting is.

For ten days you've carried a low hum of dread into everything. You answered the client's earlier messages with cheerful vagueness. You worked on Saturday morning "just to get ahead", and ended up more tired and no further along. Last night you lay awake rehearsing a phone call that hadn't happened yet.

And somewhere underneath, a quieter thought: a real professional would have seen this coming.

That thought is not true. But it is loud, and it makes the email harder to write. The longer you wait, the more the message has to explain, and the more it feels like it has to apologise for.

So the delay grows a second delay, one made of pure avoidance.

The bench conversation

A woman sits down at the other end of the bench with a thermos. Her kid is also on the slide, also in a raincoat, and you nod at each other the way parents do.

She notices the phone, the frown, the thumb hovering. "Work?" she says.

"A client. I'm running late on something and I can't find the right way to say it."

She laughs, not unkindly. "I run a small removals firm. I used to dread that call more than the actual late truck." She unscrews her thermos. "These days I just say what happened, what the new date is, and what I'm doing about it. Three lines. People mostly say 'thanks for telling me'. It's the finding out late that makes them cross."

Then her kid yells and she is gone, jogging towards the sandpit.

You sit there with that. What happened. The new date. What I'm doing about it. It isn't a trick. It's just that someone said it out loud, in a normal voice, with a thermos in her hand, and suddenly it sounds like something a calm person does.

Not a better excuse, just a steadier voice

You realise what you've been missing. It was never the perfect apology. You needed a starting point that didn't come from panic, a draft with the right bones that you could make sound like you.

So you stop trying to write it from nothing. You write the plain version: the new delivery date, what the client will see on Wednesday so they know the work is real, and one small question about the feedback they've sent so far. No "unfortunately". No "honestly". No cringing.

It takes six minutes. You read it once, change one word, and press send before your brain can open a committee meeting.

The reply comes before the raincoat has finished the slide twice more:

"Thanks for the heads-up! Next Friday is fine, we hadn't locked the campaign slot yet. Can't wait to see Wednesday's cut."

That's it. That's the thing you carried for ten days.

A different kind of Wednesday

Imagine the next time this happens, because with real projects it does happen, and not because you are bad at this.

The edit slips. You notice on Monday morning instead of the following Tuesday. You open a note, find the version of the message that fits this moment, change the names and the date, and send it before your first coffee is cold. It's a short, even-voiced email, the kind you'd be glad to receive from someone else.

Then you close it and get on with the work. There's no hum at the back of your head. On Saturday you do something that isn't an edit.

The client, for their part, learns something quietly useful about you: when something moves, you say so early, plainly, and with a plan. That is a very professional thing to be known for, and it costs one calm paragraph.
