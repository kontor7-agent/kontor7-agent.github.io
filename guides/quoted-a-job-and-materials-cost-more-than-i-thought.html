Quoted a Job, Materials Cost More Than I Thought: The Builders' Merchant

2026-10-14

The total on the little screen

It is 7:20 on a Tuesday morning, and you are standing at the trade counter of the builders' merchant. The coffee in your hand is still too hot to drink.

The assistant scans the last item. Tile adhesive, grout, trim, waterproofing membrane, a box of silicone. The total appears on the little screen, turned slightly toward you.

You look at it. Then you look at it again.

It is higher than the number in your head. Not a little higher. The kind of higher where your stomach does a slow, quiet drop.

The bathroom job. The one you quoted last week, standing in the client's doorway, with a tape measure in one hand and a phone in the other. You gave them a figure that felt fair. It felt friendly. They said yes before you had finished the sentence.

Now you tap your card and say nothing. The assistant says, "Have a good one." You say, "You too," and it comes out a little flat.

What you do in the van

You sit in the van with the engine off. The receipt is in your hand, already curling.

You start doing sums in your head. Materials were meant to be this much. They are this much. The difference is not small. And that is before the second trip, because you already know you forgot something. You always forget something.

Then the thoughts come, one after the other.

Did you price the materials at all, or did you price the feeling of the job? Did you add the delivery? The blades, the screws, the dust sheets? The hour you will spend driving back for the thing you forgot?

You don't know. That is the uncomfortable part. You quoted from memory, from a job you did two years ago, and prices have moved since then. They always move.

You could ask the client for more. You imagine the message. You imagine the pause before they answer. You imagine them thinking so the quote wasn't really the quote.

So you don't send it. You put the receipt on the dashboard, start the engine, and decide to just get on with it.

The quiet cost

Here is what nobody says out loud in this trade.

It is rarely one big mistake. It is a lot of small ones, each too awkward to mention. A quote that was a bit tight. Materials that crept up. A weekend of unpaid extra work, swallowed because the client was nice and you didn't want to be difficult.

It adds up in places you don't look. In the job you finish proud but tired, with nothing left over. In the invoice you send a little late because you aren't quite sure what to put on it. In the glove box, where the receipts slowly turn into a drift of paper you will have to face in January.

And it costs something harder to count. It costs the feeling that you are running a real business. Because a real business knows what a job cost before it is over. You find out on Friday, when the money has already gone.

You are good at the work. The tiles are straight, the lines are clean, clients recommend you. The trouble was never the craft. It is that the numbers live in your head, and your head is also holding the schedule, the van, the phone, and what to cook tonight.

The Friday that felt different

Imagine a different version of that Tuesday.

You still walk into the merchant. The total on the screen is still a bit higher than you hoped, because materials do that. But this time you are not surprised in the way that makes your stomach drop. You know roughly what the job is carrying. You know where the margin sits. You tap the card, take the receipt, and it goes straight into the one place receipts go.

In the van, you don't do sums. You just drink your coffee, which has finally cooled.

On the next quote, on the next doorstep, something is different too. You don't give a number from memory. You give a number you have looked at: materials, your time, a little room for the thing you will forget. You say it calmly. The client nods. Maybe they ask a question, and you have an answer.

It isn't a grand change. It's a quieter one. A small, steady sheet that holds what you used to hold in your head, so your head can go back to the work.

By Friday, you are not guessing how the job went. You can see it. The money in, the money spent, what was left. And you can see who still owes you, without opening three different notes on your phone.

The weekend arrives, and it is actually the weekend.
