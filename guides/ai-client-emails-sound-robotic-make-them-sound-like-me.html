AI Client Emails Sound Robotic? The Greeting Card Aisle

2026-11-03

It's 10:40 on a Tuesday, and Jonas is standing in the greeting card aisle of a stationery shop, holding two birthday cards for his sister.

He's an illustrator, self-employed for four years. He isn't really here for the cards. He's here because his phone buzzed in his jacket pocket three minutes ago and he hasn't been able to put it away since.

The message is from a client, a small publisher he's worked with since spring. It says: "Hi Jonas, thanks for the update. Is everything okay? The email sounded a bit... different. Formal. Let me know if something's up!"

He reads it twice. Then he opens his sent folder and reads what he sent yesterday.

"I hope this message finds you well. I am writing to kindly follow up regarding the outstanding invoice. Please do not hesitate to reach out should you require any further clarification. I remain at your disposal."

He didn't write it. Not really. He typed three lines into an AI tool at 8:50 the previous morning, asked for "a polite payment reminder", and pasted the result without changing a word. It was fast. It was correct. It was also exactly how nobody he knows has ever spoken.

The client has called him "Jonny" since their second call.

The email that sounds like a stranger

He puts one card back and picks up another. The front has a hand-lettered line on it. It's a bit wobbly, and it makes him smile. Somebody meant it.

That's the thing he can't shake. The email wasn't rude, and it wasn't wrong. It was just nobody. A smooth, polite, empty voice, sitting in the inbox of someone he likes.

And he knows why he used it. Writing to clients about money makes his stomach tighten. Every time he sits down to write one, the same quiet fear shows up: Will I sound pushy? Needy? Like I don't trust them? So when a tool offered to take that feeling away, he let it.

But the feeling didn't go anywhere. It just moved. Now it was sitting in the mismatch between the Jonas the client knows and the Jonas who "remains at their disposal."

What it quietly costs

He thinks about the other emails he's let a machine word lately. The price rise note that read like a terms-and-conditions page. The "no" to a rush request that sounded so corporate the client had replied with a thumbs-up and nothing else.

None of those were disasters. That's why they're easy to miss. The cost is smaller and slower. Clients start to feel a little further away. Replies get shorter. And Jonas starts to feel, in a way he'd never say out loud, a bit like he's pretending to be a company.

He's not a company. He's one person with a drawing tablet and a good relationship with a publisher who just asked, kindly, if he's alright.

He buys the wobbly-lettering card and writes a message inside it in about ninety seconds. No drafts, no deleting. It sounds exactly like him.

Why is that so easy, he thinks, and the invoice email so hard?

The small turn

On the walk home, he works it out.

The card was easy because he already knew what he wanted to say and how he'd say it. The invoice email was hard because he'd handed the whole thing over, including the part that was his: the warmth, the way he gets to the point, the little joke he makes about deadlines.

The AI wasn't the problem. The empty brief was. "Write a polite reminder" gives a tool nothing to hold on to, so it hands back the average of every polite reminder ever written. Of course it sounded like a stranger.

What he wanted was to start from something that already sounded like him, and to tell the tool who the client was, how they talk, how much he wanted to say and how much to leave out. Then to read the result out loud and change two words, not twenty.

He wasn't looking for a trick. He just wanted a better starting point than a blank box and a tight stomach.

A different Monday

Picture it a few weeks later. Monday, 9:15. The coffee is still too hot to drink.

A payment is a week late. Jonas opens a note he's prepared for exactly this, fills in the client's name and the invoice number, and tells the tool how he usually sounds: friendly, brief, no grovelling. The draft comes back close. He changes one sentence, adds "Jonny-to-Jonas-style" warmth in the greeting, and reads it aloud once.

It sounds like him on a good day.

He sends it. The knot he used to carry for this kind of email doesn't show up. By 9:25 he's back at the tablet, drawing a fox for a children's book, and the money question is simply handled. The reply, when it comes that afternoon, starts with "Sorry, Jonas, this slipped past me!" and ends with a smiley face.

The invoice gets paid. More quietly, so does the relationship.
