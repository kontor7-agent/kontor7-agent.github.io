Gave Extra Work Free Mid-Job? Pricing Variations Calmly

2026-10-25

The ladder is still in the hallway when Customer Jo says it.

It is Wednesday, about 14:20. You are halfway through rewiring a small terraced house. Your arms are dusty, your phone is buzzing in your pocket, and the quote is folded in the van's door pocket.

"While you're up there," she says, "could you move that light over the stairs? And maybe put two sockets in the garage? Shouldn't take long."

You look at the ceiling. You look at her hopeful face. Somewhere behind your ribs a small voice says: that's not on the quote.

What comes out of your mouth is: "Yeah, no problem."

The sentence that costs you a Saturday

You don't mean it badly. She is friendly, she made you tea, and she has the look of someone who hates asking for things. Saying "that'll be extra" on a ladder, with her standing below, feels like slamming a door in a nice person's face.

So you smile and carry on.

The light takes forty minutes, because the cable run isn't where anyone hoped. The garage sockets take most of the next morning, plus a trip to the trade counter for a faceplate and a length of conduit you didn't have in the van. You pay for those yourself, at the till, with the queue behind you.

Nobody talks about this part of the job. It isn't the work that wears you down. It's the quiet arithmetic you do afterwards, alone.

Friday, the van, the engine off

Friday afternoon you sit outside her house with the laptop on the steering wheel. The job is done and the final invoice is open.

There's the original quote, line by line. And there's a blank space where the light and the sockets should be. Nothing says they happened. Nothing says they cost you a morning and a handful of receipts.

You think about adding a line now. Then you think about how it will look: Why are you only telling me this now? I thought that was included. You can already hear the pause on the phone.

So you do what many people who work for themselves do. You close the laptop, send the invoice exactly as quoted, and tell yourself it's goodwill. She'll recommend you, after all.

She might. But in the supermarket queue on Saturday, when the card machine takes a second too long, you feel it. A small, sour thought: I did a day's work for free, and I'm the one who's nervous.

Not a failure of nerve

Here is what's easy to miss. You didn't fail because you're bad at business or too soft for it.

You failed because the moment of the request has no place to land. On the ladder there is no number in your hand, no sheet to point to, no calm way to say "let me add that and tell you what it comes to." So the only options are free or awkward. Of course you pick free.

The awkwardness was never really about her. It was about having nothing in front of you except your own gut feeling and a half-remembered materials price.

The same hallway, a different Wednesday

Imagine the same moment, a few months on.

Jo says, "While you're up there, could you move that light? And maybe two sockets in the garage?"

You don't flinch. You say, "Happy to. Let me note that down as an extra and I'll send you the figure tonight, so you can say yes or no."

That's it. No speech, no apology, no sour feeling. The request becomes a small, separate item with its own line, its own materials, and its own number. She gets to decide. You get to be the person who runs a proper business and is still nice about it.

And when Friday comes, the invoice is simply the quote plus the things you both agreed along the way. You don't rehearse a phone call. You don't drive home with a knot in your stomach.

The extras are just part of the job, written down where both of you can see them.

What changes on an ordinary Monday

The change is small and a bit dull, which is the point.

On Monday morning you open one sheet and see the jobs you have running. Each one shows what you quoted, what the materials have actually cost so far, and what's been added since. You can see which jobs are earning what you hoped and which are quietly drifting. You can see who still owes you.

Nothing about it is clever. It just means that "can you also..." stops being a trap and turns into the next line on a page.

Most customers, in our experience of how people tend to behave, don't mind paying for extra work. What they mind is surprises. A clear figure, sent promptly and kindly, is usually far easier to hear than silence followed by a bigger bill.
