Rate Still Too Low After Tax and Holidays? The Kayak Hut

2026-10-22

It is Thursday, 11:20 in the morning, and Mara is standing at a wooden hatch beside a lake, holding a laminated price list.

Kayak, one hour: 14 euros. Two hours: 24.

She is on holiday. Properly, for once. Four days in a rented room above a bakery, phone face-down in her bag, laptop left at home on purpose.

And she is doing sums.

Not about the kayak. About herself.

The hour that didn't add up

Mara has been a freelance illustrator for four years. Her rate is the one she picked in her second month, after looking at what two other illustrators charged and subtracting a little "to be safe." Clients have never complained. That used to feel like proof it was fine.

But the hatch has a queue, and queues give you time to think. The man in front of her is paying cash, slowly, and her mind goes where it always goes when nothing is asking for her attention.

This week, she is not earning. Nobody pays an illustrator for lying in a hammock. The invoices stop, but the health insurance doesn't. The software subscriptions don't. The tax set-aside she keeps promising to deal with doesn't.

She thinks of the last month. She worked a lot. The bank balance on the first of the month looked nothing like the hours she remembers. Somewhere between the invoice amount and the money she can actually spend, a lot goes missing. She has never looked at exactly where.

So the hour at the kayak hut costs 14 euros, and what she feels is a small, hot flicker of guilt.

That is the part nobody says out loud. Not that you can't afford the holiday. It's that you can't tell. So the holiday becomes a quiet loan from your future self, and you spend the whole time half-listening for the bill.

What it was really costing her

The man in front of her finishes counting his coins. The hatch attendant looks up.

"One hour?" he asks Mara.

"Two," she says, and then, quickly, "Actually, one."

One hour. On a day she planned to do nothing.

She takes the paddle and walks down the wooden jetty, and the thought arrives, not as panic but as something clearer. She has been treating her rate like a mood. Something she feels brave or timid about, depending on the week. She has never treated it like a fact: what has to come in per hour so that the weeks off, the tax, the slow months and the unpaid admin are already inside the price.

If they aren't inside the price, they come out of her. Out of her evenings, out of her sleep, out of days like this one.

The water is flat and green. A duck ignores her completely.

She thinks: I don't need to work more. I need to know my number.

The number she had been avoiding

She had always put it off because it felt like homework. A big, grey, adult exercise involving a spreadsheet and a bad mood. Something to do "when things calm down," which for a freelancer is a day that never turns up on the calendar.

But out on the lake, with nothing to open and nothing to answer, it looks different. Smaller. Not a financial exam, just a few honest questions. How many hours can I realistically bill in a week? How many weeks do I actually want off? What do I need to keep after the taxman and the insurance have had their share?

She doesn't know the answers yet. But for the first time she knows they are findable. They are numbers, not verdicts on her worth.

And something in her shoulders lets go.

She paddles to the far reeds, lets the kayak drift, and for forty minutes she simply enjoys the holiday. Not because the question is solved, but because she has stopped pretending it isn't there.

A different Monday

Picture the Monday after she gets back.

She makes coffee. She opens one file, spends ten quiet minutes with it, and sees a minimum hourly rate and a minimum day rate sitting there in plain figures. No drama. Just a floor to stand on.

The next time a client asks for a quote, she doesn't shave a little off "to be safe." She says her price in an even voice, because it is no longer a guess. It is the amount that lets her take a Thursday at a kayak hut without doing sums in the queue.

She may still be nervous the first time. That's normal. But the nervousness has something solid under it now.

And when the invoice goes out, it looks clean and professional, with the total, the tax line and the due date in the right places. It looks like a real business, because it is one.

Nothing about her work has changed. She still draws the same way. She just stopped quietly paying for her own time off.

If you're the one at the hatch

Maybe you recognise the flicker. You charge what feels okay, the money arrives, and still, after tax, after the weeks you didn't work, after the admin nobody pays for, it feels a little too thin. Not a disaster. Just a low hum of "something is off."

That hum usually isn't about your talent. It's about a number you haven't had a calm look at yet.
