Client Paid Late Again? The Ice-Cream Van Bell

2026-10-21

The jingle and the notification

It's a Saturday in late October, unseasonably warm, and you are standing at the edge of a playground with a paper cup of coffee going lukewarm in your hand.

Across the grass, an ice-cream van plays its tinny jingle. Your nephew is on the climbing frame, shouting "Watch me!" You are watching. Mostly.

Because your phone buzzed a minute ago, and it wasn't the bank. It was your own calendar, reminding you of something you already know: the invoice for the brand-guidelines project was due 19 days ago.

This is the same client. The same thing happened in the spring. And in January, if you're honest.

You wave at the climbing frame. You smile. Inside, a small, familiar knot tightens.

The email you keep not writing

You have thought about this email so many times that you could recite it. You just can't send it.

Hi Anna, just a quick note to check whether you've had a chance to...

Too soft. She'll skim it.

As per our agreed payment terms, the invoice is now overdue...

Too cold. She's been lovely to work with. She sends you coffee emojis. She said your last logo was "exactly what we dreamed of."

And that is the strange part. Nobody has been rude. Nobody has refused to pay. The money is simply late, again, and nobody mentions it, and the longer nobody mentions it, the more it feels like you are the one doing something awkward by noticing.

So you do the thing you always do. You wait one more day. Then another. You tell yourself it's probably just their accounts department. You tell yourself you don't want to damage the relationship.

Meanwhile your own rent doesn't wait. Your own quarterly bills don't wait. You've started checking your balance in the supermarket queue, the way other people check the weather.

What lateness quietly costs

The money is only part of it.

There is the low hum in the background of your weekend. The way you glance at your phone at the playground when you meant to be present. The way you've begun to take on one more small job than you can really handle, just to cushion the gap.

There is the little voice that says: A real business wouldn't have this problem. Other freelancers just ask for their money and move on. Maybe I'm bad at this.

You're not bad at this. You're good at the work. You're simply doing the hardest part, the awkward, personal part of getting paid, with no script, no rhythm, and no one to ask. And you're doing it from memory, in your head, every time as if it were the first time.

That's why it feels like begging. Not because asking is begging, but because every ask is improvised, emotional, and rewritten from scratch while your pulse goes up.

The bench conversation

Your sister arrives with two ice creams, a vanilla for her son and a lemon sorbet for you, and sits down on the bench beside you.

"You've got your work face on," she says.

You tell her. Not the whole thing, just the headline: the client paid late again, and you don't know how to bring it up.

She works in a hospital pharmacy, where nothing is allowed to be vague. She shrugs.

"Do they know what day it's due?"

"It's on the invoice."

"No, I mean do they know. Like, does a reminder just land, the way a reminder lands for everything else in their week?"

You open your mouth, then close it.

It had never occurred to you that a polite, predictable nudge isn't an accusation. That in most offices it is simply part of how invoices move: a gentle note before the due date, a friendly one just after, a clearer one later. Not a confrontation. A rhythm.

The awkwardness wasn't in the asking. It was in having no plan for the asking.

A different kind of Monday

Picture it instead.

It's Monday, 9:10. You open one sheet. Not seven tabs, not your inbox archaeology. One overview, with a short list of what's open and what's late and a plain note beside each line telling you what comes next: friendly nudge today, or wait until Thursday, or clearer follow-up.

The wording is already there, calm and kind, in your voice and ready to adjust. You change a name, add a sentence about the project, and send it. It takes about four minutes.

Then you make a proper coffee, because nothing is churning.

When the payment does land, you note it and the line quietly clears. When it doesn't, you already know the next gentle step, so there is nothing to dread, nothing to rewrite nine times, and no verdict on whether you're "the kind of person who chases people."

You are simply someone who runs a business with a rhythm. Your clients feel it too. Late payers often pay on time once the reminders are steady, friendly and never personal. That isn't a promise about any particular client, just what a calm, consistent process makes easier.

What you'd do with the hour

The ice-cream van pulls away, jingle fading down the road. Your nephew has moved on to the slide.

Somewhere in this scene, you've put the phone in your pocket. Not because the invoice is paid, but because you know that on Monday there's a plan for it. It's small, and it's yours.

"Watch me!" he shouts again.

This time you really are.
