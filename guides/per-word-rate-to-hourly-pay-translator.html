Per-Word Rate to Hourly Pay? The Saturday Rush Job Call

2026-10-14

The phone buzzes on the windowsill at 10:40 on a Saturday morning. You know the name before you pick up. It is the project manager from the agency you have worked with for three years.

She is friendly, as always. A rush job: 6,200 words, a technical manual, back by Monday at nine. The client is in a hurry. The rate is "a little special, since it's short notice".

You hear yourself say, "Let me check my schedule."

You have no schedule to check. What you actually need to check is something else, and you cannot name it. Is this a good rate? Is it a rate at all?

The number that means nothing

Per-word rates sound clean. Eleven cents. Twelve. They sit in an email like a fact.

But you have been doing this long enough to know a per-word rate is not what you earn. It is only what you are offered. What you earn is hiding somewhere behind it: the hours the job really takes, the repetitions the CAT tool will "discount", the terminology you have to look up because the source text is badly written, the Sunday you will not get back.

You say you will reply within the hour. You hang up and look at the garden. The neighbour is hanging washing. Somewhere in a cupboard, a mug of tea has gone cold.

You think: I said yes to the last rush job too. Did it pay?

You honestly don't know.

What you carry in your head

This is the part nobody sees. You carry a lot in your head.

There is the agency that sends lovely work but always seems to pay at day 60, sometimes day 75. You think it was the one in spring. Or was that the other one? There is the fuzzy-match discount that one client applies and another doesn't. There is a rush fee you once asked for and once forgot to ask for. There is a notebook, a folder of PDF invoices, and a spreadsheet you started in January and stopped trusting in March.

When someone asks, "What's your rate?" you answer with a per-word figure and a small knot in your stomach. Because the real answer would be: "I think it's fine. I hope it's fine."

That is a strange thing to feel after years of working in two languages with precision. You can find the one right word in a sentence. But you cannot find out whether last month paid you what you thought.

The reply you almost send

You open your laptop at the kitchen table. You type: Happy to help, I can do it by Monday.

Your finger hovers.

It is not that you want to say no. You like this agency. You like the work. It is that saying yes without knowing feels a little like signing a cheque with your eyes closed. And saying no without knowing feels like throwing away money you might have needed.

So you do what many of us do. You say yes, quietly cross out the weekend in your head, and tell yourself you will work out the maths later.

Later never comes. Later is the invoice sent in a rush, the payment that doesn't arrive, the "I'll check on it" that you never quite do.

A different Saturday

Here is how it could look.

The phone buzzes. The same project manager, the same 6,200 words, the same Monday deadline. You pick up, and you are calm.

Because you already know what a normal hour of your work earns. Not roughly. Actually. You know what your last five jobs with this agency came to once the discounts and the research time were counted in. You know whether a rush fee is something you need to ask for here, or whether they have always paid it without fuss. And you know, without scrolling through your inbox, that this agency's last invoice is still open, and has been for forty-one days.

So you say: "I can do Monday. For this one I'll need a rush rate, and I'd like the open invoice settled before I start."

Not sharp. Not apologetic. Just a professional answer from someone who has the picture in front of her.

She says, "Of course, let me check."

You put the phone down and finish the tea. It is still a little warm. You go out and help the neighbour with the washing line, because her pegs keep falling.

What actually changes

Nothing about your work changes. The translations are as good as ever. What changes is that the numbers stop living in your head, scattered across notebooks and old emails, and start living in one calm place where you can look at them.

You would still decide everything yourself. The rates, the yeses, the noes. It simply gives you the facts to decide with.
