January Freelance Goals, Never Followed? The Drawer

2026-11-09

It is a grey Thursday in November, a little after eleven. Elif is looking for a phone charger.

She is an illustrator. She works from a spare room with a window onto the neighbour's bins. The charger is not in the cable basket, not in the bag, not behind the monitor. So she opens the desk drawer, the one that sticks, and digs through rubber bands, dried-out pens and a tape measure.

Under all of it lies a notebook. The cover is dark green and still looks new.

She knows what is inside before she opens it.

The page she wrote in January

The date at the top is 2 January. The handwriting is neat, the way handwriting is when you believe in something.

Three retainer clients. Raise my rates. Five weeks off, properly off. A buffer in savings. Finish the portfolio. Be home by six.

She remembers writing it. Snow was melting off the window ledge. She had a good coffee, a clean desk, and a feeling in her chest like the first page of a sketchbook. This year, she thought, I'll actually do it.

Now she stands in the doorway of her own room, holding the notebook, and works out how far she got.

One retainer client, not three. The rates are the same as last year. The five weeks off turned into four days in August, and she answered emails on two of them. The portfolio is still a folder called "new_final_2". Most evenings she was not home by six, because she did not know where "home" ended and the spare room began.

The worst part is not the list. It is the small, familiar flinch. Again.

Why it fades by March

Nobody warns you that a goal written in January is a letter to a person who has no calendar.

It sounds big and clear. Then February arrives with a deadline, a client who pays late, a cold that lasts nine days. The list stays in the drawer. The work stays on the screen. Nothing ever connects the two.

So each week you make small decisions without the big picture in front of you. A discount here. A yes to a Friday-night request there. A quiet month, and a quiet panic. None of these choices is wrong on its own. You just never see them add up.

At some point in spring you stop thinking "I'm working toward something" and start thinking "I'm getting through the week." The notebook goes into the drawer. It isn't failure exactly. It is more like forgetting to look up.

Elif sits down on the edge of the daybed and reads the page twice. She does not feel lazy. She feels something quieter and stranger: I had good ideas. I just had nowhere to put them.

The thought that turns it

There is a moment, and it is smaller than you would think, when you stop asking "why can't I stick to my goals?" and start asking a different question.

What if the goals were never the problem? What if they just had nowhere to live?

A sentence in a notebook cannot tell you that you are three weeks behind on the income you wanted. It cannot show you that the August you hoped for does not fit between two big projects. It cannot tell you, kindly, in March, that you can still catch this.

A plan that sits next to your real work is a different thing. It is a place where the year is laid out in front of you, quarter by quarter. You can see how much you hoped to earn and how much you have earned. You can see which weeks are already spoken for and which ones are free. It lets you look at a Tuesday in spring and see where it sits in the whole year, instead of treating it as one more thing to survive.

That is all. Not more discipline. Just a map that stays open.

A different kind of Monday

Picture it, a year from now.

It is a Monday in late March. Elif makes tea and sits down with the window open a crack. She spends ten minutes looking at the year: where the money stands, who is likely to book next, what is already blocked for summer. One number is a little behind. She does not panic. She sees it in March, not in November, and there is still plenty of room to move.

She notices that the two weeks off in August are still there, marked, not negotiable. When a client asks about those dates in June, she does not squirm. She already knows her answer.

At the end of the quarter she takes half an hour to look back. What worked? What did she overestimate? What does the next quarter need? It feels less like a test and more like a conversation with someone who is on her side.

At six, she closes the laptop. Not because she is being strict with herself. It is simply that nothing is hanging over the evening.

Back at the drawer

Elif finds the charger eventually. It was in her coat pocket the whole time.

She puts the green notebook on the desk, not back in the drawer. She does not tear out the page. It is not a record of failure. It is a record of what she wanted, and what she wants is still worth wanting.

Only this time, she thinks, it will have somewhere to go.
