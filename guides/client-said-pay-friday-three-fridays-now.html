Client Promised to Pay Friday, Three Fridays Ago?

2026-10-16

It is 12:40 on a Friday, and Mara is standing in the queue at the cheese stall on the market square.

She is not looking at the cheese. She is looking at her phone, thumb hovering over the banking app. She opens it. She closes it. She opens it again, as if the number might have changed in the eleven seconds since the last look.

Nothing. The balance is the same as it was this morning.

Three Fridays ago, her client wrote: "Sorry, it's on my list, will pay Friday!" Two Fridays ago: "Finance had a hiccup, should go out Friday." Last Friday there was no email at all, which was somehow worse.

Mara edits video for small companies. She did this job well. The client even said so. And now she is queueing for 200 grams of Gouda and doing rent maths in her head.

The reply you could write, and don't

"Can I have the Comté?" the woman behind her asks gently. Mara steps forward. She hadn't noticed the gap.

She buys the cheese. She walks to the bench by the fountain and opens her laptop on her knees, because the email has been sitting in her drafts since Wednesday.

She reads it. Hi Jonas, just a friendly reminder... Too soft. She deletes "friendly." Now it sounds cold. She puts it back. She adds "I hope you're well," then removes it because she is not sure she does.

This is the part nobody warns you about when you start working for yourself. The work is the easy bit. The hard bit is the small, cringing voice that says: if I push, he'll be annoyed. If he's annoyed, he won't hire me again. If he doesn't hire me again, that's a slow month. And one slow month is all it takes.

So you wait. You are polite. You check the app.

What the waiting quietly costs

It is never only the money. Mara could probably get through the month without this invoice, if she were careful.

What it costs is the low hum underneath everything. It sits in the back of her head on Saturday morning. It follows her into Sunday dinner at her sister's, where she nods along to a story and realises she has missed the ending. It shows up at 3 am as a small, precise thought: you don't even know exactly how late it is.

Because that's the thing. She isn't sure. Was the due date the 14th or the 21st? Did she send the first reminder, or only think about sending it? Was it the invoice from the spring project that also hadn't been paid, or has that one come in?

She has the answers somewhere. In a sent folder, a notes app, the back of her head. Chasing money feels like begging partly because she is begging with no facts in her hands. Just a feeling and a draft.

She also notices something she would never say out loud: she is starting to feel a little foolish. Like a professional would have a system for this. Like "a real business" would not be sitting on a bench with a block of Comté, rehearsing a sentence.

The turn, on a bench by a fountain

An older man sits at the other end of the bench with a paper bag of rolls. He is a retired bookbinder, as it turns out. They get talking, the way strangers do when one of them is clearly staring too hard at a screen.

"Looks like a difficult letter," he says.

"It's an invoice," says Mara. "Sort of. Reminder number... I'm not sure what number."

He laughs, kindly. "When I took on commissions, I had a card for each job. Pencil. Date sent, date due, what I'd said, what came next. Nothing clever. But I never had to wonder whether I was being rude. The card said: it's the third week, this is the next step. So I took it."

He bites his roll.

"It stopped being about me. It was just the next line on the card."

Mara sits with that for a moment, while the fountain does its small, steady thing.

It doesn't have to be a mood. It doesn't have to be a courage contest she runs against herself every Friday. It could simply be a sequence: a calm first nudge, a clearer second, a firm and polite third, each already worded, each tied to a date she can see. A line you follow, not a speech you invent.

What a different Friday feels like

Imagine the same Friday, one version later.

Mara is at the cheese stall again. Her phone is in her bag. She knows, without looking, that one invoice is at step two and will move to step three on Tuesday if nothing arrives. She knows who owes her what, because it sits in one place, and the place tells her what to do next.

On Tuesday, she copies a message that already sounds like her on a good day: warm, clear, no apologies stuffed into it. She changes the name and the date. She sends it before her coffee cools.

She doesn't feel brave. She feels ordinary, in the best way. Like someone who runs a business and handles it the way businesses do.

And on Saturday morning, the hum is quieter. Not gone, because clients are still people and people are still late. But it has edges now. It has a next step. She can leave it on the desk and walk to the market with her hands free.

That is the change worth wanting. Not a harder tone, not a lawyer's voice. Just the removal of the guesswork, so the part of you that is good at your actual work gets its evening back.
