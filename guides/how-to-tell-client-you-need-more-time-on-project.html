Need More Time on a Client Project? The Bank Holiday Kitchen Table

2026-10-15

It is Saturday of a long weekend, 11:20 in the morning, and you are at your parents' kitchen table with your laptop balanced between the fruit bowl and the radio.

The radio is playing something cheerful. Your mother is peeling potatoes behind you. Someone has asked if you want another coffee, and you said yes without hearing the question.

On your screen, two windows. On the left, the project: a video edit that was supposed to be finished by Friday. It is not going to be finished by Friday. On the right, an email with one line typed so far.

Hi Jonas, quick update on the timeline…

That's it. That's all you have. The cursor blinks after the word timeline like it's waiting for you to be brave.

The sentence you keep rewriting

You know exactly what the problem is. You are not lazy. The client sent feedback late, then sent more feedback, and the second round contradicted the first. You made it work. You are good at making it work. But the honest picture is simple: you need three more working days, and the deadline gives you one.

So you try version two.

Unfortunately, I'm running a little behind.

Delete. Behind sounds like you can't cope.

Version three: Due to unforeseen circumstances… Delete. Nobody died. It sounds like an airline.

Version four is a long, apologetic paragraph that explains everything, including the feedback, your week, and the flu you had in September. You read it back and it sounds like a person asking for forgiveness. You delete it with a small, tired sigh.

"Everything alright?" your mother asks, without turning around.

"Work," you say. "Just a client email."

"On a holiday weekend?"

You laugh, a bit too quickly. This is the part nobody puts in the brochure about working for yourself. The weekend is not really a weekend. It is a weekend-shaped space where you quietly carry the unsent email around with you.

What it quietly costs

Here is what the email is really costing you, and none of it is on an invoice.

It costs the lunch you are only half at. You nod at your brother's story about the new flat and your mind is on the word unfortunately.

It costs the work itself. You've been thinking about the sentence rather than the edit, so the edit has moved forward by about nine minutes since breakfast.

And it costs a little of the feeling that you run a real business. Real businesses, you think, have project managers who say "we're adjusting the schedule" in a calm voice. You have a kitchen table and a draft folder.

The oddest part is that you have given other people good advice about this. You once told a friend that a clear heads-up is kinder than silence. You believe it completely. It just isn't your own voice you hear when it's your own deadline.

The moment it shifts

Around noon, you stop typing and look at the draft the way you'd look at someone else's.

What would you want to receive, if you were Jonas?

Probably not an apology. Probably not an essay. You would want three things: that the project is in good hands, the new date, and no surprises. You would want the sort of message that makes you think, good, they have it under control.

And that is the thing you hadn't quite let yourself see. Asking for more time is not the unprofessional part. Disappearing into a fog of apology is. A short, steady note, sent early, with a real date in it, is what professionals do. The wording just has to come out of you in a calm voice, and on a Saturday with potatoes being peeled behind you, calm is hard to find on your own.

So you try something different. Instead of staring at a blank line, you tell yourself what you actually mean, plainly. The feedback changed halfway. I need until Wednesday. The result will be better for it. Then you ask for help turning that into something warm and short, with a tone that sounds like you on your best day rather than you on your worst.

The draft that comes back is four sentences. It names the new date. It says thank you for the detailed feedback. It does not say sorry eleven times.

You change two words so it sounds like your voice, and you press send.

The rest of the weekend

Nothing explodes. That's what you notice first.

The radio is still playing. The potatoes are still being peeled. Your phone lies face-down next to the fruit bowl, and for the first time since Thursday you let it lie there.

Jonas replies within the hour: Thanks for the heads-up, Wednesday works. Three words of ordinary human kindness, and the knot you've been carrying under your ribs since Thursday loosens.

You realise the email never needed to be perfect. It needed to exist.

The afternoon is different. You carve out two focused hours on the edit, the kind that only happen when your head is quiet. Then you close the laptop and go for a walk with your brother, and you hear the whole story about the flat.

On Monday morning, back in your own kitchen, the project is a little ahead of where you feared it would be. The inbox holds no dread. Your coffee is just coffee.
