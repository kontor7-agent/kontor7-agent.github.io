Worried Your Password Reset Is Insecure? The Food Truck

2026-11-07

It's 12:40 on a Thursday, and you're fourth in line at the food truck by the canal.

The menu is chalked on a board. You've read it three times and taken in none of it. Your phone is in your hand, and on the screen is the email you sent yourself an hour ago: Reset your password. One button, one link.

The app goes live on Monday. A small booking tool for a physio studio, built mostly by you, over a lot of evenings.

Everything works. You've clicked through it forty times. But the reset email is the part you keep coming back to.

The thought that follows you into the queue

It isn't a big, dramatic fear. It's a small, sticky one.

What if that link works twice? What if it never runs out? What if someone who isn't the owner of that inbox can do something with it?

You wrote the reset flow on a Tuesday afternoon, quickly, because it felt like plumbing. Plumbing isn't the exciting part of an app. You copied a pattern you half remembered, tested the happy path, and moved on to the calendar view.

Now the calendar view is done and the plumbing is all you can think about.

The person in front of you orders two portions of dumplings. You step forward without noticing.

What the worry quietly costs

Nobody sees this part of the job.

You don't tell the studio owner, because she'd only hear "maybe unsafe" and not the "probably fine, I just haven't looked properly" that lives in your head. She's already nervous about moving her whole diary onto a screen.

You don't tell your friends either. They'd say, "Just launch, you can fix it later." That's kind advice, but it doesn't touch the knot.

So you carry it. You eat your lunch on a bench and don't taste it. You open the code on your phone, squint at a function name, and close it again. Over the weekend you'll check the same thing in your head a dozen times, while a perfectly good Saturday goes by.

The real cost isn't the risk itself. It's that you can't enjoy the thing you made. A launch should feel like opening a shop door. Yours feels like holding your breath.

The question you've been asking wrongly

On the bench, a small thing shifts.

You realise you've been asking, "Is my reset flow secure?" That's a question with no edges. You can't finish it, so you can't stop worrying about it.

What you actually need is a different kind of question: Have I looked at this the way a careful stranger would, with a list, once, from start to finish?

That one has an end. You can tick it off.

It's the same reason a pilot walks around the plane with a checklist. Pilots aren't nervous or careless. They just don't trust a feeling when they can trust a page.

You don't need to become a security expert by Monday. You need to stop relying on a memory of something you wrote in a hurry. You need a calm, finite pass over the door you're about to open to strangers, with the odd uncertain spot written down and given a name.

A different Monday

Picture it for a moment.

It's Monday, 8:50. The studio opens at nine. You've got a coffee, and the tab with the app is open, but you're not staring at it with your shoulders up around your ears.

You already did the review. Not in a panic, but over a quiet hour on Friday. Things that were fine are marked fine. The two things that weren't are fixed, and the one open question is written down in plain words, ready for when you have time.

The first real user clicks Forgot password. An email arrives, a link works the way it should, and the studio owner types a new password and sees her diary appear.

You watch none of it. You're buttering toast.

That's the difference. The reset flow is still plumbing, but now it's plumbing you've looked at. When the phone buzzes, it's a client saying thank you, not a stranger's name in your head.

Back in the queue

The dumplings arrive in a cardboard tray. The cook asks if you want chilli oil and you say yes, a bit surprised to hear yourself.

You still haven't solved anything. But you've turned a fog into a question with an end, and that's most of the relief right there. You know now that you don't have to feel confident. You only have to have looked.
