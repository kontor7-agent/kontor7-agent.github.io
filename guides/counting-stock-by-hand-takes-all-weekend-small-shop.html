Counting Stock by Hand All Weekend? The Clipboard at 3 pm

2026-11-08

It is ten past three on a Saturday. Your phone lights up on top of a box of notebooks. A photo from your friend Lena: a lake, two paddleboards, one empty spot on the jetty.

You type Next time, promise, and put the phone face down.

The clipboard is already soft at the corners. Row by row you count. Forty-one of the green ones. Twelve of the grey. Then you lose your place because the courier rings the bell, and you go back to the top of the shelf.

This is the weekend count. It started Friday after you closed the shop. It will probably finish Sunday, just before dinner.

The kind of tired that doesn't show

Imagine Ines. She is made up, but you may know her. She sells hand-dyed notebooks and fountain pens from a rented unit and a small online shop. She is good at it. Customers write to say the colours are exactly right.

But every second weekend, the shop swallows her whole.

The spreadsheet she started two years ago stopped keeping up around the time the shop did. It has three tabs, two of them half abandoned. One column says "approx." and nobody remembers who wrote it. So she counts by hand, because by hand she at least trusts the number.

Then she types it in. By Sunday evening the sheet is right for about a day and a half.

What the count really costs

It is not just the hours. You know the hours.

It is the lake you didn't go to. It is your partner's careful "No rush" from the doorway, which means there is a rush. It is the Sunday-night feeling that you spent two days looking backwards and still don't know what to order on Monday.

And under that sits the worry nobody says out loud. What if I only find out something is gone once a customer has already paid for it?

The count can't stop that. By the time you've counted, it's already old. Tuesday someone buys the last grey notebook, and your tidy number is quietly wrong again.

So the weekend work is oddly useless and strangely necessary at once. That is a heavy thing to carry. It makes you feel less like an owner and more like the person who counts the shelves.

The sentence that stopped the clipboard

It didn't come from a guru or an app store. It came from the courier, of all people.

He was standing in the doorway on Saturday, waiting for Ines to sign, and he glanced at the shelves. "You count all this yourself? Every time?"

"Every time," she said.

"Huh," he said. "The garage I deliver to just knows when they're low. Nobody counts anything. They get a little flag."

He left. She stood there with the pen in her hand.

A flag. Not a weekend. A flag.

The idea wasn't clever. It was almost embarrassing in its simplicity: what if you didn't count everything to find out what's running out? What if each sale and each delivery simply landed in one place, and the place told you? The thing she'd been doing out of loyalty to accuracy could be a small habit of a few minutes instead.

A normal Monday, afterwards

Picture it for a moment.

Monday, 8:40. Coffee. You open one file. It looks the way you'd want a shop to look when someone else is running it: calm, a few colours, no clutter. A short list sits at the top, the things getting low, with the ones that matter most first. Green notebooks, grey ink, the cardboard mailers.

You don't count anything. You don't go to the stockroom to check. You send one message to your supplier, and it takes less time than the coffee.

The shelves still exist, of course. Things still sell, and sometimes sell faster than you hoped. But you stop being surprised by them. You find out on Monday, not on the day someone's order can't ship.

And Saturday at ten past three? You're on the jetty. The phone is in a bag. Lena is losing a paddleboard race on purpose so you can win.

Nothing about the business got bigger. It just stopped sitting on top of your weekend.
