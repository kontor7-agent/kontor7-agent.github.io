Client Email Takes an Hour? The Microwave Beep at 12:40

2026-11-08

The microwave beeps at 12:40. You don't move.

The soup inside has been ready for a while. On your screen is an email that started at 11:35. It has four sentences. You have deleted it, rewritten it and pasted it into a notes file "just in case". Now you are reading it out loud, quietly, like a stranger would.

It sounds either cold or desperate. Never quite right.

Let's call you Dana. You're an illustrator, and this is an illustrative scene, not a real person. The client has asked for a third round of changes. The contract mentions two. You only want to say that, kindly, without starting a fight.

The hour nobody sees

Here is what that hour looks like from outside: nothing. No invoice sent, no drawing finished. A woman standing in her kitchen with a cursor blinking.

Here is what it looks like from inside.

"Per my last email" sounds snippy. Delete.

"Just checking in!" sounds like you're apologising for existing. Delete.

"I'd be happy to" – but you wouldn't be. Delete.

Every word carries a small bet. If this sounds too firm, they might leave. If it sounds too soft, they'll ask for a fourth round. You are writing, editing and also guessing how a person you've never seen across a table will feel in four hours.

It's tiring in a specific way. Not the tiredness of drawing. The tiredness of being careful.

What it quietly costs

The soup goes cold. That's the small cost.

The bigger one is that one email swallows the good part of the day. You meant to start the new project after lunch. Instead you spend the afternoon half-drawing, half-composing replies in your head. At six, the email is sent and you still don't feel finished.

And there is the thing you rarely say out loud: you start to dread the inbox. A message from a client lands and your stomach tightens before you've read it. You postpone replying until "you have the energy". Clients notice the silence. Some read it as unprofessional, even though you are one of the most careful people they work with.

It isn't a writing problem. You write well. It's that every email is a negotiation, and you're doing it from scratch, alone, every time.

The moment it shifts

A friend, a freelance translator, is on the phone while you wait for the kettle. She mentions she answered a tricky client message in ten minutes that morning.

"Ten minutes?" you say. "It took me an hour yesterday just to ask for a payment date."

She laughs, not unkindly. "I stopped starting from a blank page," she says. "I start from something that's already roughly right, and I make it sound like me."

That's all. No grand system. Just: the first draft doesn't have to come from you at your most anxious.

You think about it for the rest of the day. Not the tool, not the technology. The idea that the hard part, finding a first calm sentence, could already be done. That your job could be to read, adjust and decide, instead of invent, doubt and delete.

An ordinary Wednesday, later

Picture a different 12:40.

The client writes: could you just squeeze in one more small change? Your stomach does its usual small flip. Then it settles, because you know you aren't about to face a blank page.

You have a rough, friendly, clear version in front of you in under a minute. You swap one phrase for the way you'd actually say it. You add the client's name and the detail about the second round. You read it once, out loud, and it sounds like you on a good day. Firm, warm, done.

You press send.

The microwave beeps. The soup is still hot. You eat it at the window, watching a neighbour wrestle a ladder into a car, and you notice you aren't composing anything in your head.

The rest of the afternoon is yours. You draw. At six you close the laptop and it really is closed.

Nothing about your business changed overnight. You still have the same clients, the same rates, the same occasional awkward ask. What changed is that awkward no longer costs you an hour and half a day's mood.
