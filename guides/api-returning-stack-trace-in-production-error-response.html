API Returning Stack Traces in Production? The Kiosk Queue

2026-11-01

It is 8:50 on a Monday, and you are third in line at the station kiosk. You are waiting for a coffee and a bar of chocolate you don't need.

Your phone buzzes. It's Marta, the client with the booking app you built in the spring.

"Hi! My brother-in-law was testing the site and got this weird page. Is it supposed to look like that? Screenshot attached."

You open the image. It isn't a weird page. It's your API, answering a badly formed request with a wall of text.

There's an error message, then a long stack trace. You see file paths with your own folder names in them. You see the name of the database library, the line number, and a fragment of a query.

The man in front of you orders a pretzel. You stop hearing him.

The thought that arrives before the coffee

Am I exposing too much?

The question comes with a second one right behind it: how long has it looked like this?

You know exactly why it happened. During development, verbose errors were a gift. You read them, fixed the thing, and moved on. Somewhere between "works on my machine" and "live," nobody asked what a stranger would see.

Nobody did anything reckless. You were careful about a hundred other things. This one slipped through because it only shows itself when something goes wrong, and by then you aren't watching.

Still, your stomach has done that small drop, like a lift starting early.

What the quiet worry costs

The barista calls your name. You take the cup, but the coffee tastes of nothing.

On the platform, you start the arithmetic nobody has asked you to do. If one error page leaks this much, what about the other routes? Does the login endpoint answer differently for a missing user than for a wrong password? Did you ever check what a 404 says? What does the API do when someone sends an empty body, a giant body, or the wrong type?

That's the real cost. The screenshot itself is small. What hurts is the long list of I'm not sure behind it.

You think about the reply to Marta. You want to say "It's fine, just a test page." But you don't know that, and you won't say something you can't stand behind.

So you type, delete, and type again. The train arrives and you board with the draft still open.

The part where it stops being a fog

Halfway through the journey, with your forehead near the cold window, a different thought turns up.

This isn't a mystery. It's a checkable thing.

Other people have walked this road. An error response either tells the outside world what it needs to know, or it tells them what's inside the kitchen. That's a yes or no. The same goes for the next route, and the next. A question that felt like a fog is really a list of small, answerable questions, and you've simply never had the list in front of you.

You don't need to be a security expert. You need a calm, complete way to walk through the whole thing once, write down what you found, and fix what needs fixing. Then you need to be able to say to a client: "I reviewed it. Here's what I checked."

The knot loosens a little. It doesn't disappear, but you can breathe around it.

Replying to Marta

You send a short, honest message: "Thanks for flagging this. That's an error page that shows more detail than it should. I'm looking at it today and I'll confirm when it's sorted."

No panic and no excuses. Marta replies with a thumbs-up and a photo of her cat.

That tiny exchange teaches you something. Clients rarely need you to be perfect. They need you to be the person who noticed, took it seriously and came back with an answer.

A different Monday

Picture the same morning a few weeks later.

You're in the same queue and the coffee tastes like coffee. A message pings, and it's another small oddity from another client. You read it, and your stomach stays where it is.

You already know what the API says when something goes wrong. You've seen it, and you've tested it on purpose. Errors say "something went wrong" to the outside world and keep the details in your own logs, where they belong. There's a record of what you checked and when.

When a client asks, "Is it secure?", you don't flinch. You say, "Here's what I reviewed, and here's what I fixed." You can even say what you'd look at next.

You leave the station and walk to your desk with your hands free. Nobody is waiting for you to be found out.
