Can't Word the Client Email? The Café Table and the Blinking Cursor

2026-10-13

It is 2:15 on a Wednesday afternoon. You are at the café table by the window, the one you take when the home office feels too quiet.

Your cappuccino went cold a while ago. On the screen, a reply to a client. One line so far: Hi Jonas, hope you're well!

Then nothing.

The cursor blinks. You know exactly what you want to say. The invoice is twenty-three days overdue. You are not angry. You just want it paid. But every sentence that comes to mind sounds either like begging or like a threat.

So you do what you always do. You write something, delete it, and open another tab to look busy.

The email that takes longer than the work

The design itself took you a day and a half. This email has taken forty minutes, and you haven't sent it.

That is the part nobody tells you about working for yourself. The work is the easy bit. The hard bit is the small, human, slightly awkward messages around the work.

The polite nudge about money. The note that says your rates are going up in January. The reply to "Could you just quickly add one more round?" when it is the fourth "quick" thing this month. The "no, I can't take that on" that you would love to write like a calm adult and instead write like an apology.

An employee has a manager, an HR department, a finance team. You have a blinking cursor and your own nerves.

What it quietly costs

None of this looks dramatic. Nobody sees it.

But it adds up. You send the reminder two weeks later than you should have, because you were dreading it. You say yes to the extra work because "no" took too long to phrase. You let the price stay where it is for another year, because the email about it felt like a confession.

And there is the tired feeling on the walk home. You did not work that much today. So why do you feel wrung out?

Because the hard part of the day was not the design. It was words you could not find.

It can also make you feel like you are not quite a real business. Real businesses have a script for this, you think. They just send the thing.

The sentence you didn't have to invent

The woman at the next table, a stranger, is on a call. She is saying, evenly and kindly: "That's outside what we agreed, but I'm happy to quote it separately."

No drama, no apology. She says it like she has said it a hundred times.

You think: she isn't braver than me. She just already has the sentence.

That is the turning point, and it is small. The problem was never that you can't write. You write all day. The problem is that you keep starting from zero, every time, with your stomach tight, on the one kind of email where tone matters most.

What if you didn't start from zero? What if the first draft already existed, with the awkward part already done? A version that is warm, a version that is firmer, a version for the client you adore and a version for the one who always pays late. You would only fill in the names, the date, the amount. Then you would read it once, change a word or two so it sounds like you, and send it.

You would still be the one deciding what to say. You would just not be inventing how to say it at 2:15 on a Wednesday.

A different Wednesday

Picture the same café table, the same window.

The overdue invoice email takes four minutes. You paste in Jonas's name and the invoice number, pick the friendly-but-clear tone, read it, and nudge one sentence so it sounds like you. You press send. The coffee is still warm.

The price-rise note you have been avoiding since summer? You do that next, just because you are already in the flow. It reads calm and ordinary, the way it should have felt all along.

At 2:40 you close the laptop. Not because you finished everything, but because the dreaded part is simply done. You buy a second coffee and actually drink it.

The next morning the payment lands, or it doesn't, and either way you know where you stand. Monday no longer starts with a little knot about who you still need to write to.

That is what we mean by calm. Not a perfect inbox, but one where the hard messages stop being hard.
