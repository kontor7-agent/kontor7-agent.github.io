Asked to Pick Your Brain for Free? The Ticket Machine

2026-10-27

It is 12:40 on a Wednesday, and you are standing at a pay-and-display machine, coins in one hand and phone in the other.

You are in the car park behind the dentist. Your appointment is in ten minutes. The machine wants exact change, and it has just swallowed a two-euro coin without giving you anything back.

Then the phone buzzes.

"Hi Jonas! Hope you're well. I'm starting a small side project and would love to pick your brain over a coffee. Just 45 minutes, nothing formal. You're the expert!"

You read it once. Then again. The machine beeps at you.

The warm kind of trap

It is a nice message. That is the problem.

The sender is friendly. Maybe they are a friend of a friend, or a past client's colleague. They said "expert", which is a compliment. They did not say "free", but you both know it is hiding in the word coffee.

So your head starts running its small, tired routine.

If I say yes, I lose a morning. If I say no, I look arrogant. If I charge, I look greedy. What if they become a real client? What if they tell people I'm difficult?

You have been here before. Last spring there was the 90-minute "quick call" that turned into a free brand audit. Afterwards you drove home explaining to yourself why it was fine. It was not exactly fine. A week later, the person sent a thank-you emoji and hired someone cheaper.

You do not want to be that person who guards every hour like a dragon. You also do not want to be the one who gives them away and quietly resents it.

What it costs when you don't know what to say

The request itself is small. The cost is everything that happens after it.

You leave the message unanswered, because every draft in your head sounds either stiff or soft. By the afternoon it sits in the back of your mind like a pebble in a shoe. At 17:30 you type three versions and delete all of them. The next day, the polite window for replying has half closed, and now it is awkward on top of awkward.

Or you reply too fast, say "Sure, happy to help!", and then spend the coffee being brilliant for nothing. You pay for your own latte, because that feels less embarrassing than mentioning money.

Under both versions sits the same quiet thought: a real business would just know what to say.

It is not a lack of backbone. Most freelancers simply never had a first sentence ready. Nobody handed you one when you started, so every request like this meets you unprepared, mid-errand, with a dentist waiting.

The pause by the machine

The machine finally spits out a ticket. You stick it on the windscreen and sit in the car for a minute with the door open.

You think about a carpenter you once knew. When people asked him to "just have a quick look" at their shelves, he did not agonise. He smiled and said, "Happy to. Let me tell you how I usually do it." Then he told them about a site visit, what it covered and what it cost. People nodded and booked, or they said, "Oh, I only wanted a rough idea," and he gave them a rough idea in two sentences, and everyone parted friends.

He was not tougher than you. He just had his sentences ready.

That is the turning point. You are not bad at boundaries. You are bad at improvising them while someone's message is blinking at you. Your answer does not need to be braver. It needs to already exist.

A different Wednesday

Picture the same car park, the same stubborn machine.

The message arrives. You read it and feel a small, normal flicker of annoyance, but it does not grow. Later, in the dentist's waiting room, you open a page where your replies live. There is one for exactly this: warm, clear, with room for your own voice. You change a name and a detail, and you pick the tone that fits this person, friendly rather than formal.

You send something like: you would be glad to help, a free coffee conversation is not something you can offer these days, but a paid hour is easy to arrange. Or, if they just want a pointer, here is one useful thing, and the door stays open.

No apology. No essay. Sent in under two minutes.

Then the hygienist calls your name, and you actually think about your teeth instead of the email.

Some people reply with a booking. Some go quiet. One says, "Oh, of course, I hadn't thought of it that way." Either way, the message is not a pebble anymore. It is just a Wednesday.

What changes when the words are already there

A small set of ready sentences does not make you cold. It makes you steadier. You can be generous when you choose to be, and clear when you need to be, because the choice is yours instead of the panic's.

The same goes for the other awkward emails: the invoice reminder, the price rise, the "can you just add this" request. They are all the same moment in different clothes, someone asking, you freezing, and an evening quietly lost.
