Rewriting the Same Client Email? The Breakdown Lay-By

2026-10-19

It's 11:20 on a Thursday and you are sitting in a lay-by with the hazard lights ticking.

The recovery truck is forty minutes away. Your phone is at 41%. The engine smells faintly of hot pennies. And since there's nothing else to do, you open the draft you have been avoiding for nine days.

Hi Daniel, I hope you're well! Just a quick note to say that invoice 0412 seems to still be open…

You read it. Delete "just a quick note". Delete "seems to". Put "seems to" back, because without it, it sounds like an accusation.

Daniel is a good client. He's been with you for two years. He says thank you. He also pays three weeks late, every time, and you have never once said a word.

Draft number eleven

This is not the first version. There's one from Monday that sounded like a customer service bot. One from Tuesday night that sounded like a landlord. One that opened with an apology for writing at all.

You're a grown professional with a real client list, and you are sitting in a stranger's lay-by, negotiating with a comma.

The odd part is that you know what you want to say. Please pay this invoice. Also, my rates go up in January. It's two sentences. You could say it out loud to a friend without blinking.

But the moment it becomes a written email to Daniel, every word grows corners. Too soft, and nothing changes. Too firm, and the next message you get from him is polite and cold. And since he's one of your steadier clients, the stakes feel bigger than they are.

So you do what a lot of people who work for themselves do. You wait. You tell yourself you'll send it after lunch. After the weekend. After the next invoice goes out, so there'll be a better moment.

Meanwhile, the money sits there, and so does the small knot in your stomach.

What the waiting costs

Nobody puts "drafting emails" on an invoice. But it has a price.

It's the Sunday evening that is a little grey because there's a message you haven't sent. It's the twenty minutes on Tuesday spent rewording the same paragraph for the third time. It's the quiet resentment when Daniel's payment finally lands, and you realise you thanked him for paying.

And it's the thought that sits underneath, the one you wouldn't say to anyone: A real business would just send this.

But real businesses have the same awkward emails. They just don't write each one from a blank page.

The truck driver's clipboard

The recovery driver arrives, a broad man in a hi-vis jacket. He doesn't ask you to describe your engine from first principles. He has a clipboard with the same five questions on it. Where are you, what happened, did it make a noise, is anyone hurt, where should it go.

He's asked them a thousand times. He asks them kindly every time, because they're already written down and he doesn't have to think about the wording.

You watch him fill it in and something small shifts.

The clipboard isn't impersonal. It's the reason he can be warm. The hard part is already done, so all his attention goes to you.

You think about Daniel's email. About how many times you have rewritten something that was never really new. Late payment. Rate change. A polite no. A project that's grown beyond what you agreed. The situations repeat. Only the names change.

And you've been inventing the sentences from scratch every single time.

It doesn't have to be a blank page

It's a quiet thought, not a lightning bolt. Somewhere there could be a starting point for each of these moments, one you could adjust to sound like you. You wouldn't have to hand your voice to anyone. You'd just stop beginning at zero.

You imagine opening a message, finding the one for "friendly client, invoice late again", and filling in Daniel's name, the number, the date. Reading it once, changing a word, and sending it before your coffee cools.

Not eleven drafts. One, and a calm click.

The truck lifts your car onto the flatbed with a long mechanical sigh. You sit in the cab beside the driver, phone in your lap, and for the first time in nine days the email doesn't feel heavy. It just feels unfinished.

A different Monday

Picture the next one. It's 9:05, the kettle has just clicked off, and you have a short list of three things: one reminder, one rate update, one reply to a client who wants "just one more small change".

You don't brace for any of them. Each has a shape already. You tune the tone (a little warmer for Daniel, a little drier for the agency), check the facts, and read it back in your own voice. Then you send them and move on.

By 9:25 you're doing the work you actually trained for. The awkwardness hasn't vanished, because some conversations are always a little awkward. But it's smaller, and it no longer takes your whole morning.

And when Daniel's payment arrives, a week earlier than usual, you don't thank him for it. You just note it down and carry on.
