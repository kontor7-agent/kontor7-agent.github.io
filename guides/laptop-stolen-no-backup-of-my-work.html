Laptop Stolen, No Backup of Your Work? The Front Desk

2026-11-04

The form asks for a description of the item.

You are sitting on a plastic chair at the police front desk, Saturday, a quarter past ten. The pen is attached to the counter by a curly cord. You write: silver laptop, 14 inch, sticker on the lid.

The sticker is a small yellow sun. A client gave it to you after a project went well.

Then you reach the box that says Anything else we should know? and your hand stops.

Yes. Everything else. The only copy of the Haas rebrand is on that machine. So is the half-finished proposal due Tuesday, and three years of invoices, and the folder called "Final_final_USE THIS".

The café table, forty seconds

It happened so quickly that it feels unreal. You went to the counter for a second flat white. The queue was short. You even turned and looked at your table once, and the laptop was there.

When you came back with the cup, there was a rectangle of clean wood where it had been.

Now you are telling a patient officer, in a flat voice you don't recognise as your own, that you were gone for about a minute. He nods. He has heard this before. He is kind, and you can tell he is kind because he does not say you shouldn't have.

You say it to yourself instead, on a loop.

The sum nobody puts on the form

The laptop itself is a cost you can name. It hurts, but it has a number.

The other cost has no number yet, and that is what makes your stomach feel hollow. How many hours is a rebrand? How do you tell a client that the files they paid for are gone? Which of your invoices were sent, which are still open, and who owes you what?

You try to remember the last time you copied anything to the external drive. It was in spring, maybe. There was a Tuesday when you meant to do it properly, and then a call came in and the drive stayed in the drawer.

You never decided not to have a backup. You decided to do it later, many times, in small and reasonable ways. Each time felt harmless.

The officer slides a slip across the counter with a reference number on it. "For your insurance, if you have it," he says. You fold the slip into your wallet with fingers that don't feel like yours.

The call you rehearse on the walk home

On the way back, you draft the message to your client in your head.

Hi Anna, something happened and I need to tell you…

Every version sounds like an excuse. The version where you are calm and professional sounds the most false. You think about the word professional. You thought you were one. You have a logo, a website, and a rate card you finally raised last year.

You did not have a copy of your own work. It is a quiet, embarrassing thing to realise on a Saturday, with the wind in your face and nobody to tell.

The feeling underneath is not only fear. It is a small, stubborn shame. Real businesses don't lose everything in forty seconds.

Please be gentler with yourself than that. Plenty of careful people have stood at the same counter. Most of us were simply busy, and the safety net was always tomorrow's job.

The idea that arrives on the sofa

By afternoon you have borrowed your partner's old tablet. You are sitting on the sofa, checking which files might still exist somewhere. An email attachment here, a half-synced folder there.

Some of it turns up and some does not, and you take a long breath at each find and each gap.

And then, somewhere between the third cup of tea and the fourth search, a different thought arrives, quietly.

It doesn't have to work like this.

Backup does not have to be a heroic evening. It does not have to be a drive you remember on good days. It could be something that simply runs in the background on a schedule, keeps a few older versions, and writes down what it did. You would not have to think about it. You would only have to know it was there.

You have heard people say this for years. Today, for the first time, you hear it as something for you.

An ordinary Monday, later

Imagine a Monday some weeks from now. You are at a new desk with a new laptop, and the kettle has just clicked off.

Last night, while you slept, a copy of your work was made. A small log file says so in plain words, with the date and the time. You don't even open it. You know where it is, the way you know where the fire exit is, and you are happy never to use it.

Someone sends a message: Can you resend the version from before the logo change? You smile, because it is just a request. There is no cold feeling in your chest, no pause on the call.

The café table, the yellow sun and the front desk are still a story you can tell. They are no longer a hole in your week.
