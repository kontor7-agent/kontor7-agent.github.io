Client Wants Status Updates Daily? The Planetarium Seat

2026-11-04

The lights go down in the planetarium. Your niece squeezes your hand. A soft voice says the universe is about thirteen billion years old.

Your phone lights up in your lap.

"Hi Mira, just checking in. Any update on the website?"

It's 14:10 on a Saturday. You are not working. You have not worked since Friday at six. But the message sits there, glowing against your knee, and a small hot feeling spreads under your ribs.

You tilt the screen away. You tell yourself you'll answer later. You do not hear another word about the universe.

Three times by Friday

Here is the thing nobody sees. This client is not rude. He is polite, even warm. He just asks.

Monday: "How's it going?" Wednesday: "Any news?" Friday: "Can you send me a quick status?" And now Saturday, in the dark, between the stars.

Each message takes you two minutes to answer. But each one also takes the morning around it. You open the file to have something to say. You re-read the last thing you sent. You write a reply that sounds busy but calm, and you delete the sentence that sounded defensive.

Somewhere in there the actual work stops.

And underneath it sits the question you'd never say aloud: Does he not trust me?

You don't know. Maybe he doesn't. Maybe he's anxious because his own boss keeps asking him. Maybe nobody ever told him when to expect news, so he checks, the way you check the oven when no one has set a timer.

The cost nobody invoices

You can't bill for this. There is no line on an invoice for "answered 'any update?' eleven times this month."

But you feel it. You feel it as a flinch when his name appears on your screen. As a Sunday that starts with a tight chest. As a quiet resentment toward a person who has done nothing wrong, except live inside a silence you left open.

Because that's the part that stings a little, once you're brave enough to look. The silence was yours. Not out of carelessness. Nobody ever agreed on what "normal" looks like. How often you'd report back. Where the news would live. Who answers which question, and when.

So he fills the gap with the only tool he has: asking.

The walk back to the car

Afterwards, you buy your niece an ice cream she definitely doesn't need. She explains the constellations she remembers, mostly incorrectly. You nod and smile and, somewhere between the gift shop and the car park, a different thought arrives.

What if the problem isn't that he asks too much?

What if the problem is that he's never been told when he'll hear from you?

It's a small shift. It doesn't change a thing about the weekend. But it loosens something. This isn't a verdict on you. It isn't about whether you're good enough or whether he's difficult. It's a missing agreement, at the very start, that nobody thought to write down.

And a missing agreement is something you can fix. Not with this client, perhaps. But with the next one, before the first email even goes out.

A different first week

Picture it. A new client signs. Before any work begins, there's a short call with a simple shape to it. You both know what the project is, and what it isn't. You both know what you need from them and by when. And somewhere in the middle, almost casually, you say:

"You'll hear from me every Thursday morning, with where things stand. If something is urgent, I'll tell you the same day."

He nods. Maybe he says, "That's perfect." Maybe he says nothing, but his shoulders drop an inch. People are not afraid of waiting. They are afraid of not knowing.

Then Monday arrives, and it's just Monday. You make coffee. You open the file you meant to open. Your phone stays face-down on the desk, and it stays quiet, because there's nothing left for him to wonder about until Thursday.

On Thursday you write four lines. Done, doing, next, and one thing you need from him. It takes five minutes. He replies with a thumbs-up.

At six, you close the laptop. Saturday, when the lights go down somewhere, your phone is just a phone.
