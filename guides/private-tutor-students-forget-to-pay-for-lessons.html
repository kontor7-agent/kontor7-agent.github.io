Students Forget to Pay for Lessons? The 12:50 Sandwich

2026-10-17

Marit has four minutes.

The 12:00 lesson ended at 12:46, because her student had one more question about the conditional. The 13:00 lesson starts in fourteen minutes. That leaves a sandwich, a glass of water and the kitchen counter, where her phone lies face-up next to a notes app titled money??

She scrolls it with her thumb. Half the entries are crossed out. Some are not. Jonas – Sept? Priya – paid 2 lessons or 3? Check bank.

The thought arrives the way it always does, quietly and a little too fast: Who actually owes me?

She doesn't know. And she is a woman who can tell you exactly which students mix up "since" and "for".

(Marit is an illustrative character. She is not a real customer, and this is not a case study.)

The question you can't ask out loud

Here's what nobody tells you about teaching privately. The teaching is the easy part. You like the people. You like the moment when a sentence finally comes out right. You like being the person someone trusts with their exam, their promotion, their move abroad.

What you don't like is the other conversation.

"By the way, I haven't seen the payment for last month."

You write it in your head while you rinse your mug. You delete it. You soften it. You add three smileys and then remove them because smileys feel like begging. And then the student arrives on screen, happy and prepared, and you say, "Great, let's begin."

They aren't bad people. That's the trouble. They're busy adults with full inboxes, and a lesson payment is the easiest thing in the world to forget. It's small. It's recurring. It lives in a banking app they open twice a week.

But for you it isn't small. It's rent in the third week of the month.

The sandwich goes dry

Marit takes a bite and opens her banking app. She scrolls. Transfers come in with references like Lesson, English, Marit, Sept, or nothing at all. One just says thanks! with a flower.

She tries to match them against her calendar. The calendar shows lessons, but not which ones were cancelled the same morning, or which ones she moved to Friday as a favour. The notes app shows names, but not dates. Her memory shows a vague feeling about a student in the spring who "definitely paid", or maybe said she would.

The clock on the oven says 12:55.

She puts the sandwich down. Four minutes was never enough for this, and she knows it. Every Monday she says she'll sort it properly. Every Monday there's another lesson.

What it costs her isn't only the money, although some of that is probably out there, unclaimed, politely sleeping in other people's good intentions. It's the low hum under the whole week. The slight flinch when a student's name appears. The way she hesitates before saying yes to an extra slot, because some part of her wonders whether she's already working for free.

It's the feeling of not being a real business. Just a person with a laptop and a good ear for grammar.

The turning point is not dramatic

Nothing explodes. No student shouts. That Thursday, Marit simply gets a message from someone she likes: "Sorry, I think I'm behind on payments? Can you remind me what I owe?"

And she can't answer.

Not because it's rude. Because she honestly doesn't know. She tells him she'll check and get back to him, then spends twenty minutes in the notes app, the calendar and the bank statements, building the answer from scratch. When she finally sends it, she's about 80 percent sure.

She notices something afterwards. He wasn't annoyed. He was relieved to be asked, or rather, to be told. The awkwardness she had carried for weeks lived almost entirely in her own head.

That's the moment she thinks: It doesn't have to be this vague.

Not a bigger system. Not accounting software with forty menus. Just one place where a lesson taught sits next to a lesson paid, so that "who's behind?" becomes a glance, not a detective story. A place that also shows what the short-notice cancellations really cost her, instead of leaving it as a sour feeling, and that quietly tells her when her rate hasn't moved in too long.

An ordinary Monday, later

Picture the same kitchen counter. Same sandwich, maybe slightly better bread.

The 12:00 lesson ends on time, or a little late, who cares. Marit opens one file. She sees four names with a calm little marker next to them and one student, Jonas, who is two lessons behind. Not a mystery. Just a fact.

She writes him a short, friendly note. It takes under a minute because she isn't guessing and isn't apologising. She knows what she's asking for. She hits send, eats half the sandwich while it's still soft, and refills her water.

At 13:00 she starts the next lesson with her full attention. There's no hum underneath.

That's all it is. Not wealth, not a transformation. Just the ability to teach without a small unanswered question sitting on your shoulder.
