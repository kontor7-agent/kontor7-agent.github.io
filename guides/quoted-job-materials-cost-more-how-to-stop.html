Quoted a Job, Materials Cost More? The Deck Rail Call

2026-10-16

It is 12:40 on a Tuesday, and you are standing in a stranger's back garden with a tape measure in one hand and your phone in the other.

The deck is half built. The frame is square, the boards are down, and the sun is finally out. The client has just walked off with a mug of tea to take a call.

The phone buzzes. It is the timber merchant: Your order is ready. Total attached.

You open it. You look at the number. You look at it again.

Then you open the message you sent the client three weeks ago, the one with the price, and you do the sum in your head. It is not a good sum.

The quote you wrote from memory

You remember how that quote happened. You stood on this same lawn, measured twice, and said a figure out loud almost before you had finished thinking. It sounded fair. It sounded like something a client would say yes to.

That evening you typed it into a message and sent it. Materials, labour, "roughly a week." One line, one total.

What you did not do was write down the post brackets, the screws, the extra rail, the skip, the fuel for six trips to the merchant, or the two mornings you would spend on the phone sorting out the delivery. Those were all in your head, somewhere, as a vague feeling that it would be fine.

It was not fine. Timber had crept up since you last priced it. The rail the client chose was not the one you had in mind. And the screws, always the screws, were a small number multiplied by a lot.

None of it is dramatic. It is just a quiet leak. Forty here, sixty there, until the job that was supposed to pay for the month pays for about three weeks of it.

The call you rehearse in your head

The client is still on the phone. You stand by the van and start drafting the sentence you do not want to say.

Sorry, the materials came in higher than I expected, so I need to adjust the price.

You hear how it sounds. It sounds like you did not know your own trade. It sounds like an excuse, and you can already picture the pause on the other end.

So you do the other thing instead. You swallow it. You think, I'll make it up on the next one. You pay the merchant from your own account, put the receipt in the glove box with the others, and carry on screwing down boards with a small, hot knot behind your ribs.

That knot is the real cost. The money matters, but the knot comes home with you. It sits at the dinner table. It wakes you at 5:30 with the thought: Am I actually any good at running this?

You are good at the work. The deck is going to look lovely. The trouble is that nobody ever taught you to price it as carefully as you build it.

The moment it clicks

The client comes back, tea gone cold, and says something kind. "It's looking fantastic. Just so I know, are we still on the number you gave me?"

There is a long second.

And in that second you notice something. The problem is not that the number was wrong. The problem is that you have never once seen the job laid out in front of you: what you expected to spend, what you actually spent, and what was left for you.

You answered the question from your gut because your gut was all you had. There was no page to point at, no list to look down, no calm "here's what changed and why."

You realise it does not have to work this way. The number can be built on site, from a list, while you are still standing there with the measuring tape. The receipts can land somewhere that is not the glove box. The margin can be something you see before you say yes, not something you discover in the merchant's car park.

You tell the client, truthfully, that you will go through it with them properly. Then you finish the rail.

A different Monday

Picture a Monday a few months from now.

You are in someone's kitchen, looking at a wall that needs rewiring or a fence that needs replacing. You measure, you talk, you ask what they have in mind. Then you sit at the table and put the job together in one place: materials, hours, the bits you always forget, and a margin that is there on purpose.

The number you say out loud is the number you meant. You can say it without rushing and without apologising. If the client asks why, you have an answer that fits on one page.

A week later, when the merchant's total lands, there is no sinking feeling. You drop it in next to the quote, and it either matches or it does not, and either way you know within a minute. If the price of timber has jumped, you find out while there is still time to say so.

At the end of the week you can see which jobs paid properly and which ones only felt busy. You can see who still owes you. And at six you put your boots by the door, and the evening is just an evening.

Nobody has to be a spreadsheet person for this. It just needs one calm place where the quote, the receipts and the money sit together.
