Client Ignoring Your Emails? The Recycling Centre Queue

2026-10-14

Thursday, 12:20. You are fourth in line at the recycling centre, engine off, a crate of clinking bottles on the back seat.

The phone sits in the cup holder. You pick it up for the fourth time in ten minutes and open your inbox. Nothing. Under Sent there are two emails to the same client. The first went out nine days ago, the second five days ago. Both are marked as delivered. Neither has an answer.

You are Ines. You translate product manuals and website copy, and this client is your biggest project this quarter. The draft is finished. You need two answers from them before you can move on. They don't come.

The email you can't start

The car ahead rolls forward. You roll forward too, and open a blank message.

Hi Jonas, just following up on my previous email...

You delete it. Too meek. You try again.

Hi Jonas, I haven't heard back from you and I need your feedback by...

Too sharp. He might be on holiday. He might be drowning. He might have read it on his phone, meant to answer, and let it sink.

So you do what you always do. You put the phone face down on the passenger seat and tell yourself you'll write it properly later.

You don't need a better reason than that. You just need the knot in your stomach to loosen first.

What silence quietly costs

Nobody warns you that silence has a price, even when no money has changed hands yet.

It costs the afternoon you can't plan, because you don't know whether the next project can start. It costs the little flinch every time the phone buzzes. It costs the mental tab that stays open all day: did he see it? Did I say something wrong? Is he annoyed with me?

And it costs something harder to name. You start to feel like a person who has to beg for attention in her own business. Not a professional with a clear next step, just someone waiting by the door.

The truth is that you are not bad at emails. You write for a living. You can turn a German safety manual into warm, correct English before lunch. But when the words are about you, about your time, your deadline, your right to an answer, the sentences go stiff.

The turning point, in a queue of bottles

The barrier lifts. You pull forward, park, and carry the crate to the green container. Brown glass, white glass, green glass. Every bottle has its slot. You don't have to think about it. You just drop it in.

Standing there with the clinking and the cold air, a thought arrives that is almost annoyingly simple:

I don't struggle with this because it's hard. I struggle because I start from zero every single time.

Every follow-up begins with a blank page and a pile of feelings. Is this the polite nudge or the firm one? Do I mention the deadline? Do I apologise for writing again? You are making a fresh decision, under stress, for something that happens to every freelancer, every month.

What if the first draft already existed? What if you only had to fill in the name, the date and the one question you need answered, and then adjust the tone so it sounds like you?

It is a small idea. It still feels like putting something heavy down.

The same Thursday, a little later

Back in the car you open a fresh message. This time you start with what you need, not with how you feel.

Two questions. One date. One sentence that makes it easy to answer: a quick yes or no is enough. A friendly line at the end. No apology, no drama.

You read it once. It sounds like you on a good day: clear, kind, unbothered. You press send, put the phone in the cup holder, and drive home with the radio on.

Jonas replies the next morning with two words and a thumbs-up. Maybe he was away. Maybe he was buried. You will never know, and that is fine. What matters is that you stopped carrying it.

What a normal Monday feels like after

Picture a Monday where an unanswered email is just a to-do, not a verdict on you.

You open your inbox, spot the thread that has gone quiet, and know exactly what comes next. The gentle nudge on day four. The clearer one with a date on day nine. The calm last note when you need to plan around the silence. You don't have to find the words each time. You find the draft, change two details, and send.

You stop rehearsing conversations in the shower. You stop checking your phone at red lights. The waiting still happens, because clients are people and people get busy, but it takes up less room in your head.

By ten o'clock, the awkward email is done and the work you actually enjoy has started.
