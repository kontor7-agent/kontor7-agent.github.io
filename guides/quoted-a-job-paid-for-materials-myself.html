Quoted a Job, Paid for Materials Yourself? The Van, 7:10

2026-10-16

The van is parked outside number 9, engine off, and the client's curtains haven't moved yet. It is 7:10 on a Tuesday. You are early on purpose, because early feels like control.

You have a coffee going cold in the cup holder and your phone in your hand. The card app is open. Yesterday's wholesaler receipt sits at the top: cable, a consumer unit, two boxes of fittings, a roll of the good trunking because the cheap one looks awful.

You scroll up. And up. Last Thursday, the same wholesaler. Friday, the hardware shop. Saturday, a second trip because you were one box short.

Then you open your quote for this job. One line. A single number you wrote on the back of your hand, almost, standing in this client's hallway three weeks ago.

You do the sum in your head. You do it again. It still comes out wrong.

The quote that felt fine at the time

Here is what happened, and you know it by heart.

The client was friendly. The hallway was cold. You looked at the wall, looked at the old board, and said a number that felt reasonable. You said it with a calm voice, because a calm voice sounds like a professional.

You counted your time. You counted a bit of travel. The materials, you counted loosely, "roughly this much", in your head, the way you always do.

Then the job grew. A wall that wasn't what it looked like. A second visit. A part nobody mentions until you are standing there with the power off.

The client's number never moved. Yours did. And the difference is sitting on your card statement, in your name, waiting for you.

What it quietly costs

It isn't only money. It's the small flat feeling in your chest when you realise you've been working, in a sense, for free. Worse: paying to work.

It's the thought you don't say out loud, that you're not very good at the business side. That a real company would have this figured out. That you're just a person with a van and a good pair of hands, guessing.

It's the Sunday evening when you sort the receipts into a pile, then into two piles, then give up and put them back in the glove box. The glove box is the filing system, and everyone knows it.

And it's the next quote. Because after a job like this, you either quote too nervously and lose the work, or too hopefully and repeat it. Neither feels good.

The knock

At 7:25 the curtain twitches. The client opens the door in a dressing gown, holding a mug, apologising for the state of the kitchen.

You smile. You carry in the toolbag. You work.

Around ten, with the old board finally off the wall and the dust settling, you notice something. You aren't angry. You are just clear. For the first time in weeks you can see exactly what this job is: one hallway, one quote, and a hole in the middle of it that has a shape.

The hole isn't your skill. It isn't the client. It's that on the day you said the number, nothing was in front of you except your memory and a hopeful feeling.

Nobody gave you a place to write down what you would buy, what it would cost, and what you needed to keep for yourself. So you carried it all in your head. Heads are not good at that. They are good at wiring.

A different Tuesday

Imagine the same hallway, a few weeks earlier.

You look at the wall. You still say "let me work it out properly and send it tonight." That sentence alone takes the pressure off. Nobody has ever lost a good client for saying it.

Back in the van, you open one sheet. You put in the job, the parts you expect to buy, the hours you expect to spend. Next to those you see what you would actually keep at the end. Not a feeling. A figure.

Some jobs, you notice, look better than you thought. Some look thin. One you would politely decline, and the decline feels like relief rather than failure.

When the wholesaler receipt comes in, it has somewhere to go. It is attached to the job, not to a pile. At the end of the month, you do not wonder who still owes you. You can see it.

Nothing dramatic. Just a Monday where you drink your coffee while it's still hot.

Letting it be ordinary

You don't need to become an accountant. You don't need a new van, a rebrand, or a business course. You need one quiet place where the quote, the materials and the money that stays with you all sit together, so you can see them before you say the number.

That is all a good system does. It gives your memory the evening off.

The job at number 9 will finish on Thursday. You won't get back what you've already spent, and that's fine. It was a lesson with a price tag, and you've paid it. The next quote can be different.
