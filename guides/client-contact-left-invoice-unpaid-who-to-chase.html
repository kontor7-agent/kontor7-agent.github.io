Contact Left, Invoice Unpaid? Who to Chase: The Swing Set

2026-11-03

It is half-term, Wednesday, 10:40 in the morning. You are standing next to a roundabout in a playground, holding a paper cup that has gone lukewarm. Your nephew is on the swing, shouting "higher!" and you are pushing with one hand.

In the other hand is your phone.

The email you sent to Jonas last week has bounced. Not slowly, not politely. Just a grey block of text: Address not found.

Jonas was your contact at the client company. He briefed you, approved your drafts, said "love it" twice, and promised the invoice would go through "in the next run." That was seven weeks ago. The invoice is still open. And now Jonas, apparently, does not exist.

You push the swing again. "Higher!" Your stomach does something small and tight.

Who do you even write to now?

This is the question nobody warns you about. Chasing a late payment is hard enough when you know the name. But when the person has left, the whole thing goes foggy.

Should you write to the general info address and hope someone cares? Should you guess the new person on the team? Is there an accounting department, or is it one tired person with a shared inbox? And what do you even say? "Hi, you don't know me, but your former colleague owes me money"?

You start drafting in your head. Every version sounds either apologetic or furious.

So you do what many people do. You put the phone in your pocket and push the swing.

You tell yourself it is a holiday week. Everyone is away. It is not the right moment. You will look at it Monday.

But the knot stays. It sits there while your sister talks about school shoes. It sits there while you buy a bag of crisps from the kiosk. A holiday that isn't one: you are standing in the sun, and a corner of your brain is still at the desk, checking an inbox that has nothing in it.

What it quietly costs

The invoice is not huge, but it is not small either. It is the one that was supposed to cover your rent this month. You have already moved some things around in your head. A new monitor, postponed. A weekend away, "maybe later."

And there is the other cost, the one that is harder to name. You feel a bit silly. A real business would know exactly who to write to. A real business would have a name, a process, a next step. You have a bounced email and a feeling.

You also notice something odd. You are not angry at the client. You are not even angry at Jonas. You are just tired of not knowing what to do next, and of the way it makes you feel small.

The part where you stop guessing

Later, back on the park bench, your nephew has moved on to the slide. You open your laptop on your knees, mostly to avoid your phone, and look at your invoice list.

And you see it. The invoice has a purchase order reference, and a billing address on the PDF you sent. That address is not Jonas's inbox. It is a different one, a company-level one, the kind that belongs to a team and not to a person.

Nobody left that inbox.

It is a small thing. But it changes the shape of the problem. The question was never "who replaces Jonas?" The question is "where does an invoice normally go to be paid?" And you already knew the answer. You simply forgot, because Jonas had been so friendly that you started treating him as the whole company.

You write a short message. Three sentences. The invoice number, the date, the amount, and a simple question: could they confirm the status and the expected payment date? You mention your contact has moved on, so you are writing to the billing address directly.

No apology. No drama. Just the facts, in a voice you would not mind reading aloud.

You press send. Nothing explodes.

What a calmer Wednesday looks like

Here is the thing you notice, sitting there with the smell of cut grass and a half-eaten bag of crisps. Writing the message was not the hard part. The hard part was the fog before it. Not knowing which step comes next. Wondering whether now is too early, or too late, or too pushy.

Imagine, instead, a Wednesday where you just know. Where the open invoice has a status, a next step, and a sentence ready to send. Where a contact leaving is a small bump, not a crisis. Where you glance at one list, see that this one is on step two, and move on with your day.

You would still push the swing. But you would use both hands.

A simple system does not make clients pay faster by magic. What it does is take the guessing out of it. You stop deciding, each time, whether you are allowed to ask. The decision was already made, calmly, back when you were not stressed.

Monday, after

The reply comes the following Monday, at 9:12 am. A short note from someone in accounts: they have found the invoice, it had been sitting with Jonas for approval, and it has been reassigned. Payment will go out in the next run. This time, with a date.

You read it standing in your kitchen, waiting for the kettle. You exhale, properly, for the first time in a week.

Then you do one more thing. You open your list and note the name of the billing contact, so that next time, you will not have to rediscover it in a playground.

The kettle clicks off. The day is yours.
