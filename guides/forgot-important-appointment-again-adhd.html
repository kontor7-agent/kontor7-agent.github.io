Forgot an Appointment Again? ADHD and the Cycle Path

2026-10-30

It is 10:07 on a Tuesday, and Jonas is cycling along the river with the sun on his neck.

He is a freelance illustrator. He has just dropped off a parcel and is feeling pleasantly productive. Then his phone buzzes in the jacket pocket. Then again. He stops by a bench and pulls it out.

Are you joining? We're all here.

The kickoff call for the biggest project of his autumn started at ten. Three people are sitting in a video room, looking at an empty square with his name under it.

He knew about this call. He wrote it down somewhere. He is almost sure of that. That is the worst part.

The Sentence You Say Too Often

He joins from the bench, with wind in the microphone and his heart going somewhere near his ears.

"So sorry, I had the wrong time in my head."

The client is kind about it. That is somehow harder. "No stress, these things happen." But Jonas hears the small pause before she says it. He has heard that pause before, in other calls, from other people.

He has a name for the thing he does. Forgetting an important appointment again. The "again" is the heavy word. It carries every dentist slot he rebooked, every birthday he remembered on the wrong day, and every promise to "just be more careful this time."

Being careful has never worked. His brain does not forget because he doesn't care. It forgets because the appointment was in one place, the reminder was in another, and a lovely sunny morning happened in between.

What It Quietly Costs

After the call, he sits on the bench for a while. The river keeps going. He does not.

The missed minutes were not the real cost. The real cost is what follows. A low hum of I am not a real professional. A lingering worry that clients can feel it. The habit of over-apologising, which makes everything feel bigger than it was.

There is also the private tax. The extra checking at night, scrolling through three apps to see whether tomorrow holds a surprise. The Sunday dread of a week that might contain something he has already forgotten.

If you live with ADHD, you may know this hum. You are not lazy. You are not unreliable. You are running a business out of a head that was never built to be a filing cabinet, and nobody has handed you a better one.

The Page That Was Simply There

That evening, Jonas does something small. He does not download a new app or build a system with colour codes and seventeen tabs. He has done that before, and by February the tabs were a graveyard.

He prints one page. It has a box at the top that says Today's three things. Under it, a plain strip of hours where an appointment can sit where his eyes land anyway. Nothing blinks. Nothing needs a login. It lies next to his keyboard like a coaster.

The next morning, he writes three lines. The call with the client's art director goes at 14:00, in a bold pen, next to a small star. It takes forty seconds.

At 13:45, his eyes drift down to the page while he is rinsing a mug. There it is. No notification competing with forty others. Just a piece of paper that does not need to be unlocked.

A Normal Monday, Later

A few weeks on, Monday looks different, and not in a dramatic way.

He sits down with coffee and a weekly page. Ten minutes. He looks at what is coming. He sees Thursday has two calls and decides not to schedule a third. He sees a gap on Wednesday afternoon and puts a walk in it, on purpose.

The week no longer feels like a fog with sharp objects hidden in it. It feels like a room with the lights on.

The day's page holds three things, not thirty. When something unexpected lands, there is somewhere to put it that is not his memory. When he misses one of the three, it carries to tomorrow without a speech to himself about what kind of person he is.

He still has an ADHD brain. It still drifts toward sunshine and rivers. But the appointments are no longer hanging on a single thread of remembering. They are on paper, in front of him, in plain sight.

The next time he is on the cycle path at 10:07, he is just on his way to a call. He has sat down with the page that morning, he knows where he needs to be, and he arrives two minutes early with his camera on.

If You Recognise the Bench

Maybe you have sat on that bench, or its equivalent. In a car park with the engine off. In a hallway with your phone in your hand and a quiet "oh no" rising in your chest.

It doesn't mean you need to be someone else. It might mean you need one calm place where today, this week and your appointments live together, instead of scattering across five apps and your good intentions.
