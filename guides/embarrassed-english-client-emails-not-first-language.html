Embarrassed by Your English in Client Emails? Platform 2

2026-11-06

Platform 2, 8:14 on a Tuesday. Your train is four minutes late. Your coffee is too hot to drink and your thumb hovers over a client's email.

It's a simple email. Can you give us an update on the timeline?

You know the answer. You know what you want to say. But you've now read your own draft six times, and every sentence looks slightly wrong.

Is it "I wanted to follow up" or "I am following up"? Does "kindly" sound polite or like a robot from 1998? Why did you write "as soon as possible" when you meant "by Thursday"?

Let's call you Ivana. She's an illustrator in Rotterdam. She grew up speaking Croatian, learned English from films and forums, and is good enough to win clients in three countries. She is not good enough, she feels, to write an email without sweating.

The sentence that stays in your head

You can talk to clients. On a call, your English moves. You laugh, you gesture, you fix mistakes mid-sentence and nobody notices.

In writing, everything is frozen. Every small error just sits there, and it can be judged.

So you do what many people in your position do. You write the email, delete it, open a translator, paste the result, and hate it because it sounds like someone else. You ask a friend. You ask a different friend. You wait until you have the "right" energy, which never comes.

Meanwhile the email sits there, unanswered. The client waits. The wait makes them wonder.

That's the part nobody sees. You're not slow because you're lazy. You're slow because every email carries a tiny verdict about whether you're "a real professional."

What it quietly costs

The costs are small, but they pile up.

A reply that should take three minutes takes forty. An invoice reminder waits another week because you can't find the polite-but-firm tone in your second language. A price increase never gets sent, because the wording feels too risky.

You start to say yes to things you'd like to decline, because a clean "no" is the hardest email of all. You write too much, apologise too much, soften everything until the actual point disappears.

And there's the feeling underneath. If my English were better, I'd be further along.

It isn't true. Your work is the reason they hired you. But at 8:14 on a cold platform, it doesn't feel false.

The turn: a different question

The train arrives. You sit by the window, and the draft stays on your phone.

Then a thought you've never quite had before: What if the problem isn't my English? What if it's that I start from a blank page every time?

Think about it. When you draw, you don't start from nothing either. You have references, habits, a way you like to begin. You never face a blank canvas with no idea what colour comes first.

But with emails, you do. Every single time. Every reminder, every "I need more time," every "this is outside our scope" begins at zero, in a language you have to hold up with both hands.

Nobody is born knowing how to write a calm payment reminder in any language. Native speakers also stare at the cursor. They just do it with fewer typos.

What you need isn't perfect English. It's a starting point that already sounds like a kind, capable adult, and room to make it yours.

A different Tuesday

Picture the same platform, a few weeks later.

The client asks about the timeline. You read the email once. You open your own set of starting lines, pick the one that fits, and fill in the date and the project name. You change two words so it sounds like you. You read it aloud under your breath, once, and it sounds fine. More than fine. It sounds like someone who knows what she's doing.

Send. Four minutes, not forty. The train hasn't even arrived.

The coffee is still warm enough to drink.

That's the whole difference. Not a miracle, not a new language. Just the weight of the blank page lifted off your shoulders. Your English didn't change overnight. What changed is that you no longer have to build every email from scratch while worrying about being judged.

And the hard emails, the ones you used to postpone for days, become ordinary. A gentle reminder. A clear "no." A price rise with a steady voice. They stop being tests and become Tuesday admin.
