Never Know What to Post on Social Media? The Bakery Queue Test

2026-10-12

Picture a Thursday, 8:12 am. You're third in line at the bakery near your studio, and the woman ahead of you is debating between two kinds of rolls as if it were a legal matter.

You take out your phone. Not to read anything. To post something. You open the app, and the little cursor blinks in an empty caption box like it's waiting for a confession.

Three days since your last post. Maybe four. You scroll back to check, and you wish you hadn't.

The thought arrives, as it always does: What do I even post?

The Blank Box Problem

Let's say you're Marta, a freelance photographer. She is an illustration, not a real person, but you may know her anyway. She's good at her work. Clients tell her so in emails. Yet every time she opens Instagram she feels like she's been asked to give a speech she didn't prepare.

She has ideas, in a way. A half-memory of a shoot she could mention. A tip she once gave a client. A screenshot of a nice review somewhere on her phone. But none of it is in one place, and none of it feels like a post. So she scrolls other people's feeds for inspiration, and fifteen minutes later she's looking at a stranger's holiday and feeling slightly worse about her own business.

The roll debate ends. She buys a croissant she didn't want, because she wasn't paying attention.

What It Quietly Costs

Nobody talks about this part, because it sounds small. It isn't dramatic. Nobody loses a house over an empty caption box.

But it adds up in quiet ways.

It's the low hum of guilt on a Tuesday afternoon: I should be posting. It's the Sunday evening when you finally force out three posts in a row, all a bit rushed, all a bit alike. It's the posts that go up at 11 pm because that was the only moment you had the nerve.

It's the potential client who looks you up before replying, finds a feed that stops in August, and wonders, perhaps, whether you're still working. (They probably aren't judging you that harshly. But you imagine they are, and that imagining is its own cost.)

And it's the feeling of being unprofessional in a place where you're supposed to show that you aren't.

The real trouble isn't a lack of things to say. It's that every single day you start from zero. Every post is a fresh decision: what, when, where, in which tone, with which hashtags. Decision fatigue before the coffee has cooled.

The Moment It Shifts

Marta's turning point isn't a lightning bolt. It's more like a small, embarrassed laugh.

She's at a friend's kitchen table, and the friend, who runs a small ceramics studio, mentions in passing that she "just looks at the month." Not the day. The month.

"I know roughly that this week is behind-the-scenes, next week is a finished piece, and there's a Q&A somewhere around the end. I don't decide each morning. I decided once."

Marta stares at her tea. Something loosens in her chest.

She had been treating every post as a creative emergency. But what if the question was never what do I post today? What if it was just: what does this month look like?

One decision, made calmly, with a coffee and a bit of room to think. Instead of thirty small decisions made in a queue.

It doesn't take a complicated system. It takes a single place where ideas can land, where you can see the shape of the weeks, and where "drafted" and "posted" are clearly different things. That's about all it is.

A Different Thursday

Now rewind to the same bakery, a few weeks later.

Same queue. Same person agonising over rolls. But Marta's phone stays in her pocket. She is just standing there, looking at the pastries, actually looking at them. She notices the baker's flour-dusted forearms and the way the light falls on the counter.

She knows what's going out today. It's already written, roughly. It's scheduled for lunchtime. Tomorrow's is a photo she picked on Sunday while the kettle boiled.

When she gets to the studio, she opens her month at a glance, sees that the week is balanced, a bit of work, a bit of personality, one small helpful tip, and closes it again. Five minutes. She moves on to the shoot she's actually being paid for.

The feed keeps going without her having to be anxious about it. That's the odd part. Posting didn't become a bigger priority. It became smaller, and that is exactly why it finally happens.

A week later a prospective client writes: "Loved looking through your recent work, are you free in March?" She smiles, checks her calendar, and answers.

That's the whole change. It's not viral reach or a hundred new followers. It's the absence of that low guilty hum.
