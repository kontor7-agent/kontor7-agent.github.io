Forgot Which Projects You Invoiced? The Flower Stall

2026-10-17

It's Saturday, a quarter past ten, and you are third in line at the flower stall.

The tulips are the pale pink ones your mother likes. Her birthday lunch is at one. You have a tote bag on your shoulder, a coffee going lukewarm in your hand, and, for once, nothing on your calendar.

Then your phone buzzes.

It's Jonas, a designer you share a coworking desk with on Tuesdays. He's not writing about work. He sends a photo of his own bank app with a single line: Finally! The Hartmann money landed. Did yours come through too?

You smile. Then the smile stops halfway.

Hartmann. The packaging project. The one you finished in August, or maybe early September. You handed over the final files, the client wrote wonderful, thank you!, and you remember thinking: I'll send the invoice on Monday.

Did you?

The question you can't answer in a queue

You stand there, holding a coffee you no longer want, and you try to remember.

You picture your sent folder. You picture a PDF with a file name that might have said Rechnung_Hartmann_final or might have said final_v2. You picture the project folder. You picture a Monday that was swallowed by a different client's emergency.

The woman in front of you buys a bucket of dahlias. The stall owner wraps them in brown paper. You are still in August.

Here is the part nobody says out loud: it isn't only about the money. It's the small cold feeling that you run a business and can't say, right now, with certainty, what you've billed. It's the thought that a real company would know. It's the quiet worry that if you can't remember one, there might be others.

Is it two? Is it three?

"Which ones, love?" the stall owner asks.

You point at the pink tulips. Your voice sounds normal. Your head does not.

The detective work that follows

You know exactly how the next hour goes, because you've done it before.

You'll carry the flowers to the bench by the fountain. You'll open your email and search for the client's name. You'll scroll. You'll open the banking app and scan the incoming payments for amounts that look right. You'll find a payment from June and wonder whether it was the first half or the whole thing.

You'll open a spreadsheet you started in January, with the best intentions, and see that the last entry is from April.

By the time you're done, you will either have found the answer or you won't. If you find it, it will have cost you a slice of a Saturday morning. If you don't, you will carry a vague unease into your mother's birthday lunch, nod at the right moments, and think about Hartmann over the dessert.

And on Monday you will write a careful, slightly apologetic email. Not because you did anything wrong, but because you don't know whether you did.

That's what the not-knowing costs. Not a dramatic loss. Just a steady tax on your peace of mind, paid in quarter-hours and low-grade dread.

What Jonas said next

On the bench, with the tulips across your knees, you reply to Jonas: Not sure mine did. Can't remember if I invoiced it, to be honest.

He answers quickly.

Ha. I used to do that. I'd finish a job and the invoice would just... drift. Now I just look at one list. Every project has a line. It says invoiced or it doesn't. Takes me a minute on Friday.

A minute. You read that twice.

He isn't selling you anything. He isn't bragging. He's just telling you the way you'd mention that you finally got your bike fixed, a small, practical thing that turned out to matter more than expected.

And something shifts. You realise you've been treating this as a memory problem, as though you simply needed to be the kind of person who remembers. But no one remembers twenty projects, six clients and four invoice dates. That was never a job for your head. It was always a job for a page.

A Monday that feels different

Imagine the Monday after this one.

You sit down with your coffee, and before you open email, you look at one overview. Every project you've finished has a status next to it. Invoiced. Not yet invoiced. Paid. Overdue. Nothing is hiding in a folder or in your memory. If a job is done and there's no invoice behind it, it simply stands out, calmly, like a gap in a row of teeth you can fix in five minutes.

You send what needs sending. You close the file.

When a client writes, Did you ever send us the invoice for the spring project? you don't feel your stomach drop. You glance, you know, you answer. Your reply is short and friendly, and you sound like exactly what you are: someone who runs a proper business.

And when a friend sends you a photo of a bank notification on a Saturday, you just feel happy for them. Nothing else. No flicker. No oh no, wait.

That's the whole change. Not a more complicated life, just one place where the answer already lives, so your head can go back to being used for ideas.

Back at the bench

You do find the Hartmann invoice, in the end. It was sent on the second of September. It was paid three weeks ago, and you'd simply never marked it. The relief is almost embarrassing.

But you also notice how long it took, and how much of your morning it borrowed.

You tuck the tulips into the crook of your arm and walk toward the bus stop. You still have time to be early for your mother's lunch. For once, you're thinking only about that.
