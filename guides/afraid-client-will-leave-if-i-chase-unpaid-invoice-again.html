Afraid to Chase an Unpaid Invoice Again? The Window Seat

2026-11-05

The 9:12 regional train is half empty. Mira has a window seat, a lukewarm coffee, and a draft email she has been rewriting since Gütersloh.

Subject: Friendly reminder – Invoice 0417.

It is her third reminder. The invoice is for a website redesign she finished five weeks ago. The client loved it. The client said so twice. The client has not paid it.

Her cursor blinks after the word "reminder".

She types: I'm sorry to bother you again. She deletes it. She types: Just checking whether you had a chance to, and stops, because she knows exactly how that sentence ends. It ends with her sounding like someone who is asking for a favour.

The sentence in your head

The thought is always the same, and it is not about money. It is: what if this is the one that makes them leave?

This client is her biggest. They send work every month. If she presses, they might get annoyed. They might quietly move the next project to someone who never sends reminders. Someone easier.

So she does the maths nobody writes down. One awkward email against a client worth a third of her year. The email loses every time.

The train slides past a field of wet, grey cows. Mira closes the laptop halfway, then opens it again.

What waiting actually costs

Nobody tells you this part: not chasing is not free. It just bills you in a different currency.

It costs the small flinch each time her banking app loads. It costs the careful way she answers the client's cheerful new request, as if she were a guest, not a supplier. It costs the Thursday she moved her own dentist appointment because she couldn't be sure the rent would clear.

And it costs something harder to name. Every unsent reminder whispers that her work is a gift and the invoice is a rude afterthought. She knows that isn't true. She would never say it to a friend. She still feels it on the 9:12 train.

The email she would write for someone else

Halfway to the city, Mira does something odd. She imagines a friend, a photographer, sitting across from her with the same problem. Same five weeks, same silence.

What would she tell her?

She would say: You're not being pushy. You're being clear. Say what's open, say the date, say what you need. Be warm. Then stop apologising.

She would say the client probably isn't angry at all. Most likely the invoice slid under a pile in someone's accounts folder and nobody had a reason to look. People who run companies forget things. That is ordinary, not hostile.

She looks at her own draft again. The difference between that friend's email and hers is not the facts. It is the tone. And the tone was never the problem. The problem is that every time she needs one, she writes it from scratch, alone, with a knot in her stomach, on a train.

The part that changes

It dawns on her slowly, somewhere around the ticket inspector.

She doesn't need more courage. She needs to not be deciding this fresh every single time. If the first reminder was already written, calm and polite, if the second was already written and a little firmer, and she always knew which one came next and when, then chasing wouldn't be a verdict on the relationship. It would just be Tuesday.

No agonising over the opener. No wondering whether day 12 or day 19 is too soon. No lying awake rehearsing a sentence.

Just a simple list that says this one is overdue, here is the next step, and words you already trust sitting next to it.

An ordinary Monday, afterwards

Picture the same woman, a few weeks on, in the same kind of seat.

It is Monday, 9:05. She opens one sheet. Two invoices are marked as due for a nudge. She copies the matching message, changes the client's name and the invoice number, reads it once, and sends it. It takes under a minute. Her pulse doesn't change.

Then she closes the sheet and opens the actual work, the thing she is good at, and it's still morning.

The client replies on Wednesday, mortified and friendly: So sorry, it got stuck on our side, paying today. That is what happens most of the time. Not a breakup. A payment.

And when it doesn't happen, when a client goes quiet for longer, she isn't improvising. The next step is already there, plain and professional, written on a day when she wasn't upset.

She stops being the person who begs. She becomes the person who runs a business, one that happens to send reminders.

The train pulls into the station. Mira closes her laptop with the draft still unsent, but the knot in her stomach is a little looser. Somewhere between the cows and the platform she has decided that this is the last time she will write one of these from nothing.
