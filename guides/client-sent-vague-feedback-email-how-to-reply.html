Vague Client Feedback Email? The Baggage Carousel Reply

2026-10-16

The carousel hasn't started moving yet. A small crowd stands around it, staring at the black rubber flaps like they might open on their own.

Mara is one of them. Her phone buzzes in her coat pocket. She expects a message from her sister, who is picking her up.

It's a client.

Hi Mara, thanks for the logo options. Had a look with the team. Not quite there yet. Can we make it pop more? Something fresher but also more established. Thanks!

That's the whole email.

She reads it once. Then again, slower, as if a second pass might reveal a hidden paragraph. It doesn't.

Four lines, a hundred possible meanings

Three days of work went into those options. She remembers the Sunday she spent redrawing one curve until it finally felt right.

Now the answer is not quite there.

What does "pop" mean? Brighter? Bigger? Bolder? Does "fresher but also more established" mean anything, or is it two opposite wishes taped together? Is the team unhappy with the whole direction, or just the colour of one letter?

Her thumb hovers over the reply button. A suitcase finally slides out of the flaps. Not hers.

The thoughts come in a familiar order.

First: I've failed. Then: They're just being difficult. Then, quieter: Maybe I should just redo everything and hope.

That last one is the dangerous one. She knows it. A full redo, unpaid, based on a guess, would eat her whole week. And the next email might say the same four lines in a different order.

The reply you start four times

She types: Thanks for the feedback! Could you be more specific about…

Delete. It sounds like a complaint.

She types: Of course, happy to make it pop! I'll send new versions by Thursday.

Delete. That's the redo-and-hope version, and she's promised a deadline she can't back up.

She types: Hi, I'm not sure what you mean by…

Delete. It sounds like she didn't listen in the first place.

The carousel groans and starts to turn. People shuffle forward. Mara stays where she is, thumb still hovering, and feels the small, private cost of this moment.

It isn't the feedback itself. Feedback is part of the job. It's the stall. The way a vague email makes a capable person feel like a beginner for ten minutes, or an hour, or all through dinner.

She knows how to design. What she doesn't have, standing here with a wet umbrella and a dead-tired back, is the right words at the right moment.

The sentence that was already there

Her bag comes around. She lifts it off, sets it by her feet, and does something she's been meaning to do for weeks.

She opens a file on her phone: a small collection of ready-made starting points for exactly these emails. She'd saved it on a quieter day, half expecting never to need it.

She doesn't hunt for a perfect script. She just finds the one that sounds like a calm professional who has done this many times. It's friendly. It thanks the client. It asks two simple, specific questions instead of one big awkward one, and it gently names what happens next.

She changes a few words so it sounds like her. She adds the client's name and the one detail about the logo she's proud of.

Then she reads it back.

It doesn't sound defensive. It doesn't sound desperate. It sounds like someone with a process.

She presses send as the carousel goes quiet.

The whole thing took four minutes.

What the next Monday feels like

A week later, a different client sends a different fuzzy email. Can you take another look at the layout? Feels a bit off.

It's Monday, 9:10 am. Mara is at her kitchen table with her first coffee, still warm.

The old reaction starts to rise: the small drop in her stomach, the urge to close the laptop and "think about it" until lunch. But it passes faster than it used to. She knows she doesn't have to invent a reply from nothing. She has a starting point, and she has her own voice to put on top of it.

She answers, thanks them, asks what off feels like to them, and offers two ways forward. Then she goes back to the work she actually enjoys.

Nothing dramatic has changed. Clients are still sometimes vague. Emails still arrive at awkward times.

But the dread has shrunk. The reply is no longer a mountain; it's a small, doable step. The evenings she used to lose to drafting and deleting stay hers. And she sounds, to her clients and to herself, like someone who runs a real business.
