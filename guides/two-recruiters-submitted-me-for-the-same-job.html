Two Recruiters Submitted Me for the Same Job? The Ticket Barrier

2026-10-31

The barrier at platform 4 stays shut. Your ticket is in your hand, and the phone in the other hand is buzzing.

It's 8:12 on a Wednesday. The message is from Priya, a recruiter you spoke to last week. Great news, the client would love to see you Friday.

You smile. You step to the side of the queue to reply.

Then a second notification slides down from the top of the screen. An email from Tom, a different recruiter, a different agency. Quick update: your profile has gone forward for the Senior Analyst role at Halvern.

You read the company name again. Halvern.

Priya's message says Halvern too.

The barrier opens. Someone behind you says "excuse me", and you walk through without feeling your feet.

Two names on one door

On the train you find a seat by the window and open both messages side by side.

Your first thought is: what did I do wrong?

Your second is worse: will they think I'm sneaky? Will Halvern see my name twice and quietly drop it?

You try to remember. Tom called you three weeks ago about "a data role at a logistics firm". He never said the name. You said yes, send it over, the way you've said yes to a dozen calls since spring. Priya wrote ten days ago with a different job title. You said yes to that too.

Now your name is on a desk somewhere, twice, attached to two agencies and two versions of your CV.

Nobody warns you about this part of a job search. It isn't rejection. It's the small, sinking feeling of not being sure what you've said to whom.

What it quietly costs

By the time you reach your stop, you've drafted three replies in your head. One is too apologetic. One sounds like a lawyer. One blames the recruiters, which isn't fair, because you did say yes.

At your desk, you scroll your inbox looking for Tom's first email. You check a notes app, a half-filled spreadsheet from March, and a sticky note that says only "call back P." You can't find which version of your CV went where.

The real cost isn't the confusion itself. It's what the confusion does to you. You stop feeling like a candidate and start feeling like someone who is winging it. You were polite and keen and available, and it turned into a tangle you now have to explain.

The awkward email gets put off until after lunch. Then until the evening. Then you lie awake rehearsing it.

The moment it shifted

Later that week, with a coffee gone cold beside you, you stop trying to fix everything in your head.

You take one blank page and write down only what is true. Which recruiter. Which date. Which role as they described it. Which CV you sent. What each of them actually told you.

It takes less time than the worrying did.

Once it's on the page, the problem is smaller. It isn't a scandal. It's a handful of facts. You can see that both recruiters should simply be told, calmly and honestly, that you've been put forward twice for the same role. They can sort out between themselves and the client how to handle it. You don't have to guess, and you don't have to keep a secret. You just have to be clear.

The email you were dreading is four sentences long. Warm, plain, no grovelling. You send it before you can reread it nine times.

Priya replies within the hour. Tom replies the next morning. Neither of them is upset. One of them says this happens more often than you'd think.

The point isn't that it all worked out. The point is that you could finally see it clearly enough to act.

A different Wednesday

Picture the same platform a few weeks later. Same barrier, same queue, same buzzing phone.

A recruiter you've never heard of writes about a role. You smile, and you don't panic, because you know where to look. A row for every application: the company, the recruiter, the date, the version of your CV you sent, the next step. A quick glance tells you whether this role is already in the sheet.

It is. You reply in one line, politely: you've already been put forward for it through someone else, and you'd rather not be submitted twice. It takes thirty seconds.

The barrier opens and you walk through. The morning stays yours.

There's a second quiet change as well. When Friday's interview comes, you're not hunting through old emails for what you promised. The notes are in one place, along with a few stories you've already prepared. You walk in feeling like the person who's organised, not the person who's lucky to be there.
