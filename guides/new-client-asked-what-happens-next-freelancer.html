New Client Asked What Happens Next? The Garden Shed Call

2026-11-06

The question that landed in the shed

It is Wednesday, 14:05. You are sitting in the garden shed you call your office. The heater ticks. A lawnmower drones two gardens away.

On the screen, a new client is smiling. She signed off on the quote yesterday. You were thrilled. You still are.

Then she says, warmly and with no edge at all: "Great, so what happens next?"

Your mouth opens. Nothing useful is in it.

You know what happens next. Sort of. You will start the work. She will send some things. There will be feedback at some point. But all of it lives in your head, in loose pieces, and none of it comes out in order.

"Well," you hear yourself say, "I'll get started, and, um, I'll probably need a few things from you. I'll send you a list."

"Lovely. When?"

"Tomorrow. Or, end of the week."

She nods. She is polite. But you saw it: a tiny flicker, the look of someone who just wondered whether she hired a professional or a nice person with a laptop.

The part nobody sees

The call ends. You close the lid and sit there while the heater ticks.

Nothing went wrong, exactly. No one shouted. No money was lost today.

But you know what happens next, and it is not what you told her. Next you will write the "few things I need" email tonight, from scratch, and forget the one thing you actually need. Three days from now you will ask for the logins, and she will say, "Oh, I thought I'd sent those," and she hadn't. Then there will be a message asking whether a certain extra is included. You will not be sure what the quote said. You will scroll back through three emails to find out.

It is a small, quiet cost. It is the feeling of making it up as you go, every single time, while the other person assumes there is a system.

You have been doing this for four years. Somewhere in the back of your mind sits the sentence you rarely say out loud: a real business would have an answer to that question.

What you notice at the supermarket

That evening you are in the supermarket queue, holding a basket with one aubergine and a bag of rice.

The woman ahead of you is at the pharmacy counter next door, and you overhear it. The pharmacist is explaining something. Plain, unhurried: "First we'll check this, then you'll get a text when it's ready, and you collect it at this desk. Any questions?"

There is nothing impressive about it. She has said it a thousand times. The customer simply relaxes. Her shoulders drop an inch.

You think about that all the way home. The pharmacist is not smarter than you. She just does not have to invent the answer while someone watches her face.

The same call, a different Wednesday

Imagine it differently.

Same shed. Same heater. A new client says, "So what happens next?"

And you say, evenly: "Good question. I'll walk you through it. First, a short form from me, which takes about ten minutes. Then a kickoff call where we confirm exactly what's included. After that I'll ask for access to the few things I need, all in one list. And you'll get a welcome email today with the dates."

You are not reciting. You are just describing something that exists.

She says, "Oh, that's great. I like knowing the plan."

That is it. That is the whole difference. No drama. No heroics. Just a person across the screen who feels looked after before a single hour of work has happened.

Afterwards you do not rush to write that email from scratch. It is already there, waiting, with her name left to fill in. You make tea. You actually drink it while it is hot.

What changes on a normal Monday

The quiet cost goes away first.

You stop wondering whether you forgot to ask for something. You stop scrolling back through old emails to remember what you promised. When a client asks whether the extra page is included, you can point to a line you both agreed on, calmly, without any edge.

New clients start to sound different, too. They say things like "you're so organised" and mean it. You do not have to be organised in some heroic way. You just stopped carrying it all in your head.

And the work itself starts better. People who know what comes next tend to send the files on time, answer the questions, and relax. You get the first hour of the project without the fog.

The feeling you wanted all along was not "more process." It was the small steadiness of having a real answer.
