Do I Charge Enough as a Freelancer? The Plumber at the Kitchen Door

2026-10-15

The plumber is on his knees under your sink, and you are standing in the doorway holding a mug of tea you have stopped drinking.

It is Thursday, just after eleven. Your laptop is open on the kitchen table behind you, a half-finished brand guide glowing on the screen. You told yourself you would work while he fixed the leak. You are not working. You are watching his hands.

He is quick and calm. He doesn't apologise for the noise or explain himself. Twenty minutes later he wipes his palms on a cloth, writes a number on a pad and turns it towards you.

It is more than you earned yesterday. It is a lot more.

And the strangest part is this: you nod. You don't wince or negotiate. You think, fair enough, he knew what was wrong and he fixed it.

The number you didn't say out loud

After he leaves, the kitchen is quiet and the tap finally doesn't drip. You sit down in front of the brand guide.

You are a freelance designer, six years in. This project was quoted at a flat fee back in March. You remember the call. You typed the number, deleted it, typed a smaller one, and said it fast so it wouldn't hang in the air.

The client said, "Great, that works for us." Too quickly, you thought at the time. You pushed the thought away.

Now the guide is on its fourth round of changes. You have a vague feeling that you have been on this project for months, but you can't say how many hours. You know the money arrived in two instalments. You don't know what it came to per hour, and some part of you doesn't want to know.

That is the quiet knot: I am busy all the time, and I still feel like I'm working for nothing.

What "busy but broke" really costs

Nobody warns you about this part of working for yourself. It isn't one dramatic mistake. It is a long run of small, polite undercharges.

The quote you rounded down because the client seemed nervous. The "quick call" that ate an hour. The favour that turned into a habit. Each one is small, and none of them ever feels worth mentioning.

But they add up. They show up as a Sunday afternoon you spent catching up, and as a flash of resentment when a client says thanks without asking if you're okay. They show up as the sentence you never say out loud, even to a friend: I think I'm doing well, but I'm not sure I'm earning well.

It also costs you something harder to name. You start to feel like a hobbyist with a nice website. The plumber never has that feeling, because his number is simply his number. It's a fair price for a job, and it comes from somewhere.

The question under the question

That afternoon you try to answer it properly, and you find you can't.

You can't say what your rate should be. You have a feeling about what clients will accept, and a feeling about what you'd be embarrassed to ask for. Neither one is an answer. They are two moods.

What you realise, sitting there with the cold tea, is that the plumber's calm doesn't come from confidence in the way you imagined. It comes from knowing his own floor. He knows what a week has to bring in for his life to work. He knows what the van costs and what the days off cost, so the number on the pad isn't a plea. It's just arithmetic he did a long time ago.

You have never done yours. You have only ever guessed, and guessed low.

That is the turning point, and it is a small one. It isn't "I must charge double." It is: I could know my own number, and then the guessing could stop.

A different Thursday

Picture the same kitchen a few months on.

A new enquiry lands in your inbox on a Monday morning. A small studio wants a rebrand, and they ask, "What's your rate?"

The old you would have felt your stomach drop, and your mind would have gone straight to what do they want to hear?

This time you take a breath and look at a number you already know, one you worked out once, calmly, on a quiet afternoon. It isn't a guess or a mood. You write your reply, and it takes four minutes instead of forty.

Some clients say yes. One or two say it's more than they planned, and that's fine, because a no no longer feels like a verdict on you. It just tells you the job wasn't a fit.

At six you close the laptop. The week has a shape, you can see roughly where the money came from, and you don't feel the faint dread of having given too much away.

The plumber's bill sat on your table that Thursday because he knew his worth. Your version of that isn't dramatic. It is a number you trust.
