Bash Backup Script Failed Silently? The Clinic Corridor

2026-11-06

Plastic chair, 10:20 on a Thursday

You are in a clinic corridor with a cup of vending-machine tea. Your dad is behind the door, having his knee looked at. You promised you would sit with him afterwards and drive him home.

Your phone buzzes. It's a client you did a logo for two years ago.

"Hi Mira, we're finally doing the rebrand. Could you send the original source files today? The printer needs them by four."

You smile a little. Two years on, they still remember you. Then you remember something else. The source files live on your laptop, and the laptop is at home. You do have a backup, though. You wrote a bash script for it in a quiet week, and it has run every night since.

At least, you believe it has.

The folder that looked fine

You open the laptop on your knees and connect to the backup drive. The folder is there, and it has a tidy name. You scroll into the project directory and look for the date.

The newest file is from March.

It's now November. You read the number again, as if it might change.

Nothing told you. There was no error and no red banner. No email arrived saying your files are not being saved. The script simply stopped doing its job, politely and without a sound, and let you go on believing otherwise for eight months.

Somewhere in the back of your mind you remember the drive being unplugged once during a move. You remember a cron line you copied from a forum and never questioned. You remember thinking, I'll add a check later.

Later has arrived, in a corridor that smells of hand gel.

What it costs, quietly

The files aren't necessarily gone. They may still sit on the laptop at home, on a drive that is working today. That is the part you cling to.

Still, the cost is already there. Your chest is tight. You are drafting a reply in your head that sounds calm while you feel anything but. You are doing arithmetic with the printer's four o'clock, the drive home and your dad's appointment. A job that should be a thirty-second of course, here you go has become a small crisis, and you are the only person who knows.

What bothers you more is the feeling underneath. You are the person who tells clients their work is safe. You are supposed to be a real business, and a real business knows where its files are.

You answer the client with the truth, dressed kindly. Happy to help. I'm out this morning, so you'll have them by early afternoon. It's honest and it's enough, but you don't enjoy writing it.

The door opens. Your dad is ready, and you close the laptop and put on your face for him.

The turning point, over a sandwich

Later, you get the files. The laptop at home still has them, and you send them off with an hour to spare. The printer is happy and the client is happy. You are the only one who isn't.

That evening you sit on the sofa and notice what you actually feel. You aren't angry at the script. You are tired of trusting something you can't see.

You start thinking about what you really wanted. You didn't want a clever script. You wanted to know, without checking, that last night's copy exists. You wanted one small record for every run: when it started, what it saved, what it skipped. You wanted a way to try a change safely before it touched anything real. Above all, you wanted the quiet kind of failure to be loud.

None of that seems like too much to ask. It seems like something somebody should have built already, so you wouldn't have to be your own night watchman.

A different Thursday

Picture the same corridor and the same plastic chair.

The client's message arrives. You feel the usual small lift of being remembered, and nothing else. You open your backup folder and see yesterday's date. Beside it sits a short logfile saying the run finished and listing what it saved. It takes about forty seconds.

You reply, Of course, sending them now, and the files are on their way before your tea cools.

Then you put the phone away and ask your dad how the knee looks. You are there for the answer, because your mind isn't somewhere else doing damage control.

That is all you want from it. You don't want a project or a weekend of tinkering. You want to not think about backups, and to be right when you don't.
