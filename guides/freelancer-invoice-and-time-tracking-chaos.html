Can't Keep Up With Invoices and Time Tracking? The Train Platform

2026-10-13

The 7:12 to the city is eleven minutes late. You stand on the platform with a coffee going lukewarm and your phone in your hand.

A message from a client has just come in. Quick question: how many hours did we do on the website last month? Want to check against the budget.

You read it twice. Your thumb hovers.

You know roughly. Around thirty? Maybe thirty-five. There was that week with the extra round of changes. Some of it is in your calendar. Some of it is in a notes app. Some of it is only in your head, and your head is not a reliable place to keep numbers.

The answer you can't give

You put the phone in your pocket. You will answer later, when you are at your desk and can "look it up properly."

But you know what looking it up means. Three places, none of them agreeing. A half-finished invoice in one folder. A sheet from March that you stopped updating in April. A sticky note that says call back re: invoice with no name on it.

This is the part nobody sees. Not the work itself, which you are good at. The sliver of dread between the work and the money. The feeling that you are running a real business with the bookkeeping of a shoebox.

The train arrives. You find a seat by the window and watch the fields go by without really seeing them.

What the chaos quietly costs

It rarely arrives as one big disaster. It is smaller than that, and more constant.

It is the invoice you meant to send on the 1st and sent on the 9th, because you were not sure whether the last two days of the month were already billed. It is the hour or two per project that never makes it onto paper, because you stopped writing things down when the week got busy. It is the small, tight smile you put on when a client asks about hours and you say, "I'll send you a summary."

And it is the Sunday evening feeling. Not quite panic. More like a low hum. You have not done anything wrong, but you cannot prove you have done everything right.

You are not disorganised. You are one person doing the work of a studio: the designing, the writing, the coding, the client calls, and the office. Nobody taught you how to be your own back office. You just picked it up as you went, one workaround at a time.

The moment it clicks

Imagine this. A few weeks later, the same platform, the same coffee. The same kind of message arrives from a client: How many hours so far on the project?

This time you do not tighten up. You open one file. You see the project, the hours, what has been invoiced, what is still open. You type the number. It takes under a minute.

No digging. No "let me get back to you." No small apology folded into the reply.

That is the whole turning point, really. Not a new productivity religion. Not a dozen apps. Just the realisation that the hours and the invoices could live in the same place, and that you could glance at them the way you glance at a clock.

And that a simple setup could keep track for you of which invoices are open, which are paid, and which are quietly slipping past their due date. Things you are currently carrying around in your head, at your own cost.

A normal Monday, afterwards

It is Monday, a bit after nine. You make tea. You open the file and spend ten minutes there, not an hour.

Last week's hours go in. You can see at a glance that one project has more finished work than invoices behind it, so an invoice goes out today, while it is fresh and the client remembers how much they liked the result. Another invoice is marked as due this week; you make a note to nudge it, politely and without dread, because you know it is simply the date and not a judgement of your worth.

Then you close the file and start the actual work.

The hum from Sunday evening is gone. Not because everything is perfect, but because nothing is hiding. You know where you stand. When a client asks a question about hours or money, the answer is a glance away, and you sound like what you are: a professional who runs a tidy business.

Midweek, you do not wonder whether you forgot something. You just know. At six you close the laptop, and the evening belongs to you.
