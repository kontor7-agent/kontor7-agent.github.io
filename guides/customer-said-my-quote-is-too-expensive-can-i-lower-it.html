Customer Says Your Quote Is Too Expensive? Hallway Tea

2026-10-22

The tea is too hot to drink, so you hold it and look at the hallway carpet.

It's Thursday, 10:40. You're standing in a customer's narrow hallway with a clipboard under your arm. Three days ago you quoted her for rewiring the kitchen and adding four sockets. Now she's holding your quote, folded in thirds, and she says it kindly, which somehow makes it worse.

"It's just... a lot more than I expected. My neighbour said it should be about two-thirds of this."

The hallway goes quiet. A fridge hums in the next room.

Let's call you Marta. You are an illustrative electrician, one of many who work alone, and this is a scene we made up. But you may know the next thirty seconds by heart.

The pause where the maths should be

You open your mouth. What you want to say is I can do it for less, because the job is good, she seems nice, and a yes would feel lovely right now.

What you actually don't know is whether you can.

You quoted that number on the doorstep, in the first ten minutes, partly from your head and partly from a feeling. Cable. Sockets. A day and a half, maybe two. You remember adding something for the plasterboard dust. You don't remember whether you counted the trip to the wholesaler, or the second visit you always seem to need.

So the number she's holding isn't a decision. It's a guess you made three days ago. Now it's being asked to defend itself in a hallway, and it can't.

"Let me see what I can do," you hear yourself say.

She smiles. She thinks you've just agreed to a discount. You think you've just agreed to a discount too, though you haven't decided how much, and you'll find out later whether it cost you money.

What the van knows

In the van, you sit with the engine off and the clipboard on the steering wheel.

Is the quote too expensive? Or is she simply the first person to say it out loud?

Those are different questions, and you can't tell them apart. If your price is fair, the right answer is a calm, friendly this is what it includes. If there's real room in it, you could offer a smaller scope or a fair trim and keep your self-respect. But you can't tell which situation you're in, because the pieces are scattered. The materials prices are in a supplier email. Last month's receipts are in the door pocket. The hours from the last similar job are somewhere in your head, and your head says "about two days" with great confidence and no evidence.

The quiet cost isn't only this one job. It's the pattern. Some quotes you shave without knowing what they were worth. Some jobs finish and you feel oddly flat, because the money that arrived doesn't match the effort. And some evenings you say "I should really look at my margins" and then don't, because it feels like a mountain, and mountains get postponed.

Meanwhile she is waiting for a text. You type three versions. You delete all three.

A different Thursday

Imagine the same hallway, with one thing changed.

Before you stood there, the quote had been built from real parts. Materials were listed with what you actually paid. Your time was in there as hours, not as a feeling. And somewhere on the page was one quiet number: the point below which this job stops being worth your Thursday.

She says it again. "It's a lot more than I expected."

This time you don't flinch. You don't have to. You take a sip of the tea, which has finally cooled, and you say, "I understand. Let me show you where it goes." Then you talk about the cable, the safety checks and the time.

Maybe she still wants it cheaper. That's fine, because now you have choices that aren't panic. You could offer fewer sockets this round. You could suggest doing the second phase in spring. You could move a little on price, knowing exactly how little. Or you could say, warmly and without apologising, "That's my number, and I'd love to do it for you."

Some customers say yes to that. Some don't. Either way you drive away with the same feeling: you were honest, you were steady, and nobody got squeezed, least of all you.

The part that surprises people

What changes isn't mostly the money, although the money usually notices too. It's the knot.

The knot that tightens when someone says "expensive." The knot that sends you scrolling through a mental list of what you might have forgotten. The knot that makes you say yes before you've looked.

When the numbers are in front of you, the knot has nothing to hold on to. A price stops being an argument and becomes a fact you can stand next to. You can be kind about it and firm about it, because you know where your floor is.

Monday morning feels different too. You open one file with your coffee. You see what you quoted, what the materials really cost, what the margin looks like, and who still owes you from last month. Ten minutes. Then you load the van, and you're not carrying quite so much in your head.
