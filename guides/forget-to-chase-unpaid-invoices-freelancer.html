Keep Forgetting to Chase Unpaid Invoices? The Fishmonger's Queue

2026-10-14

The queue at the fishmonger's moves slowly on Saturdays. Jonas stands fourth in line, holding a paper number he doesn't need, because the man behind the counter knows everyone by face.

He is a freelance web developer. He is also, at this moment, thinking about an invoice.

Not a new one. An old one. A build he delivered in early September, to a client who said "great, thanks!" and then said nothing else. Jonas remembers the due date was a Thursday. He remembers thinking, I'll nudge them on Monday.

That was three weeks ago.

He takes out his phone, right there between the crab claws and the ice. He opens his banking app. He scrolls. Nothing. He opens his inbox, searches the client's name, and reads his own sent invoice like a stranger's letter.

Why do I keep forgetting this?

The thing nobody says out loud

It isn't really forgetting, is it?

It is a small flinch. You open the email draft, and your stomach tightens. Is it too soon? Will they think I'm desperate? What if they've had a bad month too? So you close the draft and tell yourself you'll do it tomorrow, when you feel more like a person who sends firm, friendly emails.

Tomorrow has a lot of other things in it. A call at ten. A fix that took longer than you quoted. A message from someone else who needs "just one quick change."

The invoice slides down the list. Not because it doesn't matter, but because it is the one task that comes with a feeling attached.

And while it slides, it costs you things quietly. A little sleep. A little ease when the doorbell rings and you hope it's a parcel, not a reminder about your own rent. A low hum of I'm not sure how much is actually coming in this month.

The fishmonger calls a number. It isn't Jonas's. He puts the phone away.

What the hum is really made of

Here is what Jonas notices, standing there, smelling lemons and cold stone.

The awkward part isn't the chasing. It's the not knowing. He doesn't know whether it's three invoices or five. He doesn't know whether one of them is already paid and he just never noticed. He doesn't know if the polite thing to say is "a gentle reminder" or "I wanted to check in."

Every time he thinks about it, he has to rebuild the picture from scratch. Search the inbox. Check the bank. Try to remember which project was which. By the time he has the picture, he's tired, and the email feels heavier than it is.

The work isn't the problem. The memory is. He has been using his head as a filing cabinet, and his head is already full of other people's deadlines.

It shouldn't depend on me remembering, he thinks. It should just be sitting there, in one place, where I can see it.

It is a small thought. It is also the first time in weeks that the hum goes quiet.

A different Monday

Picture it, not as a promise, but as an ordinary morning.

It's Monday, a quarter past nine. The coffee is the right temperature for once. You open one file. Not six tabs, not a search through your sent folder.

One glance, and you know. This one is due Friday. This one is a week late. This one is paid, and you can stop thinking about it. The overdue one is simply marked, plainly, without drama. No guessing, no archaeology.

You write two lines to the client. Friendly, short, the kind of message you'd want to receive yourself. Hi Sam, just checking the invoice from 4 September has reached you. Let me know if anything's unclear. You press send. It takes less time than making the coffee.

No knot. No rewriting it nine times. Because it isn't a confrontation any more. It's just a routine, like watering the plant on the windowsill.

Then you get on with your day. The rest of the week isn't haunted by a number you couldn't quite place.

That is all a simple system changes. It doesn't make clients pay faster by magic. It takes the remembering out of your head and puts it somewhere you can trust, so chasing becomes ordinary instead of emotional.

Back at the counter

"Number forty-one?"

Jonas steps forward. He orders two fillets and a handful of prawns, because it's Saturday and he has decided, somewhat unreasonably, to enjoy it.

He doesn't send the email from the queue. That would be a different story, a tidier one. But he does open his notes and write one line: Monday, first thing: one file, every open invoice, who owes what.

And walking home with the paper parcel cold against his hand, he notices something. The thought of Monday doesn't make his shoulders rise. It feels, for the first time in a while, like something that has an end.

This story is illustrative, a made-up scene, not a real person or a real customer. But if the flinch is familiar, if you've ever closed an email draft and promised yourself "tomorrow," you may recognise the rest of it too.
