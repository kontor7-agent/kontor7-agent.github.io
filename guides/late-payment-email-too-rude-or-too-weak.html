Late Payment Email Too Rude or Too Weak? The Thermostat

2026-11-09

It is Monday, 9:10 in the morning, and Mara is standing in front of the thermostat in the coworking kitchen.

She is not adjusting it. She is just looking at it, mug in hand. Somebody has turned it up. Somebody else has turned it down. Nobody ever agrees on the temperature in this building.

Mara is a freelance copywriter. This is an illustration, not a real person, but you may know her. Back at her desk, a laptop is open on a half-written email. The subject line says Invoice 0412 – overdue. The invoice is 19 days late.

The email is the same thermostat problem.

Too hot, too cold

She has written it twice already.

The first version began: As per my previous message, payment was due on the 20th. Please settle immediately. She read it out loud under her breath and winced. It sounded like a person she does not want to be. It sounded like a letter from a firm of solicitors, sent to a client who once sent her a birthday emoji.

So she deleted it and wrote the second version: Hi! So sorry to bother you, I'm sure you're super busy, just a tiny nudge in case this slipped through, no stress at all!

She read that one and felt something worse than a wince. She felt small. No stress at all. She is the one with the knot in her stomach. The rent goes out on Friday.

So she is stuck between ice and mush. Too rude, or too weak. Neither one sounds like her.

The quiet cost of the wrong temperature

What nobody says out loud is how much this costs, and not just in the invoice.

It costs the twenty minutes at 9:10, then the forty minutes at lunch when the draft is still open in another tab. It costs the half-attention in the next client call, because part of her is still composing sentences. It costs the small sinking feeling in the supermarket queue on Thursday, when she checks her banking app and the notification is not there.

And there is a second cost. Because she cannot find the right tone, she waits. She tells herself it is only a few more days. The invoice gets older, and the email gets harder to write, because now the first line has to explain why she did not write sooner.

Mara does not think of herself as someone who is scared of money. She thinks of herself as someone who is polite. But polite and unpaid is a hard place to stand.

The thing that changes the temperature

Here is what Mara notices, standing there with her mug going cold.

She has never had a problem writing to clients about the work. Briefs, drafts, feedback: she can do those in her sleep, calmly and clearly. The trouble is not her writing. The trouble is that every payment email feels like the first one she has ever sent. Each time, she is inventing the right tone from scratch, alone, while anxious.

Nobody invents a fire drill while the building is on fire. You rehearse it when things are calm, so on the day you simply walk to the door.

The idea lands quietly, not like a lightning bolt. What if the words were already written? Not stiff words, not legal words. Just a handful of warm, clear, steady ones, decided on a good day. One for the friendly first nudge. One for the second, a bit firmer. One for the day when she needs to be plain about what happens next. And a simple sense of when each one goes out, so the question is never "is it too soon, is it too late, am I being rude?" but just "which step are we on?"

The temperature is set once. After that she only has to read the dial.

A normal Monday, afterwards

Picture the same kitchen, a few weeks later.

Mara comes in at 9:10 and fills the kettle. She opens her laptop and looks at one list. Two invoices are open. One is on time, so there is nothing to do. One is six days over, and next to it sits a single line: Friendly reminder today.

She opens the matching email. It sounds like her on a good day: kind, direct, no grovelling and no edge. She changes the client's name, checks the amount, and presses send. It takes about four minutes.

Then she closes the tab and does not think about it again until Wednesday, when the answer comes back. So sorry, will pay tomorrow. Not a drama, not a fight. Just a Wednesday.

There is a different quality to the day. The knot is not there. She goes into her ten o'clock call and listens fully, because no half-written sentence is rattling around in the back of her head. At lunch she eats lunch.

Nothing about her clients changed. Some still pay late. But she no longer has to be brave from scratch every time. She has a routine, and a routine is much kinder than courage.
