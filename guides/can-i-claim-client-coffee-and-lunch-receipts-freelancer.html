Can I Claim Client Coffee Receipts? The Coat Pocket

2026-11-08

The waiter sets the little tray on the table. Two flat whites, one toastie, one slice of lemon cake. €17.40.

Your client is already reaching for her coat. "Next time is on me," she says, and you both know it won't be. You smile. You tap your card.

Then the receipt prints, thin and slippery, and a small cold thought arrives: Can I claim this? Do I need to write something on it? What if I lose it?

You fold it in half and push it into your coat pocket. You tell yourself you'll deal with it later.

The Pocket That Fills Up

Let's call you Mira. You are an illustrator, three years in. You work from a spare room with a window onto a neighbour's hedge. You are good at your job and still unsure whether you run a real business.

This is an illustrative story, not a real person. But you may know her.

Mira's coat pocket holds the lunch receipt, a coffee from the station, a parking ticket and a till slip from the art shop. There is another one in her bag and one in the car door. A few are already fading to a pale grey, as thermal paper does.

None of it feels like a big deal on the day. A coffee here, a sandwich there. It is the kind of spending that feels too small to bother with, so you don't.

Then the question starts to follow you around.

Is a coffee with a client the same as a coffee on your own? Does it matter that you talked about the project? Does it matter that you also talked about her holiday? Does anyone need to know who was at the table?

You search the question at lunchtime, standing in the supermarket queue with a basket of tomatoes. The answers disagree with each other. Some sound sure of themselves. Some sound like warnings. None of them know your country, your situation or your year.

The tomatoes go through the till. The knot in your stomach stays where it is.

The Panic Has a Season

In spring it is a small itch. By autumn it has a sound: the rustle of paper when you put your hand in a pocket.

By December it is a shoebox. By January it is a Saturday morning at the kitchen table, flattening receipts with the heel of your hand, trying to remember who you had lunch with on a Thursday in March.

You can't. The ink is gone from half of them. The others say €11.80 and nothing else. Was that the client, or was that you being tired and hungry in a train station?

The worst part isn't the work. It's the feeling underneath it. A real business would have this sorted. A real business would know.

But nobody told you the rules, and nobody sat next to you in your first year to say what to keep. So you guessed. You kept everything, or you kept nothing, and neither felt right.

We can't tell you what you are allowed to claim. That depends on where you live and how you are set up, and it is a question for a tax professional, not a blog. What we can say is this: the panic mostly isn't about the rules. It's about not having a place for the paper.

The Small Turn

Here is the quieter version of the same story.

Mira is at the same café, with the same client and the same lemon cake. The receipt prints. She takes a photo of it before she even stands up, or she slips it into a single pocket of her bag that only holds receipts.

That evening, or the next morning, or on Friday with her tea, it goes into one list. A date, an amount, one line: coffee with Anna, logo project kickoff. It takes less than a minute. The slip goes into the envelope. The thought is finished.

That is all. It isn't clever. It is a place to put things, so your head doesn't have to be the place.

When the question about the coffee comes up later, she doesn't have to remember anything. It is written down. She can show the list to whoever helps with her taxes and ask, calmly, "Which of these do you want?" The decision stays with the professional and with her. The paper is just ready.

A Normal Tuesday in November

Picture it. It is a grey Tuesday, the radiator ticking. You have a client call at eleven and a cup of tea going cold beside you.

Nobody is searching anything in a panic. Your receipts for the year sit in one list that is mostly full. A few gaps, sure, but you can see them. Beside it is a short list of year-end jobs with a few ticked and a few waiting, and you know roughly what the next step is.

You think about January for about four seconds. Then you stop thinking about it.

At the client call, someone suggests lunch next week. You say, "Yes, lovely." You don't do the small mental arithmetic of what it will cost you in worry. You just go, and you enjoy the cake.

That is what we mean by calm admin. It isn't about being tidy for its own sake. It's about getting that thin, creased slip of paper out of your head.
