Lost Track of Job Applications? The Bookshop Call

2026-10-15

You are standing between the travel guides and the cookbooks, holding a coffee you haven't paid for yet, when your phone buzzes. Unknown number. Wednesday, 11:20.

"Hi, it's Mara from Linden Logistics. I'm calling about your application."

Your stomach does a small drop. Which application? Linden. Linden. You sent so many in the last three weeks. Was it the operations role or the project coordinator one? Did you write a cover letter for that one, or only for the other? Did you apply through their site or through that job board?

"Of course," you say, in the warm voice you keep for exactly these moments. "Thanks so much for calling."

You walk toward the quiet corner by the poetry shelf and hope she says the job title first.

The call you were not ready for

She does. Project coordinator. You exhale. But then she asks, "What drew you to us?" and your mind goes pale and smooth like a wiped whiteboard.

You know you had a reason. You remember reading something on their website, a line about local deliveries, a team of twelve. You thought, I could work there. That thought was real. But it lives in a browser tab you closed on Sunday.

So you say something decent and a little general. She is friendly. She says someone will be in touch about a first conversation. You thank her twice.

The call lasts four minutes. Afterwards you stand there with your cold coffee and a quiet, specific feeling: you just spent a chance on not being prepared.

What the search actually looks like

It does not look like a plan. It looks like this:

A folder called "Applications" with files named CV_final, CV_final2 and CV_Linden_new. An email thread you search by company name and hope you find. Three sticky notes on the edge of the monitor. A half-remembered idea that you should hear back from the bakery-chain people "around now", but you can't say whether it has been six days or twelve.

And under it, the worry nobody says out loud when friends ask how it's going. Am I disorganised? Is that why this is taking so long? Would a more serious person have this sorted?

Job searching is already a lot of waiting. When your own records are fuzzy on top of that, the waiting gets heavier. You don't follow up, because you can't tell if it's too early or too late. You don't prepare properly, because you can't tell which interview is next. Silence starts to feel personal, when sometimes it is only a missing reminder.

The question you can't answer on the spot

On the bus home you try to list your applications from memory. You get to seven and stall. You are fairly sure there were eleven. Possibly twelve.

Then your phone shows another email: Could we schedule a short call this week? From a company you can't place at all.

You laugh, a little tiredly, and look out the window. It isn't a crisis. Nobody is shouting. But you can feel what it costs: the small panic with every unknown number, the evenings spent re-reading your own old emails to reconstruct what you said, the interview stories you rebuild from scratch each time because you never wrote the good ones down.

You think about the time you handled a difficult delivery delay at your last job, and how well it went, and how you could not find the words for it on that call. That story is yours. It just isn't anywhere you can reach.

It doesn't have to live in your head

Here is the quiet turn. It doesn't come as a big decision. It comes as a thought on a Thursday: what if every application simply had a line, and every line had a next step with a date?

Not a system with rules. Just one place. Company, role, when you applied, what happens next, and a small nudge when it's time to follow up. And beside it, a handful of your own stories, written once, in a shape you can pull up before any call: the situation, what you did, how it ended.

That is all it takes to change the feel of the whole thing.

The same Wednesday, later

Picture the bookshop again. Phone buzzes. Unknown number.

You glance at the screen, take one breath and answer. And when she says, "I'm calling about your application," you already know which one, because you saw it that morning. You know what drew you to them. You know what you want to ask about the team of twelve.

After the call you pay for your coffee. It's still warm.

The days around it feel different too. On Monday you spend ten minutes looking over the list, see two applications are due a gentle follow-up, and send two short messages. On Tuesday you look at tomorrow's interview, pick the two stories that fit, and read them over once. By six the laptop is shut.

The search is still uncertain. Nobody can promise you an offer. But the uncertainty is now only about the world, not about your own records. That is a much lighter thing to carry.
