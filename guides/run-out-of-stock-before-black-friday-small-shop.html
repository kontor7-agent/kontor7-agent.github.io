Run Out of Stock Before Black Friday? The Swim Bench

2026-11-04

The chlorine smell hits you at the door. It is Tuesday, 4:35 pm, and you are on the plastic bench above the pool, watching your daughter's swim lesson through a window that fogs at the edges.

Your phone is in your hand. You are not watching the lesson.

You are looking at a calendar reminder: Black Friday. It is just over three weeks away. Under it sits a note you wrote in October, when October felt far away: check stock.

You have not checked stock.

The number you carry around

Let's call you Maren. You pour candles and wax melts in a rented unit, and you sell them online and at two markets. You are the buyer, the packer, the photographer and the person who answers "where is my parcel?" at 7:15 in the morning.

If someone asked how many of the cedar-and-orange candles are left, you would say "around forty." You would say it with the confidence of someone who last counted in September.

Then you would feel your stomach tighten, because you know "around forty" might really be twenty-two. The wax supplier said three weeks last time. The jars come from a different supplier, who also said three weeks and meant five.

It is the sort of worry that does not show up on any list. It sits in the background like a fridge hum. You only notice it when you try to relax and can't.

What it quietly costs

You already know what a surprise sell-out feels like. Last year you wrote the apology emails yourself. "So sorry, this one has sold out, would you like a refund?" Each one felt a little like admitting you were not a real business.

There is a second cost, and it is quieter. Because you are afraid of running out, you over-order the wrong things. The lavender melts are stacked in a box under the table, tying up money you could have used for the thing that actually sells. You are short on one shelf and heavy on another, and you could not say with any certainty which is which.

So you do what many small shop owners do. You open the shop dashboard and look at the stock numbers, then your notebook, then a spreadsheet from two years ago with columns you no longer understand. None of the three agrees with the others.

Through the window, the instructor claps. The kids bob up and down. Your daughter waves at you, and you wave back a half-second too late.

The sentence that changed the question

The woman next to you on the bench runs a small bakery. You know this because you have had the same chat every Tuesday for a month: tired, funny, nothing too deep. Today she mentions that she is dreading the weeks before Christmas.

"Not because of the baking," she says. "Because of not knowing."

You nod harder than you mean to.

"I used to carry it all in my head," she says. "Then I stopped asking myself how many are left and started asking when do I need to reorder. It's a different question. It's much quieter."

She goes back to waving at her son. The conversation is over. But you keep turning it over, because she is right: those two questions feel nothing alike.

How many are left is a quiz you can fail. When do I need to reorder is just a date.

What a calm Monday could look like

Imagine it three weeks from now. Not perfect, just calm.

It is Monday morning. The kettle is on. You open one file, and you are not bracing for it. There is a short list of what is getting low, and it was put together without you having to remember anything. The cedar-and-orange candle is near the line where you reorder, so you send the message to the supplier now, while there is still plenty of time. The lavender melts are fine. You can leave them alone, and the cash stays where it is.

The whole thing takes about ten minutes. Then you close the laptop and get on with the actual work: pouring, packing, photographing.

On Black Friday itself you are not refreshing the dashboard every twenty minutes. Orders come in, and you are glad about every one of them. No order lands as a small stab, because you already know there is a candle on the shelf for it.

Nothing about that is dramatic. It is just a day where the shelf does not surprise you.

Back at the pool

The lesson ends. Wet hair, towels, a cold shower of questions about whether there will be chips. You pack the bag and zip the coats, and for once you do not open the shop app on the way to the car.

You don't need a better memory. You need the count to live somewhere other than your head, with a line that says reorder now before the shelf says too late.

That is a small, solvable thing. It was never a sign that you were bad at running a shop.
