Burned Out on Repetitive Work as a Developer? The Stand-Up Fog

2026-10-13

You are on mute in the Monday stand-up, camera half off, a cold coffee next to the keyboard.

Someone is talking about the roadmap. You are nodding. In the other window, a CSV is open. Again. The same export, the same odd date column, the same twelve clicks you could do in your sleep.

You probably do.

When it is your turn, you say: "Same as Friday, just finishing some data cleanup." Nobody blinks. Inside, a small voice says: I became a developer to build things.

The work nobody puts on a ticket

It doesn't look like a crisis. That is the odd part.

It looks like renaming forty files by hand because the script you wrote last spring lives somewhere on a laptop you no longer use. It looks like pasting the same API response into a document, then checking the same few things, then forgetting whether you checked the auth bit this time or last time.

Each task takes ten minutes. Each one is too small to fix properly and too regular to ignore.

So you do them. Again.

What it quietly costs

The hours are the obvious part. The less obvious part is what the repetition does to you.

You start the day already a little tired. Not from hard problems, because hard problems are the good kind of tired. This is the dull kind. The kind that comes from doing something your brain has already solved.

You stop suggesting improvements, because you suspect you'd be the one to build them, and there is no time. You half-dread Mondays, and you feel a bit guilty about that, since the job is fine, the team is kind, the pay is fair.

And there is the worry nobody says out loud: what if I'm just getting worse at this? What if I'm turning into the person who runs the chores?

You are not. You are someone with a good brain doing work a good brain shouldn't have to repeat.

The small moment that shifts it

It happens on a Wednesday, at the supermarket.

You are in the queue with a basket of pasta and oat milk. The person ahead of you packs their bags in a perfect, practised order: heavy things first, bread on top, no pause, no thought. A routine so smooth it looks like rest.

And you think: that's what I want my chores to feel like. Not heroic. Not clever. Just handled.

Not because you stopped caring about the work, but because the repeatable parts finally stopped needing you.

The thought is almost embarrassingly small. But it stays with you on the walk home. You don't need a new job. You don't need to rebuild everything. You need the boring pieces to be done once, kept somewhere findable, and trusted.

A Monday that feels different

Picture the same stand-up, a few weeks on.

The coffee is still cold, because some things never change. But when the CSV lands in your inbox, you drop it into a tool you already trust, and by the time the roadmap discussion ends, it is clean. Dates make sense. Duplicates are gone. You did not hold your breath once.

The folder of files sorts itself into the shape you wanted. Before the next release, you run the same review of your API that you ran last time, with the same questions, in the same order, so you are not relying on memory at 17:40 on a Friday.

When it's your turn to speak, you say something different. Maybe: "I've got some room this week, I'd like to take the search rework."

That sentence is the real change. Not time saved on a spreadsheet, but headspace returned to you.

The afternoon is quiet in a good way. You close the laptop at a sensible hour. The evening is yours, and for once your head is not still sorting columns.

You don't have to rebuild your whole week

This is not about becoming a productivity machine. It is about picking the three or four chores that follow you around, and letting them become ordinary: done the same careful way, every time, without you holding the whole thing in your head.

Most developers already know how. The gap is rarely skill. It is the hour to build it properly, test it, and keep it somewhere you can find in six months.

That gap is worth closing gently. One chore at a time.
