Freelance Proposal Email Takes Hours? The Buffet Car

2026-11-08

The 9:42 train is half empty, and you have a table in the buffet car with a paper cup of tea going lukewarm.

On your laptop is an email that begins Hi Katrin, thank you so much for getting in touch. You have been looking at it since the last station.

The lead is a good one. A small furniture maker wants a new website, and the brief is warm and clear. She asked for "a short proposal, whenever you can". You know what that means. It means today.

So you write the second sentence. It sounds like a brochure. You delete it. You write it again, and now it sounds like a stranger who is trying too hard. You delete that too.

The sentence that won't land

You have written hundreds of emails. You are good with clients once you're talking to them. On a call you are relaxed and funny, and you ask the right questions.

But a proposal email is different. It is you, in writing, with a price attached, and it can't take anything back.

So you do what you always do. You open an old proposal and copy the structure. You notice it still has the previous client's name in the third paragraph. You fix that. Then you read the whole thing aloud in your head and it sounds like someone else wrote it.

The tea is cold now. The trolley rattles past. A man two tables away is eating a toastie with great calm, and you envy him slightly.

What it quietly costs

Nobody sees this part. The proposal takes two hours, and you tell yourself that's just what care looks like.

But the two hours come out of somewhere. This morning it was the quiet reading you had planned for the train. Last week it was a Sunday afternoon, when you wrote a proposal in the garden while everyone else had lunch. Once it was the whole evening before a deadline, and the real work started late.

The worse cost is the one that doesn't show up on any clock. You start to dread the next enquiry. A new lead arrives and your stomach tightens before you feel any pleasure. A small, tired voice says: that means another email I'll have to get right.

And when the email finally goes, it still feels slightly off. You hit send and spend the afternoon wondering whether "I'd love to" sounded needy, or whether "as discussed" sounded cold. You refresh your inbox more than you want to admit.

That is a strange way to run a business you like.

The thing you notice at the station

The train stops for a few minutes at a junction. Outside, on the opposite platform, a station worker is holding a laminated card. He glances at it, nods, and makes his announcement. He doesn't write it fresh each time. The words are already there, and he just makes them his own with a bit of voice.

You watch him do it twice, and a thought arrives that is almost annoyingly simple.

You never start your invoices from a blank page. You never design a website from nothing, either. You have a structure, you know what goes where, and you use your judgment on the details.

So why does every client email begin as an empty white box?

The problem was never that you can't write. It was that you've been starting from zero every single time, and tired people start badly. Calm people start with the bones already in place. They only need to add themselves.

You close the laptop and finish the tea anyway. For the first time this morning you aren't staring at a cursor.

A different Tuesday

Imagine it a few weeks later. It might be a Tuesday, it might be the same buffet car.

An enquiry lands at 9:15. You read it once and feel interested, not tense. You open a framework that already has the shape of a good proposal: the warm opening, the clear scope, the price stated plainly, the next step. You fill in her name, her project, and the one detail only you would notice about her workshop.

You read it back. It sounds like you on a good day: friendly and direct, and not at all like a brochure.

It takes twenty minutes. You send it and then you look out of the window, which you rarely did before.

The afternoon is yours. The work you actually love gets the hours it was always supposed to get. A lead no longer costs you a morning, so you stop treating new enquiries as a threat.

And the same applies to the harder emails around the proposal: the polite chase for a late payment, the price rise, the "no, that's outside the scope". They are no longer separate ordeals. They are all just emails, with a starting point.
