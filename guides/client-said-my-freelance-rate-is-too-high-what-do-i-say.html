Client Said My Freelance Rate Is Too High: The Pause on the Call

2026-10-14

Thursday, 2:10 pm. The washing machine is in its final spin behind you, and Priya is at the kitchen table with her laptop open to a video call.

She is a freelance UX writer. She has just said her day rate out loud to a new client. Not typed it, not tucked it into a PDF. Said it, with her own mouth.

The client's face does a small thing. A tilt of the head. Then: "Hm. That's quite a bit higher than we expected."

The washing machine clicks off. The kitchen goes very quiet.

The three seconds that feel like an hour

You know this moment, even if your trade is different. Your mind splits in two.

One part is already negotiating. Maybe I could do a bit less. Maybe a "starter rate". Maybe they're right.

The other part is just listening to your heartbeat and watching the little square of your own face on the screen, trying to look relaxed.

Priya hears herself say, "Oh, well, I'm flexible."

She doesn't mean it. It just comes out, the way "sorry" comes out when someone else bumps into you.

The client nods, relieved. The call moves on. Twenty minutes later there is a friendly agreement at a number about a fifth lower than the one she walked in with.

She closes the laptop and sits there with a cold cup of tea.

What it quietly costs

The money is the obvious part. A fifth less, on every day of that project, adds up fast.

But that isn't what sits in her chest at 2:40 pm.

It's this: she didn't know why her number was her number. She had a feeling. She had a figure she'd once seen in a forum thread. She had a vague sense that it was "fair". So when someone pushed, there was nothing under her feet.

"Too high" can mean a lot of things. It can mean the budget really is smaller. It can mean the client is testing the water. It can mean they've never hired someone like you and have no reference point. Priya couldn't tell which, because she was too busy defending a number she couldn't explain, even to herself.

So she did what many of us do. She treated her own rate as something to apologise for.

The evening she looked at the number properly

That night, she did not do anything dramatic. She did not rewrite her website or send an angry email to the world.

She just opened a blank sheet and asked herself a very plain question: what does a working day actually have to cover for me?

Not what someone else charges. Not what feels brave. What her own year looks like. The days she can't bill because she's doing admin, chasing invoices or answering emails. The holiday she'd like to take without panic. The slow weeks that every freelancer eventually meets.

It wasn't glamorous. It took less time than she'd feared.

And when she was done, there was a number. Not a hopeful one. A reasoned one. A floor.

She looked at it for a while. It was higher than the figure she had just agreed to.

She felt two things at once: a small jolt of dismay at what she'd given away that afternoon, and a strange, quiet steadiness. Like the first time you find the stud in a wall and realise you can hang something heavy on it.

The next call

A few weeks later, a different client. Different kitchen light, same laptop.

She says her rate. The client says, "That's more than we'd planned."

There is still a pause. Pauses don't disappear. But this one is different. Priya notices that her shoulders haven't climbed toward her ears.

"Understood," she says. "Let's look at what you need and what's realistic. If the budget is fixed, we can talk about what fits inside it."

She doesn't lower her number in a panic. She doesn't launch into a justification either. She asks one question about the scope, then another. The conversation turns from you vs. me into this project, this budget, what makes sense.

Maybe they agree. Maybe they don't. Either outcome is survivable, because she isn't negotiating against her own doubt anymore.

After the call, she makes a coffee. The coffee is hot when she drinks it.

What a calmer Monday looks like

There is no trick to this. Nobody becomes a brilliant negotiator overnight.

But a rate you understand changes how a Monday feels. You open your inbox and a new enquiry doesn't make your stomach tighten. A client says "hm" and you can let the "hm" sit there. You know what you need, so you can hear what they need.

You stop treating your price as a confession.

And you notice something small and lovely: the work you take on starts to fit the life you actually want. Fewer projects that leave you quietly resentful. More evenings where the laptop closes at six.
