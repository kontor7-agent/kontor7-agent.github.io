Supplier Delivered Late Again? The Email at the Loading Door

2026-11-07

It is 9:10 on a Thursday, and you are standing at the back door of your studio with the delivery tracking page open on your phone.

The status says delayed. It said that last week too. The candles for the weekend market are on that van. Or they are not on that van. You honestly can't tell.

Imagine Marta, who makes and sells scented candles from a rented unit behind a bakery. Her wax supplier is lovely. He once drove a box over himself on a Saturday. He is also late. Again.

She opens a new email. The cursor blinks.

The three drafts

The first draft is furious. This is the third time. I cannot run a business like this. She reads it and her stomach drops. She deletes it.

The second draft is so soft it says nothing. Hi! Just wondering if there's any update, no worries at all if not! She reads that and knows it will change nothing. Next month she will be standing at the same door.

The third draft she doesn't write. She just makes tea and decides to wait and see.

You may know this one. The supplier is not the enemy. You like them. You need them. Switching would mean new samples, new minimums, new risk. So you swallow the annoyance and keep it somewhere behind your ribs.

It does not stay quiet there.

What the silence costs

The lateness is only part of the bill.

There is the customer who wrote on Tuesday asking when her order ships, and the vague answer you gave her. There is the weekend you will spend promising things you cannot be sure of. There is the small, sour feeling of being the one who always adjusts.

And there is the quiet thought that a more professional person would just know what to say. Someone with a purchasing department. Someone who doesn't rewrite a four-line email nine times.

That last thought is wrong, but it is loud at 9:10 in the morning.

The sentence that was missing

Here is what changes the morning, and it is not cleverness.

Marta stops trying to write the email. She asks herself what she actually needs from him. A date. A way to hear early when something slips. Maybe a smaller delivery sooner, rather than a big one later.

Once she knows what she is asking for, the tone gets easier. Warm about the relationship, plain about the facts. The feeling and the request stop fighting each other.

She does not need to be harsh to be taken seriously. She does not need to apologise for asking either. A supplier who is a good partner would rather read one clear message now than lose a customer's goodwill later.

The email she sends is short. It thanks him for the Saturday box. It names the last three delivery dates without blame. It asks for a firm date and a heads-up the moment anything shifts.

She presses send before she can reopen it.

A Monday when it's under control

Picture a different week.

On Monday morning you open one document, not a blank page. Next to the candle wax is a note: what you usually order, how long it usually takes, who to ask, what you said last time. When a delivery wobbles, you don't start from nothing. You fill in the gaps and the message is half written.

The same goes for the other awkward notes in your week. The reply to the customer asking where her parcel is. The product description you keep postponing. The short instructions for the helper who comes in on Saturdays and keeps asking what now?

None of it becomes effortless. But it stops being a mood you have to be in. The email takes four minutes instead of an hour of dread, and you close the laptop while the tea is still warm.

Suppliers still run late sometimes. That part is not yours to fix. What changes is how you stand in it: calm, specific, polite, and not alone with a blinking cursor.
