Client Keeps Asking for Extra Work for Free? What Do I Say?

2026-10-14

It is Tuesday, ten past two. You are on a bench outside the bakery with half a sandwich in your hand, and your phone lights up.

Hi Ines! Quick one: could you also do the social versions of the logo? And maybe a few icon variations? Shouldn't take long :)

That smiley face. You know it well.

The project was a logo. One logo, two rounds of changes. That is what the quote said, back when everyone was excited. Since then it has become a logo, a colour palette, a letterhead "just to see", and a slide template. Now social versions and icons.

None of it was ever called extra. It just kept arriving, one small request at a time.

Of course, happy to!

You delete it. That is the version where you say yes and then lie awake on Thursday doing the maths in your head.

No. Now you sound resentful, and you are not even sure you are allowed to be. Maybe it really is a five-minute job. Maybe you are being petty. Maybe a "real" professional would simply absorb it and keep the client happy.

The cursor blinks. The bakery queue shuffles forward. Your coffee goes lukewarm.

This is the part nobody talks about. The extra work itself is rarely the problem. The problem is the sentence you cannot find. The one that is warm and clear and does not make you feel like you are either a pushover or a villain.

What the free favours quietly cost

It is not only the hours, although those add up. A "quick" icon set is never quick. There is the file hunt, the export settings, the message asking which version they meant.

It is also the small drop in how you feel about this client. At the start you liked them. Now their name in your inbox makes your shoulders rise by a centimetre.

And it is the story you tell yourself: that a calm, established freelancer would not have this problem. That somewhere there are people who just say the thing, easily, without rehearsing it on a park bench.

There probably are. But most of them did not start out that way. They simply stopped writing every difficult email from scratch, in the worst possible mood, with a client waiting.

The pause that changes the afternoon

You do something small. You do not send anything. You lock the phone, finish the sandwich, and watch a pigeon work on a dropped crust.

Then a quiet thought arrives: I don't have to answer this in the next ten minutes.

Nobody is timing you. The client wrote "quick one", not "urgent". The only deadline is the one your own nerves invented.

And a second thought, a little braver: What if the words were already waiting for me?

Not a script that makes you sound like a robot. Just a starting point. A friendly, steady paragraph that says: yes, I can help with that, here is how it fits, and here is what it would look like as an addition. Something you can tweak with your own voice, your client's name and the real details of the project, and send with your pulse at normal speed.

You realise the hard part was never the maths or the policy. It was starting from a blank box while your stomach tightened.

A different Tuesday

Picture the same bench, the same ten past two, the same message with its little smiley face.

This time you read it, and nothing clenches. You think: ah, the social versions. You open a draft that is mostly there already. You change two names and add one honest sentence about timing. You read it once, it sounds like you on a good day, and you send it.

It says, in effect: Happy to help with that. It's beyond what we agreed, so here's what it would look like as an add-on. Let me know if you'd like me to go ahead.

No apology. No wall of explanation. No paragraph that begins with "I hope you don't mind me saying".

Maybe the client says yes. Maybe they say "oh, of course, thanks for flagging it". Maybe they decide they do not need the icons after all. Whatever happens, the conversation is now about the work and what it is worth, not about whether you are being difficult.

And you are back on your feet, brushing crumbs off your jeans, thinking about what to cook tonight rather than about a text you deleted four times.

Not a script, a head start

You do not need to become tougher, or colder, or someone who enjoys confrontation. You only need your words to be available when the moment shows up unannounced, as it will, because clients are people and people ask.
