Invoice Rejected, No PO Number? The Village Shop Signal

2026-11-01

The only place in the village with two bars of signal is the car park of the shop.

So at 10:40 on a Tuesday, Jonas is sitting in a rented hatchback with the engine off, a carrier bag of bread and tomatoes on the passenger seat, and a laptop balanced against the steering wheel. This is the week he promised himself would be a real holiday. A cottage, a lake, no meetings.

Then the email arrives. Subject line: Invoice returned.

He reads it twice. The client's accounts team cannot process his invoice because it has no purchase order number. Please resubmit with the correct PO reference. The payment cycle will restart from the date of the corrected invoice.

He reads that last sentence a third time.

The sentence that moves the date

Jonas knows the project manager mentioned a PO number once. Maybe. On a call, in week two, between two other things. He remembers nodding. He does not remember writing it down.

The invoice went out five weeks ago. He had mentally spent that money already: the car repair, the next month's rent, a small cushion he'd been quietly building. Now the clock starts again, and nobody wrote to tell him until today.

Nothing dramatic has happened. That's the strange part. No one was rude. A form has simply bounced, and thirty days have turned back into thirty days.

He feels the old knot under his ribs. It isn't anger. It's something closer to embarrassment, as if he should have known. As if a real business would have had that number on file from day one.

The email you rewrite in your head

He starts drafting a reply in the car park. Delete. Start again.

Too stiff: Per my previous email. Too soft: So sorry to bother you, I know you're busy, but would it be possible. He is apologising for an invoice he did everything right on, except for one number nobody gave him.

A woman walks past with a dog and a newspaper. Inside the shop, someone is laughing at the till. The lake is ten minutes away and he cannot go anywhere near it with this sitting in his chest.

There's a second quiet cost, too. He doesn't know where this invoice sits among the others. Is anyone else late? Who did he last remind, and when? The honest answer is that some of it is in his sent folder, some in a note on his phone, and some in a part of his memory that is currently occupied by the word resubmit.

So he does what freelancers do. He sends a short, slightly too friendly email, attaches the corrected invoice, and writes nothing about the delay. He closes the laptop and tells himself it's fine.

It isn't quite fine. Three days later, while skimming stones, he still catches himself refreshing his banking app.

What the knot is really about

The missing PO number is a small thing. Most clients have a quirk like it.

But the knot isn't about the number. It's about the feeling that every late payment arrives as a surprise. That each time, you start from nothing: what did I send, what did they say, when is it actually due, what do I write now, and how do I say it without sounding like I'm begging?

Chasing money shouldn't feel like standing at someone's door with your hat in your hands. It's a normal part of running a business. The people on the other side of it have processes, deadlines and forms. Most of them are perfectly happy to be reminded, politely and on time.

What makes it hard is doing it from scratch every time, while holding your own worry.

The same Tuesday, a different way

Imagine the same car park, the same email.

This time, Jonas has a short routine for new clients. Before the first invoice goes out, there's a small question he always asks: Does your accounts team need a PO number or any reference on the invoice? It takes ten seconds. It lives in the same place as everything else about that client.

And when the "returned" email still arrives, because sometimes it does, he knows what to do next. He doesn't have to compose a tone out of thin air. He has the words ready, calm and friendly, in the right order for this kind of moment. He can see which invoices are open, which are due this week, and which ones are waiting on someone else's form.

He sends one clear reply, with the corrected invoice and a polite note asking them to confirm the new due date. Then he closes the laptop and buys a second loaf of bread, because the cottage has a good oven.

The lake is still there at eleven.

An ordinary Monday, later

That's the quiet picture. On a normal Monday morning, with your coffee still too hot, you open one sheet. It shows who owes you, since when, and what the next sensible step is. Not a verdict. Not a judgement. Just a nudge.

Some weeks it tells you nothing needs doing. Some weeks it tells you one friendly line to send before nine. Either way, it takes minutes, and the rest of your day is yours.

You stop rehearsing emails in the supermarket queue. You stop opening your banking app at traffic lights. Money still arrives late sometimes. But it no longer takes your whole head with it.
