Dreading Year-End Freelance Paperwork? The Hotel Breakfast Reminder

2026-10-14

It is 7:50 on a Thursday morning, and you are standing at a hotel breakfast buffet in a city that isn't yours.

You are here for two days of work. A client's conference needs someone to photograph the speakers, the coffee breaks and the awkward group shot on the stairs. Your camera bag is on the chair. Your lanyard says your name and, under it, a company name that isn't yours.

You are holding a plate with half a croissant on it when your phone buzzes.

Reminder: Year-end admin. Start now?

You set that reminder in March, in a burst of good intentions. It felt grown-up at the time. Now it sits on the screen like a tap on the shoulder from someone you've been avoiding.

You swipe it away. It says snoozed. The croissant has lost its taste.

The thought that arrives with the coffee

You carry your tray to a small table by the window and sit down. The thought comes, quiet and familiar.

I don't even know where to start.

Not "it's hard". Not "it's a lot". Just: you could not tell anyone, right now, what the first step is.

You know roughly what's out there. There are receipts in your bag, in your jacket pocket and in that envelope you put somewhere. There are screenshots of online orders, and a train ticket you think you photographed. There are invoices in a folder you named "Invoices" and another named "Invoices NEW". There are a few payments you're fairly sure arrived, and one you're not.

Somewhere in there is a lens you bought in spring. You can picture the box. You cannot picture the receipt.

The worst part isn't the work itself. It's the fog before it. If someone said "two hours, here's the list," you'd do it. Instead you have a vague, heavy cloud with your whole year inside, and every time you look at it, it gets bigger.

What the dread quietly costs

It costs you more than you admit.

It costs you a bit of every November, when you feel behind without being able to say behind on what. It costs you the small flinch when a friend with a salary says, "Oh, I don't have to think about any of that."

It costs you the feeling of being a real business. You photograph other people's big days with calm and care. Your own paperwork lives in a heap, and some part of you suspects that a proper professional wouldn't have a heap.

So you do what everyone does. You decide to deal with it "after the busy weeks". Then the busy weeks become December, December becomes the holidays, and the holidays become a January weekend with a cardboard box and a bad mood.

The pattern is so familiar that you almost find it comforting. Almost.

The speaker who ran late

The conference runs the way conferences do. A speaker is twenty minutes late, and the organiser paces in the corridor with her phone.

You use the gap to sit on a stairwell step and answer two emails. During the second one you catch yourself doing something odd. You open your camera roll, scroll, and find three photos of receipts you took weeks ago and forgot. A taxi. A memory card. Lunch with a client.

You look at them for a moment.

They aren't lost. That's the surprise. They're just scattered. Nothing in your year has actually vanished. It's only living in six places at once, with nobody keeping a list of where.

That thought is small, but it shifts something. If the problem is scattered rather than lost, then maybe the answer isn't heroics. Maybe it's just one place where things go, each time they appear. A single list that gets a little longer on ordinary days, instead of one giant pile that gets dealt with in a panic.

You don't solve anything on the stairwell. But for the first time in months you feel like the fog has an edge.

A different kind of Monday in December

Picture it, though, just for a minute.

It's a Monday in early December. You're at your desk with a mug of something warm. Rain on the window, maybe. Your year-end isn't a cloud any more. It's a short list with some things already ticked off.

The receipts are in one place. You know what's still missing, and it's two or three items, not a mystery. The dates you need to keep in mind are written down somewhere you'll actually see them, rather than lurking in your head at 3 am.

You spend twenty minutes on it, close the laptop and go and buy bread.

Nothing about this is dramatic. That's exactly the point. When your admin is under control, it's boring in the best way. It doesn't follow you into the weekend or sit next to you at dinner.

And in January, when someone asks whether you've sorted your year, you can say "yes" and mean it. Whatever help you do or don't bring in, the decisions stay with you, and your records are ready for the conversation.

Back at the buffet

On Friday morning you're at the same table, the same window, a slightly better croissant.

The reminder buzzes again: Year-end admin. Start now?

This time you don't snooze it. You don't do the whole thing, either. You just look at it and think: all right. Let's find a place for this to live.

It's a small decision. But it's the first one that doesn't feel like facing a mountain. It feels like putting the first receipt in the first slot.
