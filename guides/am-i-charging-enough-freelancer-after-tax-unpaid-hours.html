Am I Charging Enough After Tax? The Honesty Box Apples

2026-10-20

The envelope on the passenger seat

It is Saturday morning, a little after nine, and you are parked on a gravel verge beside a wooden stall.

There are crates of apples, a hand-painted sign and a small metal box with a slot in the lid. Under it a card says: Please pay what is written. Thank you.

You are not here for apples. You are here because the day before, a letter arrived from the tax office, and it is still sitting on the passenger seat. You have not opened it properly. You have only looked at the corner of it, the way you look at a spider on the wall.

You are a freelance web developer. Let's call you Jonas. You have been on your own for four years. Things are going fine. Clients stay. Invoices go out. Nobody has complained.

And yet, standing at an apple stall, one thought will not leave you alone: am I actually charging enough?

The price someone worked out

You pick up a bag of apples. The price is chalked on a little slate. It is not round or random. Someone sat down once, counted the trees, the crates, the petrol, the weeks of picking, and decided: this is what it costs, and this is what it is worth.

Then they wrote it down and trusted it. No apology and no hovering.

You drop your coins in the slot. They make that small, satisfying clink.

Back in the car, you think about your own price. Where did it come from? You remember. Four years ago, you looked at what a friend charged, shaved a bit off to be safe, and said it out loud on a call with your voice slightly too bright.

It has been your number ever since. You have never really counted anything behind it.

The part nobody puts on the invoice

Sitting there with the engine off, you start to count in your head, not neatly, just the way worry counts.

There is the day you billed in full. But there was also the hour of emails before it, the call that ran long, and the "quick fix" on Thursday that you never mentioned. Then there is the Friday you spend on your own admin, which no client has ever paid for.

There are the weeks when nothing comes in. You carry those too, even though no invoice has a line for them.

And there is the letter. Whatever it says, you know that a part of every payment was never really yours. It only passes through your account on its way somewhere else.

So the number on your invoice looks like a decent day's pay. But the money that lands in your life after tax, holidays, quiet weeks and unpaid hours? You honestly do not know. You have never looked at that number directly.

That is the quiet cost. It is not a disaster. It is a low hum of doubt that follows you into every client call. It makes you flinch when someone says "could you do it a bit cheaper?" It makes you say yes to things at half attention, because you are not sure you are allowed to say no.

The turn

You open the letter. It is less dramatic than you feared, which is almost insulting after a week of dread. It asks for a payment and tells you when.

But as you read it, something shifts. You realise the problem was never the letter. The problem is that you have been guessing at your own business.

The apple seller did not guess. Somebody counted and wrote the price on slate.

You do not need to become an accountant. You do not need a course or a new system with fourteen tabs. You need one honest sit-down with your own numbers: what you need to take home, how many hours are truly billable, and what that makes your minimum. Then you need somewhere calm to put it, and a clean invoice to send with that number on it.

That is all. A number that is yours.

A different Monday

Picture the Monday after that.

You open your laptop with a coffee, and the knot in your stomach is not there. A client writes: "Can you do the same project next quarter, but cheaper?"

You read it once. Then you answer calmly, because you know where your floor is. Maybe you say yes to something smaller. Maybe you say your rate stays as it is. Either way you do not feel sick about it, and you do not rewrite the email nine times.

At six, you close the laptop. The evening is yours. You are not doing a secret sum on the back of your mind, because the sum is done. It sits in a file, with your name on it.

Nothing has been dramatically fixed. You simply know, and knowing is lighter than you expected.

Back at the stall

Before you start the car, you look at the honesty box once more. A neat little slot, a plain card and a price written with a steady hand.

You do not need to be bolder than anyone. You only need to be as sure of your price as the person who chalked that slate.
