Client Pushing Back on Your Price? The Ticket Inspector

2026-11-01

9:14 on a Monday, third email

The regional train is half empty. Mara has a window seat, a lukewarm tea and her laptop open on the little fold-down table.

The email sits at the top of her inbox. It is the third one this month from the same client, and the subject line is still "Quick question about the quote."

She knows it is not a quick question. It is the price again.

"We really like working with you, but is there any flexibility? Another freelancer mentioned a number quite a bit lower. Just something to think about."

She reads it twice. Her stomach does the small drop she knows well. Then the thoughts arrive, in their usual order.

Maybe I did quote too high. Maybe I should have said something different last time. If I push back, they'll go. If I give in, I'll resent this job for three months.

She opens a reply. She types, "Thanks so much for your message, I completely understand," and stops. That sounds like the start of a discount.

She deletes it. She types, "As discussed, my rates reflect..." and stops again. That sounds like a door closing.

The cursor blinks. The countryside slides past, and she doesn't see any of it.

The cost nobody sees

Here is the part she would never say to a friend: the email itself is not the problem. The problem is the whole morning it has already taken.

She was going to start the new brand project today. Instead she has been carrying this reply since Friday, like a stone in a coat pocket. It sat with her through a Saturday walk and through Sunday dinner, when she nodded at a story and heard none of it.

There is a quieter cost too. Every time a client leans on the number, a small voice asks whether she is really a business or just someone who has been lucky so far. She has sixteen drafts of replies like this one, and none of them was ever sent.

So she does what many people in her place do. She rewrites the same two sentences until they mean nothing, then closes the laptop and tells herself she will answer "when her head is clearer."

The inspector

"Tickets, please."

A man in a navy jacket is working his way down the carriage. Mara fishes out her phone and holds the screen up. He glances at it, nods, says "Thank you, have a good trip," and moves on.

That is all. No negotiation, no apology on either side. The price on the ticket was the price. He did not ask whether she felt it was fair, and she did not offer a long explanation of why she had bought it.

She watches him go and thinks, oddly: when did I stop being able to do that?

She does not mean being cold. She means being plain. A calm sentence that states what something costs, with a friendly tone on top and nothing grovelling underneath.

She realises she can already do it with a tired parent, a stranger or a shop assistant. The only place it gets hard is the one inbox where the money is hers.

What she was missing

It was not courage, and it was not a better argument. What she lacked was a starting point.

Every time this happens she begins from nothing. A blank reply, a pounding heart, and the whole question of tone invented from scratch, while a person she likes waits on the other side.

What if the first draft already existed? Warm, steady, honest about the price, with room for her to change a word or two so it sounds like her? What if she only had to choose how firm to be, instead of working out how to be firm at all?

She doesn't solve it on the train. She just feels the idea settle in, and it is a relief on its own. The knot loosens a little.

She writes a short reply that morning, nothing fancy. It thanks them, says she is glad they enjoy working together, and says plainly what the quote includes and what it covers. It leaves one open door: if the budget is the issue, she is happy to talk about adjusting the scope rather than the rate.

She reads it once and presses send before the station announcement finishes.

A different kind of Monday

Imagine the same morning with the reply already half written, waiting in a folder. The email arrives, the stomach drops a little, and then you open a page, pick the version that fits, change the client's name and send it.

There is no Friday-to-Monday stone. You are on the platform at your destination with your hands free, thinking about the project you are about to start, not the one you are worried about.

And if the client does walk, which sometimes happens, you walk away with your price and your Monday intact. That is a different feeling from the one you have now.

The next email will come, because it always does. What changes is that you stop meeting it alone.
