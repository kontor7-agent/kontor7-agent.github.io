Disk Full From Old Log Files? The Kitchen Showroom Alert

2026-10-19

The sample drawer slides open with a soft click. Oak, walnut, a grey that the salesperson calls "warm slate." You are holding two door fronts up to the light when your phone buzzes against your hip.

Disk usage at 98%.

The message comes from the small server that runs your sister's online shop. You set it up two years ago, a favour that quietly became a standing job. Your partner is asking which handle looks better. You say "the left one" without looking at either.

The folder you don't dare open

You step toward the window, next to a display of taps that don't connect to anything. You open the terminal on your phone, which is a bad idea, and you do it anyway.

There is a directory called logs. It is enormous. Files with dates going back further than you can remember, some with .1 and .2 and .gz on the end, some with names you don't recognise at all.

Your thumb hovers over the screen. You know that some of these files are harmless. You also know that one of them might be something a program is still writing to, or something you'll wish you had next month when a customer says the checkout broke on a Tuesday.

So you do what many people do. You close the terminal and put the phone back in your pocket.

"Left one, then?" your partner says.

"Left one," you say again. You're smiling. Your stomach isn't.

What it costs when nothing is wrong yet

Nothing is broken at this moment. That's the strange part. The shop still loads. Orders still arrive. The only thing that has changed is a number, and the number is climbing.

But the number is now sitting in the back of your head while the salesperson explains soft-close hinges. You do the quiet arithmetic. How fast does it fill? Is it tonight? Is it Thursday? What happens when it's full, does the shop just stop?

You can't remember when you last checked whether the backups ran. You think they did. You think. There's a script somewhere, written on a good day, and you haven't looked at it since.

That's the real weight. It isn't the disk. It's the fear of deleting the wrong thing, and the fear of deleting nothing, and the sense that you are the only person who could sort it out, on a weekend you promised yourself was yours.

The drive home

In the car, your partner talks about worktops. You nod in the right places.

At the red light by the garden centre you realise what has been bothering you most. It isn't that the disk is full. It's that you have no rule. Nobody ever decided how long a log should live, or what counts as old, or what gets kept somewhere else first. Every cleanup so far has been a mood. A hurried afternoon, a few rm commands, a held breath.

A mood is a poor policy for a server somebody else earns their living on.

The light turns green. You think, quite clearly: this doesn't have to be a judgement call every time.

The idea that lets you breathe out

What if old logs simply rotated out on a schedule? What if there was a copy of the important things before anything was removed? What if you could ask for a rehearsal first, see exactly what would go, and only then let it happen?

And what if every run left a short note behind, a logfile that says what it did and when, so you could read it on a Monday over coffee instead of guessing at midnight?

None of that is exciting. That's the point. It's the opposite of a drama.

A different Monday

Picture the following week. You open your laptop at nine with a mug beside you. You glance at the little report from the weekend run. Backups finished. Old logs rotated. Disk at a comfortable number that hasn't moved much.

You don't feel clever. You feel ordinary, which is better. The shop hums along. Your sister texts about a new candle line, not about a crash. You answer that instead.

And the afternoon you used to lose to nervous tidying turns into an afternoon. Just that. You go for a walk. You look at the kitchen brochures with your partner and actually have an opinion about the grey.

The server is still a server. It will always have more files than you want. But it stops being a small private worry you carry around in your pocket.
