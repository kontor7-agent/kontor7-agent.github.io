Applied to Too Many Jobs? The Unknown Number Call

2026-10-15

The phone buzzes just as the self-checkout says unexpected item in the bagging area.

Unknown number. A city code you half recognise.

You have a basket of lightbulbs, a bag of rice and a pack of sponges. You also have a sudden, hot feeling in your chest. You pick up anyway.

"Hi, this is Jana from the talent team. Thanks for applying to the Operations Coordinator role. Do you have a few minutes?"

Operations Coordinator. You say "Of course!" with more brightness than you feel. Behind it, your mind is flipping through a card index with no labels. Was that the one with the nice website? The one that wanted a cover letter? The one where you typed the salary into a little box and then wondered if it was too high?

"Great," says Jana. "Can you tell me what drew you to us?"

The machine beeps again. You wedge the phone against your shoulder and say something warm and vague about "the mission" and "growth." It's the kind of answer that works for any company. Jana can probably tell.

Forty-one tabs, no memory

Later, on the bench outside, you open the laptop you shouldn't have brought shopping. The browser has forty-one tabs open. Some are job ads that have since disappeared. Some are confirmation emails with subject lines like Thank you for your application! All of them look exactly the same.

You try to rebuild the picture. There's a note on your phone with half a list. There's a spreadsheet from three weeks ago that stops at row 14. There's a sticky note on the monitor that says call back Thurs?? and doesn't say who.

You've sent a lot of applications. You know that. What you don't know is which ones got a reply, which ones you were meant to follow up on, and which ones quietly closed while you weren't looking.

Nobody warns you about this part of a job search. The hard part isn't only the rejections. It's the fog. It's the low hum of I should know this that follows you from the kitchen to the bus to bed.

What the fog quietly costs

It costs you a good first minute on the phone. You wanted to sound prepared. Instead you sounded like someone being quizzed on a subject she'd forgotten she signed up for.

It costs you follow-ups you meant to send. Somewhere in that pile is a company that said "we'll be in touch next week," and that week has come and gone with no one noticing.

It costs you evenings. You sit down to apply to one more role and spend forty minutes just trying to work out where you already applied. By the time you start, you're tired, and the cover letter reads like it.

And it costs you something harder to name: the feeling that you're good at things. You're a person who normally keeps track. You've planned events, managed budgets, remembered everyone's birthday. Yet this, the thing that matters most right now, is held together with sticky notes and hope.

The sentence you write on the bench

The turning point isn't dramatic. You close the extra tabs one by one and say quietly, to nobody, "It doesn't have to be like this."

Nothing is wrong with you. A job search is simply a lot of small moving parts: dates, names, links, replies, next steps. The human head is a poor place to store them, especially a head that's also worried about rent and whether you should have said "mission" back there.

What you need is one calm place. Somewhere each application lives with its date and its status, and somewhere something nudges you when a follow-up is due. A place where the interview you're nervous about has its own corner, with your stories already written down, so you're not inventing them at the last second.

That's all. Not a grand system. A list that remembers for you.

A different Tuesday

Picture the same kind of call a few weeks on.

You're in the same supermarket, the same aisle with the same sponges. The phone buzzes with an unknown number. Your stomach doesn't even jump.

"Hi, this is Marcus from the hiring team, thanks for applying to the Project Assistant role."

You already know which one that is. You remember the posting, the day you applied, the line you liked in their job description. You know it's been nine days. You know you'd planned to nudge them on Friday.

"Thanks for calling, Marcus," you say, and your voice is steady. "I was hoping to hear from you."

It's a small thing. But it changes the whole conversation. You're not scrambling. You're a person with a plan who happens to be standing next to the rice.

When you get home, you don't spend an hour reconstructing your own history. You make a quick note, tick a box, glance at what's next this week, and close the laptop. The rest of the evening is yours. You cook something that isn't toast.

The job search is still a job search. There'll still be silences and "we've decided to go in another direction." But the fog is gone, and without it the whole thing is lighter. It's easier to be kind to yourself when you can see exactly what you've done.
