First Email to a New Client? The Over-Proved Sourdough

2026-11-08

The dough has been on the counter since eight. It is now 10:40 on Sunday, and it has doubled, then some, and is starting to sag over the rim of the bowl.

Tobias doesn't notice. He is looking at his laptop, where a reply from Friday sits in bold.

"Lovely, we'd like to go ahead. How do we start?"

It's from the owner of a small physiotherapy practice. She wants a new website. It's the best enquiry he has had all quarter, and he has been staring at the reply box for forty minutes.

Four lines that took an hour

He has typed Hi Frau Albrecht, thanks so much, super excited! and deleted it. Too keen. Like someone who can't believe his luck.

He has typed Dear Frau Albrecht, please find below the next steps. and deleted that too. Too stiff. Like a bank.

The cursor blinks. The dough sighs.

Here is the odd part. He has built forty websites. He can talk through a navigation menu with a stranger for an hour and never sweat. But this one email, the one that says welcome, here is how we'll work, makes his shoulders climb toward his ears.

Because it isn't really an email about the website. It's the email where he is supposed to sound like a real business.

What you're really afraid of

Nobody says this out loud, but it's usually something like:

What if I sound too casual and she decides I'm not serious?

What if I sound too formal and she feels she's hired a robot?

What if I forget to mention something, and three weeks from now there's an awkward conversation about it?

What if she's already comparing me to the agency in the next town, and this email is the moment she notices I'm one person at a kitchen table?

So you rewrite it. You rewrite the greeting. You rewrite the sign-off. You open another tab and read how other people write these emails, which makes it worse, because they all sound like they have a receptionist.

Half the morning goes. You haven't written a line of actual work. You've been writing hello.

The bowl, finally

At 11:20 he smells the dough. Properly. Sour, a little boozy, past its best.

He laughs, once, out loud, in the empty kitchen. He tips the bowl toward the bin, then stops and pokes it. It's not ruined. It's just sagging. He folds it a few times, shapes it, puts it in the tin. It will be a flatter loaf than planned. It will still be bread.

Standing there with flour on his wrists, something loosens.

He has been treating this email as a performance. Something that has to be perfect before anyone sees it. But Frau Albrecht doesn't want a performance. She asked a simple question: how do we start? She is a bit nervous too. She has never hired a web designer. She is waiting for someone calm to tell her what happens next.

That's all the email has to be. Calm. Warm. Clear about what happens next.

Not clever. Not impressive.

What if the words were already half there?

He sits back down. This time he doesn't open a blank box and hope. He writes the way he'd talk to her if she were standing in the room: friendly, plain, one idea per paragraph. He says thank you. He says what will happen this week. He says when she'll hear from him next.

It takes eleven minutes.

He reads it once. It sounds like him on a good day. He sends it at 11:47, and the knot behind his ribs, the one he has carried for most of the morning, simply isn't there anymore.

What he thinks, closing the lid, is that it didn't need to cost him an hour. The hard part was never the writing. It was starting from nothing, every single time, with everything riding on the first sentence.

A starting point would have been enough. Something with the greeting already warm, the tone already right, and a couple of gaps to fill in with her name and his dates.

A different Monday

Picture the next one. Not Sunday, not the dread. Just a Monday.

A new enquiry comes in at 9:15 while the coffee is still too hot to drink. You read it, and you feel the small pleasure of yes, I can do this. Then you answer it. Not in an hour. In ten minutes, in your own voice, with the details filled in and nothing important forgotten.

She writes back by lunch. The project starts with an easy first conversation instead of a nervous one. You're not somebody winging it. You're somebody who knows how this goes.

And the sourdough, because it's a Monday and you have time, comes out of the oven a little flat and perfectly good.

If this is you right now
