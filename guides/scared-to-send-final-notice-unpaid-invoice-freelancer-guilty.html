Scared to Send a Final Notice? The Kite Hill Sunday

2026-10-31

The wind on the hill is perfect for kites. Mara has her nephew's red one in one hand and her phone in the other, and she is not watching the sky.

She is watching a draft. The subject line reads Final notice: Invoice 0147. Underneath, three sentences she has rewritten since breakfast. The invoice is for the illustration series she delivered in September. It is now 41 days past its due date.

Her thumb hovers over Send.

"Auntie Mara, it's falling!"

She looks up. The kite is tumbling. She pulls the line, too late, and it folds into the grass. Her nephew laughs. Mara smiles back and feels the knot in her stomach pull tighter.

The word that sticks

Final. That is the word she cannot get past.

Final sounds like a door closing. Final sounds like the client's face when they read it. Maybe they are having a hard month. Maybe their finance person is on leave. Maybe they will think she is cold, or greedy, or not the sweet, easygoing illustrator they hired.

She has already sent two reminders. Both began with "Just a gentle nudge!" and one ended with a smiley. She did not feel like smiling when she typed it.

So she locks the phone and puts it in her coat pocket. The kite goes up again. For ten minutes it almost works. Then the pocket buzzes, which is only her sister, but her whole body jumps anyway.

What the guilt costs

Nobody warns you about this part of working for yourself. The work itself is fine. The drawing is the good bit.

The hard part is the small, private cost around the money. The Sunday you can't quite relax. The way you check your banking app while the kettle boils, then check again. The way you quietly decide not to buy the good paper for the next job, because you don't know yet whether September's money is coming.

And under it all sits a strange shame. You did the work. You delivered on time. You are the one who is owed. Somehow you are the one apologising.

Mara has done the sums in her head so often she could recite them. Rent on the first. The new tablet pen she is not buying. The holiday she is not booking. None of it is dramatic. It is just a low hum that follows her up the hill and back down.

The lunch in the café by the car park

Afterwards they warm up in the little café by the car park. Her nephew has hot chocolate. Her sister, Jo, has the paper. Mara has the phone face-down on the table like something that might bite.

Jo notices anyway. "Still the invoice?"

Mara nods. She explains the draft, the word final, the smiley she regrets.

Jo puts the paper down. "When I got my first proper tenant, I couldn't ask for the rent either. I kept thinking a good person wouldn't make it awkward." She stirs her coffee. "Then I realised the awkward part was never the asking. It was that I had no idea what I was allowed to say next, so every email was a coin toss."

Mara thinks about that on the drive home.

She has never really had a plan for this. Not once. Every reminder has been improvised on the day, in whatever mood she happened to be in, with whatever courage was left. Of course it feels like begging. She is making it up from scratch each time, alone.

The turn

That evening she does not send anything. Instead she opens a blank note and writes one question: What if this had steps?

Not a threat or a performance. A calm order of things: a friendly first note, a clear second, a firm but kind third. Words chosen in advance, on a day when nothing is on fire. A date for each step, so she is never deciding in a panic whether today is the day.

She reads the question back and feels something unclench. If the next step is already written down, sending it is not an act of aggression. It is just the next thing on the list. The client is not being accused. They are being reminded, politely, on schedule, the way anyone reasonable would want to be.

She deletes the word final from the draft, rewrites the message in a plain, steady voice, and saves it for the morning. Then she goes and makes dinner.

A different Monday

Picture the version of this where it has already been sorted out.

Monday, 9:05. Coffee beside the laptop. A small overview shows which invoices are open, which are a few days late, which have crossed a line. Next to the late one there is a note: Send reminder two today. The email is ready, and it sounds like her, only calmer.

She reads it once, changes a name, and presses Send. It takes three minutes. Her shoulders stay where they are.

The afternoon belongs to the drawing. When Sunday comes, she stands on a hill with a red kite and her phone stays in her pocket, because there is nothing in it she needs to dread.

That is all she wanted. Not to become tough, just to stop feeling like she had to beg.
