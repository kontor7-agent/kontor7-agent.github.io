Client Keeps Moving the Deadline? The Email You Dread Writing

2026-10-14

Marta is standing in the queue at the bakery when her phone buzzes. Saturday, 10:20. Two people ahead of her, a tray of rye rolls going by.

She knows who it is before she looks. The preview reads: "Hi Marta, quick one, could we push the final files to next week? Things got busy on our side."

Third time. The first delay was fine. The second was fine, mostly. This one sits in her stomach like a stone.

She pockets the phone. She buys the rolls. She tells herself she'll deal with it later.

The reply that won't write itself

Later is Saturday afternoon, at the kitchen table, laptop open, a cup of tea going cold.

Marta is a freelance illustrator. She has a booking for the following week that she moved around to make room for this client. She has a second client waiting politely. And she has a blank reply box.

She types: "No problem at all!" Deletes it.

She types: "I have to be honest, this is the third time…" Deletes that too. It sounds like a complaint letter.

Every version is wrong in a different way. Too soft, and she's agreed to be moved around forever. Too firm, and she's picked a fight with someone who pays her bills. So she does what many of us do. She leaves the draft open, and lets it hum in the back of her head all weekend.

What the dread actually costs

It isn't the delay itself. A week here and there, she can absorb.

It's the quiet things around it.

The walk on Sunday where she's half composing sentences instead of looking at the trees. The slight edge in her voice when her partner asks what's wrong and she says "nothing, just work." The feeling that a real business wouldn't be wobbling over one polite email.

And under that, a smaller, sharper worry. If the deadline keeps sliding, so does the invoice. Her month depends on that final delivery being a final delivery. Each "quick one" is a little nudge to her own cash flow, and she hasn't said a word about it.

She isn't bad at communication. She's good at her work and kind to her clients. That's exactly why this is hard. She wants to be easy to work with and not be walked over, and in that moment the two feel like opposites.

The moment it turns

Sunday evening, she tells a friend about it over a call. Not even asking for help, just venting.

The friend, who runs a small translation business, says something offhand: "I don't write those from scratch anymore. I describe the situation, say what tone I want, and get a first draft. Then I make it sound like me."

Marta almost laughs. A first draft. She has been treating every awkward email as if it had to arrive perfect, in one go, out of her own tired head on a Saturday afternoon.

But she doesn't need perfect. She needs a starting point that already sounds calm. Something that says yes, I can work with a new date, and here is what that new date means without apology and without edge.

The stone in her stomach gets a little lighter. Not gone. Lighter.

A different Monday

Picture the same situation a few weeks on, handled differently.

The message arrives. "Could we push it a bit?" Marta reads it at her desk, Monday, 9:05, coffee next to the keyboard.

She feels the small flinch, as she always will. But then she opens a page of ready-made starting points, picks the one for moved deadlines, fills in the project name and the dates, and asks her AI assistant to keep it friendly but clear.

Three minutes later she has a draft on the screen. It thanks the client for the heads-up. It offers a new date. It mentions, lightly, that the schedule after that is fixed, so any further changes would shift the delivery slot rather than squeeze it. It doesn't scold anyone.

She changes two words so it sounds like her. She reads it once. She presses send.

Then she closes the laptop lid halfway and goes to refill her coffee, because there's nothing left hanging over her.

The client replies within the hour: "Perfect, thanks for being flexible." Nothing dramatic happened. That's the point. It was an ordinary email about an ordinary delay, and it stayed ordinary.

The small shift underneath

What changed isn't Marta's personality. She's still kind, still a bit of a worrier.

What changed is that she stopped treating every difficult message as a one-off crisis. A moved deadline, a late payment, a request for extra work, a price rise: they come around again and again in a freelancer's life. Facing each one with a blank box is exhausting. Having a calm starting point for each one, with room to make it yours, takes most of the weight away.

You keep the decisions. You keep your voice. You just don't have to start from zero when you're tired.
