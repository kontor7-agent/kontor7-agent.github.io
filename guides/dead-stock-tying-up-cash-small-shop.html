Dead Stock Eating Your Cash? The Sale Table Outside

2026-11-05

It's Saturday, 11:40, and Ines is dragging the sale table out onto the pavement again.

Same table. Same hand-written sign. Same ceramic egg cups in pastel green, the ones she was so sure about three summers ago. She sets them in a neat row, as if neat could make them move.

A man walks past with a coffee. He glances at the table. He keeps walking.

She tells herself it's fine. It's just a slow hour.

But she knows it isn't the hour. It's the egg cups.

The shelf that never changes

Ines runs a small shop for gifts and kitchen things. Twelve years of choosing, unpacking and arranging. She is good at it. Customers say the place feels like a friend's living room.

What they don't see is the back room.

There is a stack of boxes behind the door that she steps around without looking. A shelf of candle holders from a trade fair she got a little carried away at. Tea towels with a pattern she loved and nobody else did. Every item was a reasonable decision on the day she ordered it.

Now they just sit there, quietly holding her money.

That is the part nobody says out loud. A box on a shelf feels like an asset. But when the rent is due, you can't pay it with egg cups.

The call she doesn't want to make

At 14:10 her phone buzzes. It's her supplier, friendly as always. Autumn range is ready. Would she like to reorder the linen napkins before the price goes up?

The napkins are a bestseller. She knows that. She also knows she ran out of them in the first week of October and has no clear idea how many she sold, or when, or what she should have kept on the shelf.

"Can I come back to you tomorrow?" she says.

She hangs up and stands behind the till, looking at the shop. All those full shelves. And yet the napkins, the thing people actually ask for, are gone.

That's the strange, unfair arithmetic of it. Too much of what doesn't sell. Too little of what does. Both at once, and both costing her.

Where the knot sits

She doesn't talk about it at home. Her partner thinks the shop is doing fine, because it looks busy.

But Ines feels it in small moments. When she hesitates before ordering something new because the account is thin. When she says yes to a discount she can't afford because at least it turns a box into cash. When she wakes at five and does the sum in her head, and the sum never comes out the same twice.

It isn't panic. It's a low, steady unease. The feeling of running a real business with a notebook and a hunch.

That evening she sits on the back-room floor with the lights on and counts the egg cups. Thirty-one. She has a vague memory of buying forty-eight.

She laughs, a bit tiredly. She isn't even sure how many she's sold.

The moment it shifts

Nothing dramatic happens. No lightning bolt.

She just realises, sitting there among the boxes, that the problem was never her taste. Her taste is fine. The problem is that she can't see her stock. Not what's moving, not what's sleeping, not what she should reorder before it runs out.

She's been deciding in the dark. And the dark is expensive.

A small, plain system would change that. One place where every item has a number, a reorder point, a value. Not a grand piece of software. Just the shelf, written down honestly, so she can look at it in two minutes instead of guessing.

She thinks about that for a while. It doesn't feel like a big idea. It feels like a door that was unlocked all along.

A different Monday

Picture it a few weeks later.

Monday, 8:30. Coffee on the counter, shutter still half down. Ines opens one file and looks at it for a few minutes. She can see that the napkins are getting low, and that it's time to order, calmly, before anyone is disappointed. She can see that the pastel egg cups have barely moved, so she doesn't reorder them, and she has a quiet plan for the rest: a bundle, a gift set, a fair price.

She can also see how much money is sitting in the back room. Not as a guilty feeling, but as a number.

When the supplier calls, she doesn't ask for a day to think. She knows what she needs.

The sale table comes out on Saturday, but only when she chooses it, and with something she actually wants to clear.

The shop looks the same. The shelves aren't fuller. They're just right. And somewhere in the back of her mind, the five a.m. sum has gone quiet.
