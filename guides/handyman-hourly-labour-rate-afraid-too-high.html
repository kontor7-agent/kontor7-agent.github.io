Handyman Hourly Rate Too High? The Village Hall Chairs

2026-11-02

The village hall smells of floor polish and old tea. It is Wednesday, 19:10, and you are sitting on a folding chair that is too small for you, with a committee of six looking at you kindly.

They want the stage door fixed. The hinge is sagging, the frame has a bit of rot, and the lock hasn't caught since spring. You looked at it ten minutes ago. Half a day, maybe a bit more.

Then the chairwoman smiles and says, "So, what do you charge an hour?"

Everyone goes quiet. Someone's spoon clinks against a cup.

In your head, three numbers appear at once. The one you wish you could say. The one your last customer paid. And a smaller one, the one that feels safe.

The number that comes out of your mouth

"Oh, I don't mind. Whatever's fair."

That is not an answer, and you know it. The chairwoman nods, relieved, and moves on to the raffle. Nobody has done anything wrong. They are volunteers who raise money with cake sales. You can feel the thought sitting heavily in your chest: if I say the real number, they'll think I'm robbing them.

So you say something lower. Almost a whisper of a figure. They look pleased. You smile back.

Driving home, the van is quiet. You keep doing the sum you didn't do in the hall. Fuel. The drill that died in June. The days nobody rings. The hours you spend driving to a job, buying the screws, writing the message afterwards. None of that was in the number you just said.

You are a good handyman. People tell you so. But you are three years into working alone, and nobody ever sat you down and showed you how an hourly rate is meant to be built.

What the fear is really about

It's rarely about the money itself. It's about being judged.

You are afraid of the pause on the other end of the phone. Of being the expensive one. Of someone telling their friend, "He's good, but he's not cheap." So you round down, and down again, and tell yourself it's good for business.

Meanwhile the quiet cost builds up. A Saturday spent fixing a gate for a rate that wouldn't cover a decent lunch. A small resentment at a customer who did nothing but accept the price you offered. Sunday evenings with a notebook, wondering why a busy week still leaves the account so thin.

Being busy and being paid fairly are different things. It took you a while to notice that.

The turning point is small

It doesn't arrive with a drumroll. It arrives on a Thursday, at the kitchen table, with a mug of tea and a pen.

You start with a different question. Not "what will they accept?" but "what does one hour of my work actually have to carry?" The van. The tools. The unpaid driving. The slow weeks. The week off you'd like to take in August without panic.

Seen that way, the number isn't greedy. It's just honest. It isn't a speech you give a customer. It's a figure you know, the way you know the length of your own tape measure.

And something changes in your shoulders. Knowing your number is not the same as shouting it. It simply means that when someone asks, you aren't guessing.

Nobody in the hall was ever going to take your head off. What you were afraid of was the gap between what you needed and what you could say out loud. A figure you trust closes that gap.

A different Wednesday

Picture the same folding chair, the same tea, the same question from the chairwoman.

"So, what do you charge an hour?"

You don't flinch. You say your rate in the same voice you'd use to say the door needs a new frame. You add that you'll write it down for the committee, with the materials listed separately, so they can see exactly what they're paying for.

She nods. Someone asks whether you can start before the autumn show. You say yes.

The room stays warm. Nobody gasps. The raffle carries on.

On Monday morning, the quote is already done. You open it on the tailgate before the first job, check the materials line, and see the margin sitting there plainly. Not a feeling, a figure. You drive off with your coffee still hot, and the day isn't loud with doubt.

At six you're home. The receipts for the hinges are already logged. You know who owes you and who doesn't. The evening is yours.
