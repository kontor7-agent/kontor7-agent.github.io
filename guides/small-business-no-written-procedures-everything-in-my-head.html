No Written Procedures? The Helper Who Asked 'What Now?'

2026-10-24

The bell over the door rings at 8:57.

Marta is behind the counter of her small online-and-market shop, the back room stacked with mugs, tape, and boxes. She sells handmade ceramics and gifts. Today is the first day of Jonas, a part-timer she hired three weeks ago. She has been looking forward to this. She has also been dreading it.

"Morning," he says, and puts his bag down. "So. Where do you want me to start?"

She opens her mouth. Nothing comes out.

This is a story we made up, but you may know the feeling.

The sentence that won't come out

It's not that Marta doesn't know what to do. She knows everything. Which supplier is slow in October. Which customers want their parcel wrapped twice. Why the blue glaze goes on the top shelf. What she says when someone writes that the mug arrived chipped.

It's all in there, somewhere behind her eyes.

But "where do I start" has no one-line answer. Every task she thinks of drags three more behind it. First the orders, but check the stock count, but the count is off because of the market on Saturday, but that's in a notebook, which is in the van.

"Let me show you," she says. And she does. She talks fast, walks from shelf to shelf, and pulls things out of her memory while Jonas nods politely and tries to keep up.

By ten, his phone is out. He's taking photos of the shelves, because there's nothing else to write down.

The quiet cost of knowing everything

Marta has never called it a problem. It was just how she works. Alone, it worked well enough.

But around eleven, with Jonas packing a box slightly differently from how she would, a few small truths surface.

She has not had a full weekend off in two years, because nobody else knows how Saturday works.

She once took a holiday and spent it checking her phone by the pool. "Quick question, where's the label printer password?" Three messages a day. She called it a break. It wasn't.

She has answered the same customer question forty times from scratch, each time slightly differently, each time as if for the first.

And under all of it sits a thought she doesn't say aloud: If I got ill for a week, this would stop. Not slow down. Stop.

That doesn't feel like owning a business. It feels like being the business.

The question behind the question

At lunch Jonas sits on an upturned crate with a sandwich.

"Can I ask something?" he says. "Is there a place where it's written down? Not a big manual. Just, like, how you do the refund thing. Or what you say when a parcel is late. I don't want to ask you every ten minutes."

He isn't complaining. He's being kind. He wants to be useful without being a burden.

Marta looks at him and laughs, a little tiredly. "No. It's all in my head."

"Okay," he says. "Could it go somewhere else?"

That's the whole turning point. Not a crisis. Not a lost client. Just a quiet question over a sandwich, and the sudden thought that her head was never meant to be the filing cabinet.

She had always imagined writing procedures as a giant, boring project. Binders. Flowcharts. A month of weekends. So she never started.

But Jonas didn't ask for a binder. He asked how the refund thing works.

One thing. Written in plain words. Then the next.

A different Monday

Picture the same shop a few weeks on.

It is Monday, 8:55. Jonas comes in, hangs up his jacket, and opens a page that says what the week looks like. Which orders go out first. How a late parcel gets answered. Who to email when the clay delivery slips.

Marta is still there, with her coffee, but she isn't narrating. She's answering the one real question of the morning instead of forty small ones.

When a customer writes about a chipped mug, the reply isn't invented from scratch. It's already half there, in her own warm voice, and she changes two words.

And when she says "I'll be away Thursday and Friday," nobody panics. Not Jonas, not her.

It doesn't make the business run itself. It simply moves the knowledge out of one tired head and onto a page where two people can use it. The knot in the stomach loosens a little. You become the owner again, not the memory.
