Forget to Bill Retainer Hours? The Glass Meeting Room

2026-10-19

Monday, 10:02. You are in a glass meeting room that isn't yours, on a chair that is slightly too low, and the client's operations lead has just said the sentence you were hoping for.

"We love having you on the retainer. Honestly, we don't think about that part of the business anymore."

You smile. You say thank you. Under the table, your thumb presses into the side of your finger.

Because you know something she doesn't. The retainer covers twenty hours a month. You have not worked twenty hours a month. Some months it was closer to thirty. The extra went into quick fixes, a Slack message answered on a Saturday, a landing page "while you were in there", and none of it has ever reached an invoice.

Let's call you Mira. You build websites for small companies. This client was your first steady one, and the retainer felt like a lifeline: the same amount, the same day, every month.

The hours that went missing

Nobody decided to give the work away. That's the strange part.

It happened in small, reasonable steps. A request came in on a Tuesday, took forty minutes, and felt too small to write down. Another came on a Thursday while you were already in the project. You told yourself you'd add it up at month end.

Month end arrived in the middle of other things. You sent the usual invoice for the usual amount, because that was easy and the client never asked. The extra hours stayed in your head, and your head is not a ledger.

So now, in a glass room with a bowl of lemon sweets on the table, you're doing arithmetic you've avoided for a long time. Three months? Five? You honestly can't say. That is the part that stings most. It isn't just that you gave hours away. You don't even know how many.

What it costs you quietly

It costs money, of course. But the money is almost the smaller part.

It costs the feeling that you're running a real business. A real business knows what it did last month. You know roughly. You know "a lot".

It costs you a little resentment, the kind that comes out sideways. You answer the Saturday message with a bright exclamation mark and then close the laptop with a bit too much force. You like this client. You don't want to start disliking them for something you never put on paper.

And it costs you the conversation you can't have. How do you say "I think I've been doing more than we agreed" when you have no dates, no numbers, nothing but a feeling? You'd sound vague. Worse, you'd sound like you were complaining about work you chose to do.

So you say nothing. You nod. You take a lemon sweet you don't want.

The walk back to the car

Outside, the car park smells of wet tarmac. You sit in the driver's seat without starting the engine.

The thought that arrives isn't dramatic. It's almost gentle. This isn't a character flaw. You aren't careless, and you aren't bad with money. Nothing was ever set up to catch these hours. They had nowhere to land.

You'd been treating it as a memory problem, as if you needed to try harder to remember. But a retainer is a promise with a number inside it, and a number needs a place where it's counted. One spot for the hours you agreed. One spot for the hours you actually spent. Next to each other, so you can see the gap before the month closes instead of three months after.

That's all. No big system. Just a place where the hours go, and an invoice that knows about them.

A different Monday

Picture the same meeting room a few months from now.

The operations lead says the same warm thing about not having to think about your part of the business. You smile, and this time it's a real smile. You know where you stand. Last month: twenty-four hours against twenty. You noted it on the day, not at the end of the month. The extra four are already on a line of their own, with a clear, friendly explanation you've been sending for weeks.

Maybe the client says yes without a pause. Maybe they ask to adjust the retainer to twenty-five hours. Maybe they say the small requests can wait. All three outcomes are fine, because for the first time there's something real on the table to talk about. Numbers make a calm conversation. Feelings make an awkward one.

And on the drive home you aren't doing sums in your head at red lights. Your Sunday evening is just Sunday evening.
