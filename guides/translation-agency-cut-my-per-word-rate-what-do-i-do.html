Agency Cut Your Per-Word Rate? The Meter Reader Visit

2026-11-08

The email arrives at 8:52 on a Monday, between a newsletter and a reminder about your dentist.

Subject line: Update to our rate structure.

Marta reads it standing up, one hand still on the kettle. Three friendly paragraphs. Words like "alignment" and "market conditions". And then, tucked in the middle of the second one, the sentence that matters: the per-word rate for her main language pair is going down, effective next month.

She reads it again. It doesn't change.

Marta is an illustrative character, but you may know her. You may be her.

The doorbell

She has not yet answered the email when the doorbell rings. It is the man who reads the gas meter, once a year, always on a day she has deadlines.

She leads him to the cupboard under the stairs. He crouches, writes down a number, and says something cheerful about the weather. She nods. Her mind is somewhere else entirely, in a spreadsheet she doesn't have.

Because here is the thought that has followed her from the kitchen: Is this a lot, or a little?

She has no idea. She knows the old rate by heart. She knows the new one now. What she doesn't know is what either of them has ever meant per hour. Some jobs go fast. Some are full of fuzzy matches and half-translated memory segments that she fixes line by line. Some arrive on Friday at four with "ASAP" in the subject.

The meter man says goodbye. The door clicks shut.

She stands in the hallway with the strange feeling of having been asked a question she can't answer. Not by him. By the numbers.

The reply you write in your head

Back at the desk, the replies begin. They arrive in the mind fully formed, the way they do.

The angry one: After six years, this is how you treat people?

The grateful one: Of course, thank you for letting me know! (Which she would never send, and which scares her a little, because part of her is already tempted.)

The cold one. The brave one. The one that just says "understood" and swallows it.

None of them is built on anything. That is the quiet problem. When you don't know your real numbers, every conversation about money turns into a conversation about feelings. And the agency, whatever else it is, has numbers. It has a spreadsheet somewhere with her name in a row.

She thinks about the agency that paid sixty days late last spring, and how she could never quite remember which invoice it had been. She thinks about the discount for repetitions she accepted without checking what it did to a normal day. She thinks about the evenings she gave to a rush job without ever asking what the rush was worth.

It isn't that she lacks skill. It's that she has never been able to see the whole picture in one place.

What you actually need before you answer

Not a speech. Not a ultimatum. Not a dramatic exit.

What Marta needs is a calm sentence she can say because it's true. Something like: Here is what this agency means for my month, and here is what I can accept. Or: I can keep this work, but not at this rate for this kind of text. Or even: I'll gently move my time toward clients who pay better.

Any of those is a decision. Any of them belongs to her. But she can only make one when she can see which jobs are worth it, which agencies pay on time, and what a good day really pays per hour once the fuzzy matches and the rush weekends are counted honestly.

This is not a crisis. It is a Monday with a missing view.

The same Monday, a little later

Picture it differently. The email arrives at 8:52. She reads it, feels the small drop in her stomach, and then opens one file.

In it, the last months are already laid out. This agency, that agency. Word counts, discounts, rush fees, the real hours behind each job, and the date each invoice was finally paid. She glances at the row for this client and sees what she has always half-known and never quite proved.

Maybe the news is better than she feared. Maybe it is worse. Either way, it is clear, and clear is a very different feeling from dread.

She makes a second coffee. She writes a short, polite, steady reply, one she is willing to stand behind whatever comes back. She sends it at 9:30 and moves on to the text due at noon.

The afternoon is just an afternoon. The meter man's number is in a notebook somewhere. The agency's reply, when it comes, is not a verdict. It is the start of a conversation between two people who both have numbers.

That is what a small system buys you. Not victory. Just footing.
