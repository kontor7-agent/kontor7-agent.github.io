Client Wants More Revisions Than Agreed? The Pottery Class Pause

2026-10-15

It is Thursday, 12:40, and you are standing in a pottery studio's tiny kitchen with a paper cup of tea going cold. Your phone buzzes against the counter.

The client has written. The subject line says: Just one tiny thing.

You already know. It is round four. The contract, the quote, your own careful email in March all said two rounds of revisions. You are on your fourth, and the "tiny thing" is moving the entire layout around.

You read it once, standing up. Your stomach does that small drop. Then your thumb hovers over the reply box and you think the thought you always think: It would be quicker to just do it.

The yes that costs a Saturday

Let's call you Mira. You design brochures and websites for small businesses, and you took this pottery class on Thursdays because you promised yourself one hour a week that is not a screen.

The hour is almost over, and the client's message is sitting inside it like a stone in a shoe.

So you do what you always do. You say yes. "Of course, happy to adjust!" Three exclamation marks, so it sounds easy and cheerful.

It isn't easy. It will take most of Saturday morning. And the part nobody sees is the small resentment that comes with it. You are quietly adding up the hours you won't bill. You are making the project less profitable, and you are making yourself a little less fond of a client you actually like.

That is the worry underneath the worry. It isn't really about the revision. It's about whether saying no will make you look difficult. Whether they'll leave. Whether a "real" professional would just absorb it.

What the pause is really made of

You have drafted the polite no before. Probably three times this year.

"Per our agreement, additional revisions are billed at..." Too stiff. Delete.

"I'd love to help, but..." Too apologetic. Delete.

"Sure, no problem!" Sent. Regretted.

The trouble is not that you don't know what you want to say. You know. The trouble is that the words have to carry two things at once: a firm boundary and real warmth. And at 12:40 on a Thursday, with a cold tea and a ticking clock, you can't find the tone.

So the boundary slides. Not because you are weak. Because wording a good no, again, alone, in the gaps of your day, is genuinely tiring.

The turning point, in a studio kitchen

The teacher of the class, a woman with clay up to her elbows, comes in to refill the kettle. She sees your face and asks if everything is alright.

"Client wants a fourth round of changes," you say. "We agreed two."

She shrugs, not unkindly. "So tell them what two rounds got them, and what a third would look like. People usually just want to know where the edges are."

That is all. Nothing clever. But it lands, because it reframes the whole thing. You are not refusing. You are showing the edges. A client who has never been told where the edges are will keep walking until they find one.

And you realise the hard part was never the idea. It was the sentence. Finding the right first line, in a calm voice, every single time.

What if you didn't have to start from a blank box? What if the sentence were already warm, already clear, already yours, and all you did was change the names and the details?

The same Thursday, a different way

Imagine it again.

The message arrives. Your stomach, for once, stays where it is. You open a short note you keep for exactly this, adjust two lines so it sounds like you, and read it aloud under your breath.

It thanks them. It says how much you like where the project has landed. It says the two agreed rounds are now complete, and that further changes are happily done as an add-on, with a simple way to go ahead. No lecture. No apology. No exclamation-mark armour.

You press send, pick up your tea, and, still warm enough to drink, it tastes like tea again.

Maybe the client says yes to the add-on. Maybe they say the last version was fine after all. Maybe they go quiet for a day. Most of the time, the relationship survives a clearly stated edge. Often it improves, because people tend to relax when they know where they stand.

And your Saturday stays yours.

What a calm Monday looks like

It isn't dramatic. That is the whole point.

On Monday, a client asks for "one more small round" and you don't flinch. You don't rehearse in the shower. You don't draft at 22:40 and reread in bed.

You take a minute, send a kind and clear reply, and get on with the work that actually needs you. Your boundaries stop being a mood and start being a habit. You feel, quietly, like someone who runs a real business, because you do.
