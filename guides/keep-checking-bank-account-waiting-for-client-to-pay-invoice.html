Checking Your Bank for a Client's Payment? The Bus Tour

2026-10-31

It is 11:20 on a Saturday, and you are sitting on the top deck of an open-top bus in a city you have lived in for six years.

Your mother is next to you in her good jacket. She has wanted to do this tour since she arrived on Thursday. The audio guide says something about a cathedral and a bridge. She leans out to take a photo.

Your thumb is already on your phone. Banking app. Pull down. The little wheel spins.

Nothing. Same balance as ten minutes ago, and the same as at breakfast.

The refresh that doesn't refresh anything

Lena, let's call her that, is a freelance designer. She isn't real, but you may know her. She sent the invoice for the brand work eighteen days ago. The due date was last Saturday. Since then she has checked the account at the kettle, at the bus stop and in the bathroom, and once before she was properly awake.

She knows it makes no difference. The money won't arrive faster because she is watching. But the checking has become a small ritual, like touching a pocket to make sure the keys are still there.

"Everything alright?" her mother asks, still looking at the cathedral.

"Yes. Just work."

It is not just work. It is rent on the first. It is a tax prepayment she doesn't want to think about. It is the quiet arithmetic she runs in the background of every conversation: if it comes Monday, fine. If it comes in two weeks, I move things around. If it doesn't come...

She never finishes that last sentence.

What the waiting actually costs

The invoice isn't the only thing that is late. Her attention is late too. She is on a bus with her mother, who has travelled five hours to be here, and half of Lena is in a banking app.

She also hasn't written to the client. She has written to them, kind of. Four drafts sit in her mailbox. One starts with "Just a gentle reminder" and is so gentle it apologises for existing. One sounds almost angry. One she wrote at 6:40 this morning and deleted before the coffee finished.

Something in her treats the reminder as a verdict. If she asks, she becomes the person who doesn't trust her clients. If she doesn't ask, she becomes the person who is quietly funding someone else's business. Neither feels good. So she does the third thing: she checks the account and waits.

People who work for themselves rarely say this out loud, but waiting for money is lonely. There is no finance department to ask. No colleague across the desk to say "Oh, they're always slow, just nudge them." There is only you, a balance and a feeling that you should be above all this.

The bus stops at a red light

The bus pauses at a junction. The guide drones on about a market square. Lena's mother turns and looks at her properly.

"You've looked at that phone nine times since the station."

"Eleven," Lena says, and laughs, because what else is there to do.

"Is someone not paying you?"

It is the first time anyone has asked it that plainly. And Lena realises the problem is not that the client is late. Clients are sometimes late. The problem is that she has no plan for what happens next. There is no "on this day I write this, and on that day I write that." There is only dread, a refresh button and four unsent drafts.

A plan would not make the money arrive sooner. It would only mean she didn't have to hold the whole thing in her head on a Saturday morning, on a bus, next to her mother.

The same Saturday, a different way

Imagine the same bus. The same cathedral, the same slightly crackly audio guide.

But somewhere in a tidy list at home there is a line for this client. It says what is overdue, by how many days, and what the sensible next step is. Maybe that step is a friendly note on Monday. Maybe it's a firmer one a week later. Either way the words already exist, calm and polite and clear, and nobody had to invent them at 6:40 in the morning.

So Lena's phone stays in her pocket. Not because she has stopped caring, but because the caring has somewhere to go. The waiting has a shape now. It has a next date.

She would watch her mother point at a pigeon on the cathedral roof. She'd buy two coffees at the next stop. On Monday at nine she'd open the list, see the step, send the note, and go back to her real work.

Nothing dramatic. The money might still take a few days. But the knot in her stomach would be smaller, because the situation would no longer be a secret she carries alone. It would just be a line on a page, with a next step beside it.

Back on the top deck

In the real version of the story, Lena puts the phone away at the next stop, mostly out of embarrassment. She enjoys the second half of the tour about as well as anyone can with a loose thread tugging at her.

On the way home she tells her mother, "I think I need a better way of doing this part."

Her mother says, "Good. You've been checking that phone since you were twenty-six."

That is the whole turning point. No revelation, no big overhaul. Just the quiet recognition that chasing money doesn't have to be a mood. It can be a routine, as ordinary as watering a plant.
