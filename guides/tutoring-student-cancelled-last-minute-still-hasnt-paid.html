Student Cancelled Again and Still Unpaid? The Red Man

2026-10-20

The pedestrian light is red, and it stays red for a long time.

Marta stands at the crossing with her tote bag cutting into her shoulder. It is Thursday, 13:05. She has just walked out of a café where she was meant to meet a student for a 13:00 lesson. Her phone buzzes in her hand.

Hi Marta, so sorry, something came up at work. Can we skip today? Next week?

The message arrived at 12:58. She was already sitting at the table, with the book open and a flat white going cold.

This is the third time this month.

And then the other thought arrives, the one that has been circling for days. Didn't he also owe her for the last three lessons? Or was it four? Did he pay the October ones and just not September? She isn't sure. That is the part that makes her stomach tighten.

The light stays red.

The question you can't answer at a crossing

Marta teaches English to adults: people preparing for job interviews, a woman moving abroad next spring, a man who wants to stop dreading international calls. She is good at it. Her students say so, and they keep coming back.

But standing there, she cannot say what she is owed.

She knows roughly. She keeps lesson dates in her calendar, payments are scattered across her bank app and two payment apps, and a few people pay in cash with a smile and an "I'll give you the rest next time." In her head there is a loose picture, like a drawer that won't quite close.

She also can't say how many lessons were cancelled late this month, or what those empty hours cost her. She just knows that her week has holes in it, and that the holes always seem to land on the same days.

The light turns green. She doesn't move for a second.

What it quietly costs

It isn't only the money, although the money matters. An hour that is cancelled at 12:58 is an hour she cannot sell to anyone else. She was ready, and she had prepared. She will not be paid for it, and she will feel too awkward to say so.

Then there is the other cost. Marta rewrites the same message in her head: Hi, just a quick note about the lessons we've had so far… She deletes it. It sounds like begging. It sounds like she is keeping score on someone who is only having a hard week.

So she says nothing. She replies, No problem, see you next week! and adds a smiley face, because that is what a nice person does.

And she carries the knot home with her. It sits next to the dinner she is cooking and the show she is half-watching. It is there on Sunday evening when she opens her laptop and tries to work out who still owes her.

The turning point is small

Here is what changes things for her, and it isn't dramatic.

A few weeks later, she sits down on a Monday morning with a mug of tea, the window open, and a quiet kitchen. She has one place where each lesson lives: who, when, whether it happened, whether it was cancelled late, and whether it has been paid. She does not have to piece it together from three apps and her memory.

She types in last week's lessons. It takes a few minutes. The overview fills in, and she can see it.

There is one name with a gentle flag next to it. Not a red alarm, just a clear fact: three lessons taught, none paid yet.

Something in her shoulders drops.

It is not that the problem has vanished. It is that she is no longer guessing. She knows the number. And a message with a real number in it is easy to write, because it is not an accusation, only a clear note between two adults: Here's where we are. Shall I send the invoice again?

A normal Thursday, afterwards

Picture the same crossing, the same Thursday, the same buzz in her hand.

So sorry, something came up. Can we skip today?

She still feels the small sting. But she opens her log and marks the lesson as a late cancellation. She sees, in plain numbers, how many of those she has had this month and what they added up to. For the first time, she can decide calmly how she wants to handle it: a gentle reminder of her cancellation terms, or a conversation about moving his slot to a time that actually suits him.

It's her decision, made in daylight, not at 23:00 with her heart racing.

The light turns green. She crosses. She buys a pastry she doesn't strictly need, because she suddenly has a free hour and she knows exactly where she stands.

That is the whole difference. The students are the same, the cancellations are the same, and the world is no more punctual than it was. But she can see it, and so it stops being a fog she walks through.
