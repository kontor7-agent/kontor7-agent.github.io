Candles Online, Any Profit After Fees? The Bottle Bank

2026-10-18

It is 10:20 on a Saturday morning, and you are standing at the bottle bank with a tote bag of clinking glass.

They are not empty wine bottles. They are the jars you ordered in the wrong size, the ones that cracked in a test pour, the ones that never quite sat right with the lid. Each one makes a hollow clunk as it drops into the bin.

Your phone buzzes in your coat pocket. You know the sound. It is the payout notification from the shop platform.

You pull it out with one glove still on. A number sits there, smaller than you hoped.

And the thought arrives, the one you have been walking around for weeks: I sold a lot of candles this month. So why does it feel like I have nothing?

The question you don't ask out loud

You sell candles. Soy wax, cedar and fig, a winter one with orange peel that people keep reordering. The reviews are kind. The orders keep coming.

By any visible measure, it is going well.

But at the bottle bank, with the last jar in your hand, you realise you could not answer a simple question if someone asked it. Do you actually make money on these?

You know what a jar costs. You roughly know the wax. The wicks, the labels, the little box, the tissue paper you bought because it looked right. The platform takes a bit. The payment processor takes a bit. Postage is partly covered by the customer, partly by you. Then there is the free sample you slip into every parcel because it feels like good manners.

Each cost is small. Together they are a fog.

You priced the candles once, months ago, by looking at what other makers charged and picking a number that felt brave but not too brave. You have not looked at it since.

What it quietly costs

The cost is not only money, though maybe money too.

It is the way you hesitate when a friend says, "You should do this full time!" You smile and change the subject, because you cannot say whether that would work.

It is the market stall where someone asks for a discount on three candles and you say yes too fast. You are not sure whether you just gave away your margin or only a little goodwill.

It is the Sunday you spend pouring until your back aches, with a small voice asking whether the hours are even paying you back.

And it is that feeling nobody mentions in the maker groups: I'm busy, but I might not be running a business. I might be running an expensive hobby that other people kindly buy from.

That is a lonely thought to have next to a bottle bank.

The turning point

You close the lid on the bin. The glass settles. For a moment it is quiet.

And something small shifts. You realise the fog is not a sign that you are bad at this. It is just that nobody ever put the pieces side by side for you. The jar, the wax, the fee, the postage, the sample. All of it sitting in different places: a notes app, a supplier email, a platform dashboard, your head.

The problem is not your candles. It is that the numbers have never been in one room together.

That thought is almost a relief. A fog can be cleared. You don't need to become an accountant. You just need to see, for each candle you make, what it costs, what the fees take, and what is left.

One product at a time. Nothing dramatic.

A different kind of Saturday

Imagine the same bottle bank a few weeks from now.

Same tote bag, same clink of glass. The phone buzzes with a payout, and this time you read the number and nod. You know roughly which candles carry the month and which ones you love but should price a little differently. You know what a market day really brings in compared with an online order.

When someone at the stall asks for a deal, you can decide. Not out of panic, but because you can see what the deal costs you. Sometimes you say yes. Sometimes you smile and say, "That's already my best price." Both feel fine.

The Sunday pouring session feels different too. It is still work, but it is work with a point you can see.

You drive home with the radio on. There is no knot in your stomach waiting for you at the kitchen table. The evening is simply yours.

If you recognised yourself

You don't have to wait for a bad month or a tax deadline to find out where you stand. Sometimes it is enough to sit down once, put the costs in one place, and let the picture fill in.
