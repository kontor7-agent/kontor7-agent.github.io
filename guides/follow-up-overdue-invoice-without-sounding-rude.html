Overdue Invoice Follow-Up Without Sounding Desperate

2026-10-16

Imagine a freelance illustrator we'll call Tomás. It's Thursday, 12:40, and he's standing in the queue at the farmers' market cheese stall. Six people ahead of him. His phone is in his hand, and he isn't looking at cheese.

He's looking at an email draft that begins: "Hi Jan, just a gentle little reminder, no stress at all, but..."

The invoice is nineteen days overdue. Eleven hundred euros. The rent is not a theoretical idea this month.

He deletes "no stress at all." The sentence is a lie. He's quite stressed.

Then he deletes "gentle little." That sounds like begging. He types "As per my previous email," and winces, because that sounds like a man sharpening a knife.

(This is an illustrative scene, not a real person. But you may know it from the inside.)

The two voices in your head

When an invoice goes overdue, most of us hear two voices.

The first says: Don't be difficult. They're probably busy. If you push, they'll never hire you again.

The second says: You did the work. You delivered on time. Why are you apologising for asking to be paid?

Both are loud. Neither writes a good email. So the draft swings between grovelling and furious, and in the end you do the thing that feels safest: nothing. You close the laptop. You tell yourself you'll look again on Monday.

Monday comes. You don't look.

What the silence quietly costs

The unpaid invoice is only the visible part.

There's the small knot that shows up whenever the client's name appears in your inbox. There's the checking of your bank app in odd places: the bus, the queue, the bathroom at a family dinner. There's the new job you take on for the same client while the old one is still unpaid, because refusing feels too awkward.

There's also the strange shame of it. You run a business, and you can't ask a person for what they owe you. It makes you feel like someone pretending to be self-employed.

None of that is a character flaw. You're just writing an important message from scratch, in a bad mood, with no plan for what comes next. That would make anyone freeze.

The cheese stall

Back at the market, Tomás reaches the front.

"What can I get you?"

He asks for two hundred grams of the hard, nutty one. The stallholder cuts it, weighs it, wraps it. No drama. She doesn't apologise for the price. She doesn't explain herself. She says the number, he taps his card, the little machine beeps.

It's the simplest transaction in the world. Something was handed over, so something is handed back.

He walks away with the paper bag and thinks something he hadn't quite thought before: Why does it feel so different when it's an email?

The answer, he realises, is that at the cheese stall there's a system. There's a price. There's a moment when you pay. Nobody has to invent the social rules on the spot.

With his invoice, there's nothing like that. Every reminder is improvised. Every reminder feels like a personal confrontation, because he has no sense of where he is in the process. Is this the first nudge? The third? When is it fair to be firmer? He doesn't know, so every email carries the weight of all of them at once.

What changes when the next step is already decided

The turning point isn't a cleverer sentence.

It's the idea that a payment reminder is a step, not a verdict on the relationship. Day one after the due date: a short, friendly note. A little later: a clear, still-friendly follow-up with the invoice attached again. Later still: a firmer one that names a date. Each step is already decided before you feel anything about it.

When the order is settled, the wording gets easier too. You aren't deciding whether you're allowed to ask. You're just doing what comes next.

And it's quieter than you'd expect. A calm reminder doesn't sound rude. It sounds like someone who runs a tidy business, because that is exactly what it is. Most clients, honestly, just forgot. An invoice slipped under a pile, a payment run missed, a colleague on holiday. A clear, kind note gets it moving again.

A normal Thursday, afterwards

Picture the same market, a few weeks later.

Tomás is in the same queue. His phone is in his pocket. He doesn't take it out.

Somewhere in a file at home, there's a simple list of what's open, what's due, and what the next step is for each. He looked at it on Monday morning with his coffee. Two items needed a nudge, so he sent two short emails in about four minutes and then got on with drawing.

No draft was rewritten nine times. No word like "desperate" or "rude" crossed his mind. He didn't feel braver. He just felt organised, and it turns out that feels a lot like being brave.

The cheese is good. The bank app can wait until tonight.
