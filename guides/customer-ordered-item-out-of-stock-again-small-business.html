Customer Ordered, Item Out of Stock Again: The Saturday Market Buzz

2026-10-12

The phone buzzes in your apron pocket just as a woman asks whether the green comes in a bigger skein.

You smile at her. You say you'll check. Then you glance down at the screen, because you always glance down.

New order. Indigo Dusk, 3 skeins.

Your stomach does the small drop it has learned to do. You turn your head toward the crate under the table, the one that held the indigo yarn this morning. You already know what's in it. Tissue paper and a price tag.

The last skein left an hour ago, in a paper bag, to a man who said it was a present. You were happy about that. You are still happy about that. But now there is a stranger at home with a confirmation email, waiting for three skeins that do not exist.

The sentence you start writing in your head

The woman with the green skein is still there. You find her a bigger one. You wrap it. You take her money and say thank you, and the whole time another part of you is drafting an email.

Hi, so sorry, it seems that…

It always starts the same way. It seems. As if the shelf did something to you.

The truth is that the website said "in stock" because you typed a number into it three weeks ago, after the last dye day. Since then you've sold at two markets, filled four online orders, and given one skein to your sister. The number in your head and the number on the site drifted apart quietly, like two clocks nobody wound.

Nobody did anything wrong. That's what makes it so tiring.

What it costs, quietly

The refund isn't the expensive part. Refunds are easy.

The expensive part is the small lurch in the chest each time the phone buzzes. It's the stall owner next to you asking if you're okay, and you saying "fine, just a work thing" while your hands go a bit cold. It's the Sunday you spend counting boxes in the spare room instead of doing anything else, because you don't trust what you think you know.

It's also the other, stranger cost. You've started to wonder whether you look like a real shop. You picture the customer reading your apology and thinking, oh, one of those. A hobby with a checkout button.

You've worked hard to be more than that. You dye at six in the morning with the window open. You've turned down a wholesale offer because you wanted to keep the colours right. It stings that a miscounted shelf can make all of that look careless.

The email, and the quiet after it

At the end of the day you carry the folding table to the car, and you write the email in the driver's seat, engine off, rain starting on the windscreen.

You are honest. You offer a refund, or a new batch in two weeks with a small thank-you tucked inside. You read it three times. You send it.

Then you sit there a little longer than you need to. Not because you're sad. Because you realise you're tired of this specific feeling. You are not tired of the work. You love the work. You are tired of not being able to trust your own shelf.

The thought that arrives is a plain one, almost boring:

What if I just knew?

Not a big system. Not software with a login and a monthly bill. Just a place where what you have, what's coming in, and what's getting low all sit side by side, and where the thing nudges you before the shelf goes empty rather than after. A calm little picture of your stock that you could look at over coffee and believe.

A different Monday

Picture it a few weeks later.

It's Monday, around nine. The kettle clicks off. You open one sheet, and it takes less time than the toast does. You see that the indigo is getting low, and that the pale grey is fine for a month. There's a quiet note beside the indigo: time to dye more.

So you plan Wednesday's dye day around it. Not in a panic. Just on the calendar, in ink, like any other appointment.

Saturday comes. You set up the stall. A customer asks for three skeins of indigo, and you say yes, and you mean it, because you know exactly how many are in the crate.

When the phone buzzes in your apron, you let it. You finish talking to the woman holding the green wool. You look at the screen afterwards, with a mild curiosity, as if it were the weather.

That's the whole change. Not more sales, not a fancier shop. Just the small, steady feeling that the shelf is telling you the truth.
