Out of Content Ideas Every Week? The Florist's Till Roll

2026-10-23

Monday, 10:40 am. The shop is quiet. The kettle clicks off in the back room, and you are standing at the till with your phone in one hand and a roll of receipt paper in the other, because you were supposed to be changing it.

You open Instagram instead. The little box asks what's on your mind. Nothing is. You scroll up to your last post: a bunch of tulips, three weeks ago. Fresh in today! It got eleven likes, and two of them were your sister.

Let's call you Marit. You run a small flower shop with one part-time helper on Saturdays. Nobody is paying you to be a content person. Nobody ever asked you to be. But the shop needs to be seen, and you know it.

So you stare at the screen and think the same sentence you think every week: What on earth do I post?

The weekly blank

It isn't that you have nothing to say. You spend all day with beautiful things and interesting people. A woman came in on Friday to buy flowers for her mother's new flat. A man bought the same yellow roses he buys every year on the same date and never explains why.

But when the phone is in your hand, all of that goes quiet. What's left is a vague feeling of I should be doing more.

So you do one of three things. You post something rushed and a bit sad. You copy a quote from somewhere and add a heart. Or you do nothing, and the guilt arrives around Wednesday and stays until the weekend.

None of that is dramatic. That's what makes it so tiring. It's a small, repeating weight, like a pebble in your shoe on a long walk.

What it quietly costs

The cost doesn't show up on any receipt. It shows up in other places.

It's the Sunday evening when you tell yourself you'll sit down and plan the week, and then you end up watching a series with your phone face-down on the sofa, feeling vaguely like a bad business owner.

It's the busy week before a holiday, when you have the most to show and the least time to show it, so the shop goes silent online exactly when people are searching.

It's the small voice that says other shops seem to have it together. They post at the right time, with the right photo, as if there were a person in a back office doing nothing else. (There isn't. It's usually one tired person, like you.)

And it's the creeping idea that you're not very good at this. You are good at it. You're good at flowers, at customers, at the early market run. You've just never had a place to put your ideas where they could wait for you.

The idea that was already in your pocket

The turning point isn't clever. It happens on a Thursday, while a customer waits for her bouquet to be wrapped, and she says, almost to herself, "I never know how long these last. Do you have any tips?"

You answer for two minutes. Cut the stems at an angle, change the water, keep them away from the fruit bowl. She nods and says, "You should tell people that."

And you think: that's a post. In fact, you realise, it's three. And you've answered a question like that, in one form or another, about once a week for years.

That's the quiet discovery. You were never short of ideas. You were short of a place to catch them. They arrive while your hands are busy, and by the time you're at the screen, they've gone.

So you start jotting them down. Not in a notebook that gets lost, but somewhere with a shape. A spot for each week. A spot for what kind of post it is. A spot to say whether it's an idea, a draft, or done. Somewhere you can look and see the month at once, and notice, with some relief, that you already have more than you thought.

Nothing about it is complicated. It just stops the ideas leaking out.

A different Monday

Picture the same Monday, 10:40 am. The same quiet shop, the same kettle.

You open one file. This week's row is already half full. Tuesday: the tip about stems that you gave on Thursday. Thursday: a photo of the yellow roses, with a line about the man who comes every year. Saturday: a reminder about the weekend market stall. Two lines are still empty, and that's fine, because there's a short list of spare ideas beside them.

You spend fifteen minutes. You write two captions, pick the photos, and then you change the receipt roll, because you finally have time to.

Nothing is perfect. Some posts will still land softly. But the knot that used to sit under your ribs on Monday morning isn't there. The week has a shape. You can see it, and you can go back to the flowers.

Sunday evening is yours again too. Nobody is planning anything. The phone stays face-down because you want it to, not because you're avoiding it.
