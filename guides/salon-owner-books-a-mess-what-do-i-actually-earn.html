Salon Books a Mess? The Tuesday Your Accountant Asked

2026-10-13

The phone buzzes on the counter between the hairdryer and the cold coffee. It's Tuesday, 1:10 pm, your only gap of the day, and the name on the screen is your bookkeeper's.

"Quick question," she says. "What did the salon bring in last quarter? Roughly. Just so I know where we are."

You look at the till drawer. You look at the card reader. You think of the notebook with the coffee ring on the cover, the app with the payouts, the photos of appointment pages on your phone.

"Roughly," you say. "Let me get back to you."

You hang up and stand there, one hand on the back of the chair. You've done thirty-odd clients a week for years. You know every one of their names, their partners' names, how they take their colour. And you cannot say what you earned.

The numbers that never quite agree

It isn't laziness. You know that. It's that the money comes in through three different doors.

Cash goes in the tin. Card goes to the reader, and arrives in your bank a day or two later, minus a little. Tips land in both places, sometimes mixed with the actual price. A regular pays for next time. A friend gets "the usual mate's rate" and you forget to write it down.

So on Sunday nights you sit with the notebook and the banking app and try to make them agree. They almost do. Almost is the problem. A gap of forty euros could be a missed entry, a refund, a tip, or a mistake in your head. You never find out which, so you round it and close the notebook.

That's the quiet thing nobody says out loud in this trade: you are good at the work and unsure about the business. And that unsureness has a taste, a little flat, like a day-old coffee.

What the gap in the diary really costs

Later, between a trim and a blow-dry, you glance at the appointment book. Thursday, 10:15. A line through it. The third no-show from the same person this spring.

You remember being annoyed. You do not remember what it cost. Was it one hour of an empty chair, or the hour plus the colour you'd already mixed, plus the person you turned away because you were "booked"? You shrug and call it part of the job.

But a shrug is also a number. It's just a number you've decided not to look at.

And there's the other question, the one that arrives around the time the light goes early: which of your services actually pay? The long balayage that eats a Saturday. The quick fringe trim you do for almost nothing. The treatment you added because a client asked once. You feel that some of it is great and some of it is quietly costing you, but you couldn't point to which.

The sentence that changed the afternoon

That evening you tell your sister about the call, half laughing, half not.

"But you don't need accounting software," she says. "You just need one place where it all lands. Cash, card, the no-shows. Same page. Then you look at it for ten minutes."

It sounds too small to be true. You have always assumed that clarity would be a big project, something for a quiet fortnight that never comes. A course. A new system. A weekend of sorting.

But what if it's a page? A calm, plain page that you fill in as the day ends, while you sweep up, and that does the adding for you?

A Monday that feels different

Picture it a few weeks on.

It's Monday, 8:40 am. The salon smells of clean towels and the first coffee. Before the first client, you open one file. Last week's cash sits next to last week's card, and they match, or they don't, and if they don't you can see the day it slipped. The Thursday gap shows up with a figure beside it. Not a punishment, just a fact you can now use: maybe a deposit for new clients, maybe a calmer sentence when someone books again.

You see which services earn their hours. You may keep all of them. You may nudge one price by a small amount and say it without flinching, because now you know why.

When the bookkeeper calls, you don't say "let me get back to you." You say a number. Then you put the phone down and go and greet the person in the doorway with your whole attention.

Nothing dramatic has happened. You haven't become a different person. You've just stopped carrying the whole business around in your head, and your shoulders sit a little lower.
