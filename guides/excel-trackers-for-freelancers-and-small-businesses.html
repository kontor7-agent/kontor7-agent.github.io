Six Spreadsheets, One Question: Excel Trackers for Freelancers

2026-10-12

The client's face fills half your screen. Behind her, a bookshelf and a very green plant. It is 10:05 on a Thursday morning, and she has just asked a perfectly reasonable question.

"Quick one before we start: where are we on the budget for the spring project? And is the last invoice still open?"

You smile. You say, "Sure, one second."

Then you start to hunt.

The one-second that takes four minutes

Imagine Mara. She designs packaging for small food brands, mostly on her own, sometimes with a freelance illustrator she hires in. She is good at her work. Clients say so. Her spreadsheets are another story.

There is invoices_final.xlsx. There is also invoices_final_NEW.xlsx, which she suspects is the real one. Hours live in a notes app and partly in her head. The quote for the spring project is in an email from February. Expenses are a photo folder on her phone and a vague promise to herself.

So while the client waits, Mara shares nothing, mutes herself "to check something," and opens file after file. Her cursor drifts. Her heart rate does too.

She comes back with: "I think we're about on budget. Let me confirm by email this afternoon."

The client says, "No problem at all." And it truly isn't a problem for her. But Mara hears the small pause before she says it. She feels the tiny drop from "professional" to "someone who is winging it."

Nobody says that out loud. It just sits there for the rest of the call.

The quiet price of scattered numbers

The frustration is rarely one big disaster. It is a hundred small ones.

It is the Sunday evening when you think, did I send that invoice? and cannot say yes or no. It is the moment you open a file and wonder which version you are looking at. It is the faint dread when someone asks "how much did that cost you?" and you answer with a confident guess instead of a number.

You are not disorganised. You are just running a small business with tools that were set up one at a time, in a hurry, each for a single problem. A sheet for this. A list for that. A folder nobody dares to rename.

Each piece made sense on the day you made it. Together they make a house where every light switch is in a different room.

And the cost adds up in ways that do not show on any sheet. Mornings that start with searching instead of working. Mental space spent remembering, which is exactly the space you wanted for the creative or interesting part of the job. A small flinch every time someone says, "Can you just send me the numbers?"

The afternoon she stopped patching

After that call, Mara did not fix anything straight away. She did what most of us do: she made tea and told herself it was fine.

But later, while waiting at the supermarket checkout behind a man with a very full trolley, she caught herself thinking something new. Not I need to get more organised. She had thought that a hundred times, and it always sounded like a to-do list with her own name on it.

She thought: Why am I the filing system?

That was the turning point, and it was not dramatic. It was a small, almost funny realisation. A business this size does not need a clever, personal, one-off setup that only she understands. It needs the boring, ordinary basics, laid out the same way every time, in one place, so that the answer to "where are we?" is always a glance away.

Not a bigger system. A calmer one.

What a normal Thursday feels like after

Picture the same call, a few weeks on, in the same chair, with the same plant on the client's shelf.

"Quick one before we start: where are we on the budget? And is the last invoice still open?"

Mara does not mute herself. She does not say "one second." She glances at a single, familiar workbook, finds the project, and says, "You're a little under what we planned, and the last invoice goes out on Friday. I'll flag it so you see it."

That is the whole difference. Not a miracle, just a sentence that comes out easily.

The rest of the day follows that tone. Coffee is still warm when she opens her laptop. She knows what is waiting for payment, what is coming in, and what the month looks like, without assembling it from six places first. When the illustrator asks about hours, there is an answer. When the tax-deadline week arrives, it is a busy week, not a scavenger hunt.

The invisible weight of "I should check on that" gets lighter. Evenings feel like evenings. The part of her brain that used to hold forty loose ends is free to think about the next label design instead.

She does not feel more like a business owner because of colourful dashboards. She feels more like one because nothing is hiding.
