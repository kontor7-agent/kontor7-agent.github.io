Sent a Quote, Heard Nothing? The Checkout Belt Wait

2026-11-07

Friday, 17:40. The supermarket queue is long, and Dario is holding a basket with bread, a bag of rice and a bunch of bananas he doesn't really want.

His phone is in his other hand. He has checked it four times since the shelves with the pasta. No message. No missed call. Nothing from the woman with the bathroom.

He sent her the quote on Tuesday morning. Strip out the old tiles, level the floor, new tiles, new fittings. Three pages, tidy, with a number at the bottom that he typed, looked at, deleted and typed again.

Now it is Friday, and the silence has a shape.

The story you tell yourself in a queue

The belt moves one item forward. So does the story in Dario's head.

Too high. It must be too high. She got a second quote, and it was lower, and she's too polite to say so. Or she thought it over and decided he is the expensive one. Maybe he should write: "Happy to adjust." Maybe he should just call. No, calling is pushy. Calling sounds desperate.

He puts the phone in his pocket. He takes it out again.

If you work for yourself, you know this queue. It might be a different shop, a different job, a different trade. But the thought is the same: Did I price myself out of it?

The strange part is that you can't answer it. You don't know whether she is on holiday, comparing three offers, waiting for her husband, or has simply forgotten. Silence has no information in it. So your mind fills it with the cruellest explanation available.

The question underneath the question

Here is what Dario doesn't say out loud, not even in the van.

He isn't really worried she said no. He's worried about what the number was made of.

Because when he stood in her bathroom on Monday, tape measure in hand, he priced it the way he always does: a look at the walls, a knock on the floor, a quick sum in his head, plus a little "for safety". The tiles she liked were a bit dearer than he'd guessed. He'd meant to check. He hadn't.

So there are two fears in the queue, and they pull in opposite directions.

If she says no, was it too high?

If she says yes tomorrow, was it too low?

That second fear is quieter and costs more. A yes you can't stand behind feels like a win for about a day. Then the merchant's invoice arrives, and the evenings of the third week start to feel long.

Dario has had that job before. The one that looked fine on paper and turned out to pay him almost nothing for ten days of work. He doesn't think about it often. He thinks about it in queues.

What a quiet phone can't take from you

The cashier says something. Dario nods, pays, and carries the bag to the van.

He sits for a minute with the engine off. A man in the next car is struggling to fold a pushchair.

Here is the thing he finally lets himself notice. The silence is hard because the number feels like a guess. If he had known, really known, what the job costs him in materials, in days, in the van and the dust and the wages he owes himself, then the silence would just be silence. A customer deciding. Not a verdict on his worth.

A guess feels like a confession waiting to be found out. A number you can stand behind feels like a fact. Facts are easier to wait next to.

He's not going to solve this in a car park. But for the first time all week he asks a different question: not was it too high? but do I know what this job needs to earn?

That one has an answer. You can find it out.

A different Friday

Picture the same Friday, a few months from now.

Same queue, same bananas. The quote went out on Tuesday, and it still hasn't been answered.

But the quote was built on site, with the materials priced at what they actually cost, his own time in it as a real line and not a hopeful afterthought, and a margin he had looked at with open eyes. He knows the lowest number he'd happily say yes to. He knows the number he'd feel good about. The one he sent sits comfortably between them.

So when the phone stays quiet, he can think: She'll answer when she answers. If she wants it cheaper, I know exactly how much room there is. And if she says no, that's allowed.

On Monday he sends one short, friendly line: "Just checking you got my quote, happy to answer any questions." It doesn't sound like begging, because it isn't. He isn't hoping to be rescued from a bad price. He's offering a good one.

The evening is his. The phone stays in his pocket. The rice cooks.

That's the whole change. The customer still decides. Dario simply stops deciding, on her behalf, that he was wrong.
