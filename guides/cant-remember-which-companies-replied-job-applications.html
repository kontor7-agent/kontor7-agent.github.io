Can't Remember Which Companies Replied? The Dentist Call

2026-10-15

The phone buzzes on the little glass shelf in the dentist's waiting room. Unknown number, a city you half recognise.

You step into the corridor, next to the poster about gum health, and answer in your best calm voice.

"Hi, this is Marta from Brightline. I'm calling about your application."

Brightline. Brightline. Your mind goes white.

Was that the product role? The one with the long cover letter? Or the one you sent at midnight, half asleep, with the CV you meant to update? You say, "Of course, thanks so much for calling," and hope the pause didn't sound like a pause.

The pile you don't talk about

You have sent a lot of applications. You stopped counting somewhere around the second month. Not because you didn't care, but because counting hurt.

Some went through job boards. Some went by email. Two went through those portals that make you retype your whole life into tiny boxes. A few were a quick message to someone you once worked with.

And now they live in six places. A browser bookmark folder. An email label called "Jobs?". A note on your phone. A half-finished spreadsheet from week one, when you still believed you'd keep it up.

Nobody warns you about this part. The search itself is tiring enough. But the quiet fog on top of it is worse: Did they reply? Did I reply back? Was that a no, or just silence?

What the fog quietly costs

It costs you the first thirty seconds of every call, when you should sound sharp and instead sound polite and vague.

It costs you the follow-up you meant to send last Thursday. Somewhere in your head there's a vague feeling that you should write to someone, but you can't say who.

It costs you Sunday evenings, scrolling through your inbox, searching for the word "unfortunately" to work out which doors have closed.

And it costs you a small piece of confidence. You start to feel like you're winging it. Like a person with this many applications ought to be more organised. Which, of course, makes it even harder to open the mess and look.

Back in the corridor

Marta is kind. She says, "Is now an okay time to talk about the role?"

You say yes. Then, because honesty is easier than bluffing, you add, "Could you remind me which team this is for? I've been applying widely and I want to make sure I'm thinking of the right one."

She laughs, a real laugh. "Of course. It's the operations lead position."

The call goes fine. It really does. But walking back to your chair you notice something. Your shoulders are up near your ears, and the feeling isn't about Brightline at all. It's the sense that you have a lot going on and no single place that holds it.

The nurse calls your name. You put the phone away. In the chair, staring at the ceiling light, you think: it doesn't have to be like this.

The small shift

The idea isn't dramatic. It isn't a new app with notifications and badges, or a perfect system you'll abandon in nine days.

It's one calm place. Every application on its own line: the company, the role, the date you sent it, what happened next, and when you meant to follow up. A page you can open in ten seconds and understand.

That's all. The mess stays the same size, but it stops living in your head.

A normal Thursday, afterwards

Imagine it. Your phone buzzes at the dentist, or at the supermarket, or on the tram. An unknown number.

You answer. While the person introduces themselves, you glance at your list and see the company, the role, the day you applied, even the one line you noted about what excited you. You say, "Great to hear from you. I was hoping to talk about the operations lead role."

You can hear the small warmth in their voice when they realise you know exactly who they are.

On Sunday evening there's no inbox archaeology. You open the page, see three applications waiting on a reply, and one that is a week past the day you planned to check in. You send a short, friendly message. Done. The laptop closes before dinner.

And before an interview, the preparation isn't a scramble either. You've got your stories ready, the times you led something, fixed something, handled a difficult colleague, written in a way you can reach for rather than invent on the spot.

The search is still a search. It's still uncertain, and some answers will still be no. But you're no longer carrying a second job on top of it: remembering.
