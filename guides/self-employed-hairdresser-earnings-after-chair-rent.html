Self-Employed Hairdresser, No Idea What You Earn After Chair Rent?

2026-10-14

The first of the month, 12:40, the queue at the discount supermarket.

Your phone buzzes in your coat pocket. You know what it is before you look. The chair rent, gone from your account, the same as every month.

Mira (an illustrative character, but you may know her) has rented a chair at the same salon for three years. She has a loyal Thursday regular, a good hand with balayage, and a booking page that fills up faster than she expected when she started.

She stands there with a basket of pasta, oat milk and one overpriced shampoo she tells herself is research. The person ahead of her is counting coins. And Mira thinks the thought she has thought every first of the month:

I work all week. So what do I actually take home?

The number that isn't there

She could answer busy. She could answer tired. She could answer my feet hurt.

She can't answer the question with a number.

Card payments arrive in her account two days late, bundled together. Cash goes into a small zip pouch in her station drawer. Some of it is tips, some of it is a deposit, some of it is the €20 she used to pay for the colour that ran out on Wednesday. Last Friday she bought foils and a gown from her own pocket and didn't write it down, because there was a client in the chair.

So when someone asks, "How's the self-employed life treating you?", she says, "Good, busy!" and smiles. It's true. It just isn't the answer.

Nobody warns you about this part of renting a chair. You were trained to make people feel good in the mirror. Nobody sat you down and taught you to look at your own week.

What it quietly costs

The worry doesn't shout. It hums.

It hums when a client asks whether you'll raise your prices, and you say "maybe next year" because you honestly don't know whether you can afford to. It hums when a colleague mentions a course and you do mental arithmetic you can't finish. It hums on Sunday evening, when you sit on the sofa with a shoebox of receipts and a calculator, and the cash in the pouch never quite matches the story in your head.

And it hums when the no-shows happen. The 2 pm slot stays empty, and you tell yourself it's fine, you used the time to sweep and reorganise the colour shelf. But somewhere in the back of your mind a small voice asks how many of those gaps there were this month, and what each one cost.

You don't ask it out loud. You're not sure you want the answer.

The pause by the till

Back in the supermarket queue, Mira reaches the till. The cashier says, "Nice day for it," and Mira says, "Yes." She taps her card. The payment goes through. She breathes out, a little too relieved for a €14 basket.

That's the moment it clicks. Not a big dramatic one. Just a quiet, slightly embarrassing thought on the pavement outside, shopping bag cutting into her fingers:

I'm relieved a €14 payment worked. That's not a business. That's guessing.

She isn't bad at money. She's good with people, good with hands, good with colour. What she is missing is a calm place where the week lands: what came in, how it came in, what the chair costs, which gaps hurt, which services pay for the time they take.

Not a complicated system. Just somewhere to look.

A different kind of Monday

Imagine the same salon, a few weeks on. It's Monday, your quiet day, and the street outside is wet and grey.

You make a coffee. You open one file, once, for ten minutes. Last week's cash and card are already sitting side by side, and they agree with each other. You can see the rent as a plain line, not a dread. You can see the two no-shows from Wednesday and, for the first time, what they actually cost you in time you couldn't fill.

You notice that the long treatment you love doing earns less per hour than the quick one you've been quietly rushing through. That isn't a verdict. It's just a fact you can now see, and it gives you something to decide on.

The next time a regular asks about prices, you don't say "maybe next year." You say, "It's going up a little in January. Thank you for being with me." And your voice stays steady, because you know your numbers.

Then you close the laptop. The afternoon is yours.

Nothing dramatic has changed. The chair is the same chair. The rent is the same rent. But the knot you used to carry from the 1st of the month onward has loosened, because you can see where you stand.
