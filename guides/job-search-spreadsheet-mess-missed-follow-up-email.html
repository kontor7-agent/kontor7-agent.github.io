Job Search Spreadsheet a Mess? The Coach Window Seat

2026-10-27

The coach pulls out of the station at 8:40, and Dana finally has twenty minutes of nothing.

She has her phone, a lukewarm tea in a paper cup, and the window seat. She opens her job search spreadsheet to feel organised for a moment.

It does not go well.

There are four tabs. One is called "Applications", one "Applications NEW", one "Apps (use this one)" and one just "Sheet4". Some rows are yellow. Some are red. One cell says "wait?" and nobody remembers what for. A column titled "Followed up?" holds a mix of "yes", "y", "ish" and a lot of blank.

Then she scrolls to a row for a design studio. She remembers it clearly: a warm first call, a friendly "we'll be in touch next week". She was going to send a short note after ten days.

That was nineteen days ago.

The email you meant to send

Your stomach does the small drop it does when you realise you've missed something quietly important.

It's not a disaster. It's worse in a way, because it's invisible. Nobody is angry. Nobody will ever tell you that the moment passed. There's just a studio somewhere that may have picked someone else, or may have been waiting for exactly the nudge you didn't send.

Dana opens her inbox and searches the studio's name. Three threads come up. One is from the recruiter, one is an automated receipt, and one is from a different company with a similar name. She stares at it for a moment, unsure if she ever replied to that one either.

The coach hums over a bridge. A man across the aisle is asleep with his mouth slightly open. Dana types "Hi Katrin, just checking in" and deletes it. It sounds like begging. She types something warmer. Deletes that too.

What it quietly costs

The spreadsheet was never the real problem. It was only the place where the problem became visible.

The real cost is the low hum of not knowing. Every time her phone buzzes, she wonders which company it might be. When an unknown number calls, she thinks, "Is this the one I applied to in September?" She can't say for sure, so she lets it ring.

It costs her Sunday evenings, which she now spends scrolling through old rows trying to reconstruct the story of her own search. It costs her a little confidence, too. She is applying for jobs that ask for "strong organisational skills", and her own system looks like a drawer of unmatched socks.

And it costs her the feeling of being a candidate who has things in hand. Maybe that doesn't show in an email. But it shows in how she answers when a recruiter calls out of the blue and says, "Remind me what drew you to us?" and she has to say, "Of course, one moment."

The sentence on the motorway

About halfway to her sister's, with fields sliding past, Dana stops typing and just looks out of the window.

It occurs to her that she wouldn't run anything else this way. She wouldn't plan a trip, or a move, or a friend's birthday with four tabs and a column of "ish". The search matters more than any of those. It's her next job.

The thought is quiet, not dramatic: this doesn't have to be a maze.

What if there were one place where each application lived on a single line? Where it was obvious at a glance who she'd spoken to, what she'd sent and when a gentle nudge was due? Where the follow-up wasn't something to remember at 23:00 but something that simply showed up and said, "This one, today"?

She sends the studio a short, kind note from the coach, two sentences, no grovelling. Then she closes the sheet and watches the fields until her stop.

A different Monday

Picture the same search a few weeks on.

It's Monday, 8:15. You open one workbook, not four tabs. It takes about a minute. You can see what's waiting for a reply, what's due a nudge this week and what has gone quiet long enough to let go of with a clear head.

A recruiter rings from a number you don't recognise. You let it ring once, pick up and say hello, and by the time they've introduced themselves you've already found the role, the date you applied and the line you wrote in your cover note.

Before an interview, you aren't scrambling through old emails. You have a few real stories from your work, shaped so you can tell them calmly, and a list of the questions that tend to come up. You walk in a little lighter.

The search is still a search. It still has waiting in it, and some silence. But the silence stops feeling like your fault. The knot loosens, and your evenings come back.
