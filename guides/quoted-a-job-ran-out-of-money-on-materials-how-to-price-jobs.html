Quoted a Job, Ran Out of Money on Materials? Skip Yard

2026-10-24

The queue at the skip yard moves one lorry at a time. You are third, engine ticking, a weighbridge ticket on the dashboard that you haven't dared to read properly yet.

It's Thursday, 10:25. Picture Dani, a one-person landscaper. Dani isn't a real person, just someone you might recognise. The garden path and low wall job is two days in. The quote was £2,400, agreed on a Tuesday afternoon with a cup of tea in the client's kitchen.

The phone is propped on the steering wheel. Your banking app is open. The balance has a number in it that makes your stomach go quiet.

The number you wrote on the back of an envelope

You remember the quote well. You walked the garden, measured with your eyes, and said the figure out loud before you could talk yourself out of it. It sounded fair. It sounded like something the client would say yes to. They did, almost before you finished the sentence.

What you didn't do was add things up properly. Not the slabs, which went up since the last time you ordered. Not the sand and cement, bought in two trips because the first load wasn't enough. Not the skip, or the fuel, or the extra half day the old roots ate.

The quote was never wrong on purpose. It was a feeling, and feelings don't carry receipts.

So the card paid for the slabs. The card paid for the sand. The card is now, politely, close to the end.

What it costs, quietly

The money is only part of it. The client has paid a deposit, and that is already spent. The final payment is the thing keeping the whole job afloat, and it won't arrive until Saturday week, if it arrives on time.

You don't say any of this to the client. You smile, you say it's going well, and it is. The path is straight and the wall looks good. They say the work is lovely.

But at the skip yard you do the sum you've been avoiding. If the job finishes on the quoted day, you will have been paid for it, and you will have earned roughly what a teenager earns behind a till. If it runs a day over, you'll be paying to work.

You wonder if this is what everyone else is quietly dealing with too. Whether other people in the trade know something you missed. Whether you're simply bad at this part.

You aren't. Nobody ever showed you a calm way to do it, and the quote on the back of the envelope was the only way you had.

The weighbridge ticket

The lorry in front pulls forward. The man in the hi-vis reaches through your window for the ticket and says the weight. It's heavier than you hoped. Of course it is. Soil and old concrete weigh more than they look.

You pay. The card goes through. Relief and dread arrive at the same time.

Then you notice something small. The ticket in your hand is a piece of paper with a date, a weight and a price. It is the one cost on this job you can see clearly, in black ink. Everything else lives in your head, or in a glovebox of crumpled receipts you haven't looked at since March.

And a plain thought lands, not dramatic at all: I don't need to be better at guessing. I need the numbers somewhere I can see them before I say a price.

A different Tuesday afternoon

Imagine the next quote.

You're in the same kind of kitchen with the same kind of tea. You walk the garden, you take your notes, and then you sit with the job for a few minutes. The materials sit in one column. Your time sits in another. The skip, the fuel and the awkward extra hour for the roots are all there, in daylight.

A number comes out the other end, and you know what's left for you inside it. You say the price slowly, without the small flinch in your chest.

Some clients say yes. Some say it's more than they thought. And you can answer calmly, because you know where the figure comes from. You can offer a smaller job rather than a cheaper one. You can wait.

Then the job starts. The slabs get bought, and the receipt goes into the same place as everything else. By Friday you can see whether the job is behaving or drifting, while there is still time to say something to the client.

And on the day the work is finished, you know who owes you what, and by when. The final payment is no longer a hope. It's a line on a page.

What changes on an ordinary Monday

Nothing dramatic. You wake up and the week has a shape. You aren't mentally rehearsing sums in the shower. You don't open the banking app with one eye closed.

You drive to the first job knowing that the quote you gave was a real one. Not the cheapest, not the boldest, just yours, with your own wages inside it.

That's it. That's the whole change. It sounds small, and it's the difference between a trade that wears you down and one you can keep doing for twenty years.
