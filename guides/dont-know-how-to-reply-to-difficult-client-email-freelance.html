Can't Reply to a Difficult Client Email? The Ironing Board Monday

2026-10-14

It is 7:52 on a Monday, and Mira is standing at the ironing board in the hallway. The iron is warming up. Her phone is propped against a pile of clean towels.

The email arrived four minutes ago. The subject line is just Re: Logo files. The first line reads: "Honestly, this isn't what we discussed."

She reads it once. Her stomach drops a little. She reads it again, slower, as if the words might rearrange themselves. They don't.

Mira is a freelance brand designer. She is three years in, working alone from the corner of the spare room. This client has been difficult from the start, though never in a way you could point to. It is a tone, a sigh between the lines, a way of saying "quick favour" that is never quick.

The reply you start and delete

She opens a reply and types: Hi Daniel, thanks for your feedback! She deletes the exclamation mark. It sounds too eager. She puts it back, because without it she sounds cold.

Then she types: I'm sorry you feel that way. Delete. That sounds like a fight.

Then: As per our agreement... Delete again. Too stiff, like a lawyer she can't afford.

The iron clicks off and on. A shirt sits half-pressed on the board, one sleeve done, the rest waiting.

This is the part nobody warns you about when you go freelance. The work itself, the design, the drafts, the calls, you can do. What drains you is the paragraph where you have to be firm and kind at the same time, with nobody to ask whether it sounds okay.

There is no colleague to lean over and say, "Show me, I'll read it." There is only you, the blinking cursor, and the thought that one wrong sentence might cost you a client.

What it quietly costs

By 8:20 she hasn't sent anything. She has made tea she isn't drinking. She has told herself she'll answer "properly" once she's at her desk.

But the email follows her. It sits in her chest while she packs her bag. It sits there on the walk to the tram stop, where she drafts replies in her head, each one a little angrier than the last, and each one something she would never send.

The cost is not dramatic. Nobody sees it. It is an hour of focus gone before the day has started. It is a client who gets a slow, vague answer because she was too tangled up to write a clear one. It is the small, tired voice that says: a real business would know how to handle this.

And on bad days it is the temptation to just give in. Fix the thing for free. Say yes to the extra round. Keep the peace.

The peace you buy that way is always a little too expensive.

The sentence you didn't need to invent

Here is what Mira doesn't notice at first. Her trouble isn't that she can't write. She writes beautifully when the email is about something easy.

The trouble is that she is starting from a blank page, at the exact moment her pulse is up. Nobody does their best thinking like that.

What if the first draft didn't come from a blank page at all? What if it came from a calm version of the situation, one that already had a shape: acknowledge what they said, state what was agreed, offer the next step? You would only have to fill in the names and the details.

Then you could do the part that is really yours, which is deciding. Is this a fair request? Do I want to hold this line? What do I want to say? The wording would no longer be the obstacle.

Nobody can make that decision for you, and nobody should. But the wording was never meant to be a lonely, hour-long job.

The same Monday, a little later

Picture the other version of that morning.

The email still arrives at 7:52. Her stomach still tightens for a second, because she is human. But she knows where to turn. She finds the situation she is in, fills in a few details, and has a first draft in front of her within a couple of minutes.

It isn't perfect. She changes a word here, softens a line there, makes it sound like her. She reads it aloud once in the hallway. It sounds steady. It sounds like someone who runs a real business, which she does.

She sends it, finishes the shirt, and drinks the tea while it is still warm.

The client may reply well or badly. That part was never in her hands. But the rest of her Monday is hers again: the morning walk, the focus at her desk, the design waiting for her instead of the argument she was rehearsing.

That is the difference. The email is no longer an event. It is just an email.
