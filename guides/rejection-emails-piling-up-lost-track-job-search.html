Rejection Emails Piling Up, Lost Track? The Photo Booth

2026-10-29

The photo booth at the station has a curtain that never quite closes. Mira sits on the little stool with her phone in one hand and a two-euro coin in the other. She needs a passport-style picture for an application form. One of the forms asks for it. Or maybe it was two of them. She isn't sure.

Her phone buzzes against the coin.

Thank you for your interest in the position. After careful consideration...

She doesn't open it. She reads the preview and that's enough. It's the fourth one this week. The worst part is the small, cold question that comes right after: Which job was that?

She scrolls. "Junior Project Coordinator" could be the logistics firm. Or the one with the nice website and the beanbags in the photos. Or the third one, the one with the odd salary range. She can't tell. The curtain flaps. A man outside coughs politely, because Mira has been sitting there for a while now.

The inbox that stopped making sense

This is how it goes after a few weeks of searching. At the start you were careful. You wrote each cover letter by hand. You remembered every company name and every contact. You felt busy and hopeful.

Then the numbers grew. Ten applications became thirty. Some went through job boards, some through company pages, one through a friend of a friend. Replies came in different shapes: a polite no, a silence, an automated "we received your application", a call from a number you didn't save.

Now your inbox is a pile. A rejection sits next to an interview invite, which sits next to a newsletter about "five CV mistakes you're probably making". You open the wrong one at the wrong moment. Your stomach does a small drop even when the news is fine.

Nobody tells you what this costs. It isn't only the rejections. It's the constant low hum of I should know this. It's the Sunday evening when you tell yourself you'll "sort it all out" and then don't, because you're already tired. It's the worry that a recruiter will call and you'll have no idea who they are.

Mira feeds the coin into the slot. The booth counts down. She makes the face she thinks of as reasonable and employable. Flash. Flash. Flash. Flash.

In the last frame, she's laughing, because the curtain has swung open and the man outside is holding his own coin like a ticket at a deli counter.

Four strips of paper

The pictures come out warm from the slot. Four small versions of her, getting a little more honest each time. The first looks like someone about to apologise. The last looks like someone who, for one second, stopped trying so hard.

She sits on a bench by the departures board and looks at the strip for longer than she needs to.

Here is what she notices. The woman in the last frame doesn't look like someone who's failing. She looks like someone who's doing a very large thing with no map. Thirty applications is not a sign of chaos. It's effort. The only thing missing is a place to put it.

That is the turning point, and it is a small one. Nothing dramatic happens. No offer arrives and no email changes everything. She simply thinks: I don't need to be better at this. I need one place where it all lives.

One row per application. The company, the role, the date she applied, what happened next. A note on when to follow up, so she doesn't have to hold it in her head. Somewhere to write down what she'd say if they asked about a time she handled a hard situation, so the answer isn't invented from scratch while her heart pounds.

That's all. It sounds almost too plain to help. But she can already feel her shoulders drop an inch.

A normal Thursday, later

Picture the same bench a few weeks on, same station, ten past eight in the morning. Mira has a coffee and a phone, and the phone buzzes.

Unfortunately, we have decided to move forward with other candidates.

It still stings. It's allowed to. But the question that used to follow, the cold which one was that?, doesn't come. She knows. It was the logistics firm, the second round, the one she'd honestly felt lukewarm about. She marks it closed in a few seconds and moves on to the line below it, where a reminder says it's a good day to send a short, friendly follow-up to the company with the beanbags.

She finishes her coffee. She doesn't spend the walk to the office rehearsing a dozen possible disasters. The search is still a search, and still uncertain, but it's no longer a fog. It has edges. When a recruiter calls from an unknown number at lunch, she can glance at one page and say, "Yes, of course, the coordinator role. Thanks for calling."

In the evening she has an hour she didn't expect to have. She uses it for something that isn't job hunting at all.

The rejections haven't stopped. What's changed is that they've stopped meaning I've lost control of this. They just mean one door closed, in a list where the other doors are visible.
