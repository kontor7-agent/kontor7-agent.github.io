How to Politely Fire a Difficult Client: The Green Man

2026-10-28

You are standing at a pedestrian crossing on Monday morning, 8:52, waiting for the green man.

Your phone is in your hand. The draft is open. It has one line in it: Hi Jonas, I wanted to talk about how we work together.

That's all. You wrote it on Friday, and it has not moved since.

The light stays red. A cyclist ticks past. You read the line again and think what you always think: maybe one more month. Maybe it gets better.

It won't. You know it won't.

The client who takes more than he pays

Jonas is not a villain. That's the problem. He's pleasant on calls. He says "quick favour" and means two hours. He sends feedback at 7:15 on a Sunday, and then a second message that contradicts the first. His invoices are paid eventually, after a nudge, then another nudge, then a sigh in your own chest.

You do the maths without wanting to. He is your second-biggest client, and he takes up the most room in your head. Most of your Sundays have a small grey cloud over them with his name on it.

You imagine the email you'd need to write. Every version in your head goes wrong. One sounds cold. One sounds like an apology. One sounds so polite that he'd miss the point and reply with great, see you Thursday!

So you do nothing. And doing nothing has a price. You carry it into the supermarket queue, into the shower, into the 3 a.m. wake-up when you rehearse a speech nobody asked for.

The pause that isn't about the email

The light turns green. You cross anyway, thumb hovering over the draft.

Here's what you notice, halfway over the zebra stripes: you aren't afraid of the email. You're afraid of what the email says about you. That you're not grateful enough. That a "real" business would just cope. That the income will vanish and prove you wrong.

Nobody tells you that ending a client relationship is one of the most ordinary things a self-employed person does. It feels like a verdict. It's really a Tuesday.

The people you admire have done it. Quietly. Without a drama. They just had words ready when the moment came.

That's what's missing. Not courage. Words.

Words you don't have to invent at 8:52

You get to your desk and open a blank page. Then you do something small. Instead of starting from nothing, you ask for help with the first sentence. Not a whole speech. Just: how would a calm person say "I won't be continuing after the end of the month"?

The answer is plain and kind. It doesn't over-explain. It doesn't blame. It thanks him for the work, names the end date, and offers to hand things over cleanly.

You read it out loud, quietly, in your kitchen voice. It sounds like you on a good day.

You change two words. You add the name of his last project. You take out an apology you never owed.

It still isn't fun. But it's an email now, not a verdict.

What you notice after you press send

You check your own contract first, because you're careful like that. (If you're unsure, a professional can look it over. This story can't do that for you.) Then you press send at 9:40 and stand up to refill the kettle.

Your hands are slightly warm. That's all.

Jonas replies at 11:15, shorter than you expected. A little stiff. Understood, thanks for letting me know. Then, an hour later, a second line asking about the files.

The handover note you'd drafted covers that. You paste it in.

That afternoon the strangest thing happens. You've got room. Not money, not yet; you'll need to fill that space, and you know it. But the grey cloud has lifted off Sunday. You answer another client's message in four minutes instead of forty because your head isn't braced for the next ping.

A week later you catch yourself on the same crossing at the same hour, phone in pocket. The light is red. You just wait.

The after, on an ordinary Monday

It's not that clients become easy. It's that you stop dreading the conversation with them. You have a tone for the late payer. A tone for the price rise. A tone for "that's outside what we agreed." A tone for "I'm not the right fit for this anymore."

You don't write them from scratch while your pulse is up. You open something you've already shaped, change the names, and send it like a professional. Which you are.

Monday starts at nine, not at 3 a.m. the Thursday before.
