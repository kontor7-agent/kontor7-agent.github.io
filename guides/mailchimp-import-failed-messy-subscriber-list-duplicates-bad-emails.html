Mailchimp Import Failed? The Van Outside the Job

2026-11-07

It is 8:05 on a Thursday morning. You are parked outside a client's front gate with the laptop balanced on the steering wheel and a thermos going cold in the cup holder.

The spring workshop announcement is meant to go out at noon. You wrote it on Sunday. You even like it.

But the import has failed. Mailchimp shows a red banner and a number: 212 contacts could not be added. Underneath, a link to a file of errors that tells you almost nothing.

You read it anyway. "Invalid email address." Again. "Invalid email address." Again.

The list you built in four different places

You know how this happened. Nobody sets out to build a messy list.

There were the sign-up sheets from the autumn open garden, typed up on a Sunday afternoon. There was the export from your old website form. There were the names people sent you by email, because they didn't trust the form. And there was that one spreadsheet your sister helped with, back when you thought you would never need more than fifty addresses.

Now you have almost two thousand rows, and you can see the trouble from the driver's seat.

One person appears three times, each with a slightly different spelling. Someone's address ends in .con. Another has a space hiding at the end, invisible until it breaks something. A row near the bottom has a phone number where the email should be.

You scroll. It is like looking for a loose thread in a jumper. The more you pull, the more comes with it.

What it quietly costs

It is a small problem. You tell yourself that, twice.

But small problems have a way of growing in the head. You think about the woman who wrote to you in October, asking to be taken off the list, and wonder whether she is still on it. You think about the people who will get the same email twice and silently decide you are a bit careless. You think about the ones who signed up in good faith and never heard from you, because their address bounced somewhere in the dark.

It isn't really about email. It is about the feeling that your business is held together by good intentions and one lucky weekend.

The client's door opens. She waves from the step, coffee in hand, ready to walk the garden. You wave back and close the laptop on a red banner.

For the next hour you talk about raised beds and drainage, and you are good at it. But a quiet part of you stays in the van, scrolling.

The moment it stops being a mystery

Back at the van, you don't open the spreadsheet. You sit for a moment, and a different thought turns up.

What if the problem is not that the list is hopeless?

What if you have simply been doing by hand, with your eyes, a job that is basically the same every time? The same kinds of mistakes keep appearing: doubles, stray spaces, typos, junk where an address should be. They are not creative mistakes. They are tidy-up work. A thing that could be checked, and checked the same way each time, without you squinting at row 1,184.

The relief is not dramatic. It is more like a window being cracked open in a stuffy room.

You also realise what you actually want. You don't want a clever trick that rearranges everything and hopes for the best. You want to see what would change before anything changes. You want a short, honest list of what was fixed and what was left alone, so that you can trust it.

A different Thursday

Picture the same morning, a few weeks on.

You are in the van again, same thermos, same gate, same client waiting with her coffee. But the laptop is shut, and it is shut with nothing on your mind.

The list was cleaned on Tuesday, in the time it takes to drink one cup of tea. You glanced through a short summary of what had changed: some duplicates merged, some obvious typos flagged, a handful of rows set aside for you to look at yourself. Nothing vanished without you knowing.

The import went through. The workshop announcement left at noon, and you were not even near a screen. You were kneeling by a flower bed, pointing at where the roses should go.

Then, at about two, a reply lands from someone on your list. She wants a place at the workshop. Her address was spelled correctly this time, and the message arrived without a hitch.

It is a very ordinary moment, and that is the point. Your list is something you can rely on rather than something you dread.
