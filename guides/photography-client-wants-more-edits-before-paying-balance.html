Client Wants More Edits Before Paying? The Dog Groomer

2026-10-20

It is Saturday, 10:40, and you are standing outside a dog groomer's window with a coffee going lukewarm.

Inside, your spaniel is being washed. That is your free hour. You meant to spend it doing nothing at all.

Instead you are reading an email on your phone. Mara, let's call her, would know the feeling. She shoots portraits and small family sessions. Three weeks ago she delivered a gallery of 62 edited images. The client loved them. She said so. Twice.

Now the email says:

"Hi! Everyone is so happy. Before we sort out the rest, could you just brighten a few of the indoor ones, and maybe remove the lamp in the background on about ten of them? And my sister would like a version in black and white. Then we'll finalise everything!"

The rest. That is a friendly word for the balance.

The sentence that sits in your chest

You read it again. Nothing in it is rude. That is the problem. If it were rude, you would know what to do.

Instead you feel the small, familiar knot. Maybe I should just do it. It's only ten photos. She was so nice at the shoot. If I say no, I'll look difficult. If I say yes, I'll look like a pushover.

And underneath, a quieter thought you would never say out loud: I'm not even sure what she has already paid.

You think it was a deposit. Half? A third? You remember writing something in a message. Was the gallery due on the 3rd, or was that the delivery for the other client? There is a notes app, a sent folder, a PDF named "invoice_final_v2", and a spreadsheet you made in a good mood last spring.

The dog groomer waves from inside. Twenty more minutes.

What it quietly costs

It is not only the ten lamp edits. It is the way a loose ending turns into an unpaid weekend.

You have lost evenings to "just one more round" before. You have delivered a gallery and then waited, politely, for a payment that never got a date. You have had that moment of typing "Just a gentle reminder…" and deleting it because it sounded like begging.

Most of the discomfort is not about the client. She is probably lovely. It is about not having the facts in front of you. Without them, every reply feels like a negotiation you have to improvise, with your own pulse in your ears.

So you do what many of us do. You put the phone in your pocket and try to enjoy the pavement. You don't answer. That costs something too: a Saturday with a low hum of unfinished business.

The turn

The groomer's door chimes. Your dog comes out smelling of oatmeal and looking offended.

On the walk home, a different question arrives, calmer than the first ones. Not how do I say no? but what is actually true right now?

What was agreed. What was paid. When the gallery went out. What the balance is, and what it is for. Whether this request is a small thank-you-sized favour or a new piece of work.

You realise that you could answer all of that in under a minute, if it all lived in one place instead of five. And that with those facts, the reply would write itself: warm, clear, no apology in it. Perhaps a favour on two images, with the rest quoted as an extra. Perhaps the final invoice sent with a friendly date. Your choice, made with a steady voice.

None of it needs a new personality. It needs a clear view.

A different Saturday

Picture the same email, a few months on.

You open one page before you answer. The family session is a single row: shoot date, deposit received, gallery delivered, balance open, due the 28th. Next to it, a colour that tells you where things stand without you having to remember.

You read the client's message once. You know your agreement, so you know what is included and what isn't. You write three friendly sentences. The balance reminder sits in the last one, not as a threat, just as a next step.

Then you put the phone down. The coffee is still hot. Your dog is still offended, but now you are smiling at it.

That is all the change is. Not a grand system. Just the shoots, the deposits, the gallery deadlines and the invoice status where your eyes can find them, so the next email is a five-minute reply and not a weekend mood.
