Mind Racing at Night? Can't Remember Tomorrow? The Fridge

2026-10-29

It is 3:20 in the morning and Tomasz is standing in his kitchen with the fridge door open. He isn't hungry. The light is just something to stand in.

He runs a one-man garden maintenance business. Hedges, lawns, a few regular clients, and one very particular woman on Birch Road who wants her roses done before the lawn. He knows tomorrow has a list. That's the problem: he knows it has a list, and he can't hold the whole list at once.

Call Mr Hanlon back about the fence quote. Petrol. The strimmer line is nearly gone. Birch Road, roses first. Did he reply to the email from the school caretaker? Something about Thursday. What was Thursday?

He closes the fridge. He opens the notes app on his phone. There are forty-one notes in there, and none of them is the one he needs.

The list that keeps rewriting itself

You may know this one. You lie down tired, and your head switches on like a shop at opening time. Every task you've half-held all day walks to the front and asks to be remembered right now.

So you rehearse them. You say them in order, then in a different order. You promise yourself you'll remember in the morning, and the promise doesn't feel solid enough to sleep on.

The worst part isn't the tasks. It's the quiet bargain underneath: if I let go, one of them will fall. So you hold on. You hold on until 3:20, and then you stand in a fridge light.

Tomasz had tried the usual things. A paper diary that lived in the van and never at home. An app with colours and streaks that made him feel behind the moment he missed a day. A big whiteboard in the hall, covered in marker, until he couldn't tell last week's urgent from this week's.

Every system asked him to be a slightly different person, someone who sits down on Sunday and plans everything neatly. He wasn't that person. He was someone who needed it to be simple enough to use on a bad day.

What the morning cost

He slept badly and got up at 6:10. By 6:50 he was in the van with a coffee going cold in the holder, and he had already forgotten the strimmer line. He remembered at the hedge on Elm Close, with half a hedge to go, and had to drive to the hardware shop in the middle of a working morning.

Nothing terrible happened. That's what made it hard to explain to anyone. No disaster, just a day with small leaks in it: forty minutes lost here, a call returned late there, a client's tone a little cooler than usual. And underneath it, the feeling that he was running a business from memory and goodwill.

He wasn't lazy. He was carrying everything in the one place that wasn't built for storage.

The small thing his sister said

On Sunday his sister came for lunch, saw the whiteboard, and laughed gently. "You don't need a better memory," she said. "You need somewhere to put it down."

She didn't hand him a method. She had a single printed page from a pad stuck to her own fridge, with three lines at the top and a bit of space below. "Three things that matter tomorrow," she said. "The rest can wait underneath. That's all."

It sounded too small to be the answer. That was exactly why he tried it.

That evening he sat at the kitchen table with a pen and wrote down what was in his head. Not in order, not neatly. Just out. Then he picked three. Roses first. Strimmer line. Call Mr Hanlon. Everything else went underneath, where it could be seen but didn't have to be held.

He noticed something odd. His shoulders came down an inch.

A normal Wednesday

It's a few weeks later in our story. It is 6:40 on a Wednesday and the kitchen smells of toast.

The page is on the table, written the night before in about four minutes, before the dishes, before the telly. Three lines at the top. A short tail of smaller things. A few words about Thursday and the caretaker, so Thursday is no longer a mystery.

He doesn't have to wonder what's tomorrow. Tomorrow is already written down, and he can see its edges.

The night before, he'd glanced at the page, put the pen down on top of it and gone to bed. The list still tried to start up once or twice, the way a fridge hums. But now, when a task floated up, he could answer it: it's on the page. And the thought passed.

No one at 3:20 am. No fridge light.

In the van he has the strimmer line, because it was line two. He does the roses first on Birch Road. The woman there says, "You're early," and he says, "I like to be," and for once it's true.
