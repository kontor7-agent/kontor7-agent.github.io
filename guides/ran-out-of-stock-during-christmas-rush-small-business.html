Ran Out of Stock at Christmas? The Courier at the Door

2026-11-03

Saturday, 8:52 am, the courier is early

The courier is already on the doorstep. He holds his handheld like a man who has a lot of doors left today.

You are standing at the packing table in the spare room. The box in front of you should hold three wooden advent calendars, one for each order on the list. You have two.

You check the shelf. You check behind the shelf. You check the shelf again, as if the third calendar might have been shy the first two times.

On the laptop, the shop page still says In stock. It said so yesterday, too. It said so when the order came in at 23:14, from someone in Leipzig who wrote in the note field: It's for my daughter's first Christmas.

The sentence you have to write

You hand the courier two parcels and say "sorry, one more in an hour". He shrugs. Of course he does. It is not his Christmas.

Then you sit down and open the message window. The cursor blinks. You write the sentence every small shop owner learns in December: Unfortunately, there has been a delay.

You delete "unfortunately". You put it back. You delete "delay", because it isn't a delay. It's a hole. You don't have the thing, and you told someone you did.

The supplier's workshop closed for the season on the 5th. You know, because you called them on the 4th. They said the next batch would arrive in the second week of January. You wrote that on a sticky note and put it somewhere "safe".

The sticky note is now under the roll of tape. It has been under the tape for five weeks.

What it quietly costs

The lost sale is the smallest part. Everyone could survive one lost sale.

The larger cost is the weekend. You planned to take your sister's kids to the lights on Sunday. Instead you will spend it re-reading order notes and wondering which other items on that page are not really there.

It costs trust, too. Not theirs, yours. You start to feel like a hobbyist with a nice logo, even though you've packed hundreds of orders and built the whole thing from your own kitchen table.

And it costs the next three nights. You will lie awake running a count in your head. Twelve of the small ones? Eight of the big ones? Did the last order take the red one or the green one? A shop in December runs on numbers you carry around in your head, and your head is tired.

Nobody tells you this part when you start. They talk about products and photos and prices. They don't say that the real December enemy is the quiet gap between what the page says and what the shelf holds.

The refund you didn't want to send

By Sunday afternoon, one customer has cancelled. Two others wrote kindly, which is somehow worse. You refund the order, and the notification pings in the hallway like a small door closing.

You stand at the shelf and count properly, with a pencil, in the cold light of the spare room. It takes forty minutes. You find two items you thought were gone and one you thought you had plenty of that is down to a single piece.

That is when the thought arrives, calm and a little annoyed with you: This isn't a December problem. It's a "nobody told me when to worry" problem.

You didn't run out because you were careless. You ran out because the warning lived in your memory, and your memory was also carrying invoices, gift wrap, a dentist appointment and your mother's birthday.

It doesn't have to be held there. A shelf can have a number next to it. The number can know when it's getting low. And the knowing can happen on a Tuesday in October, not on a Saturday morning with a courier waiting.

An ordinary Monday, next December

Picture it a year from now.

It's the first Monday of December. You make tea and open one file. Nothing flashes, nothing shouts. Two lines are marked, quietly, as time to reorder. You knew about one of them already. The other surprises you, in the good way: you caught it with three weeks to spare.

You send two short emails to your supplier before they close for the season. You reply to a customer who asks whether the small calendar will be back. You say "yes, it's here, and I'll send it Thursday" and you mean it.

The courier arrives at 8:52. You hand him the box. It holds exactly what the list says it should.

Then you close the laptop, put on your coat, and go and see the lights with the children on Sunday.

Nothing about your shop is bigger. You haven't worked harder. You simply stopped carrying the count in your head.
