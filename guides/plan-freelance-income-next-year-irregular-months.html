Plan Freelance Income When Months Differ: Gym Desk

2026-10-18

The annual plan at the front desk

It's Tuesday, 12:35. You're standing at the gym reception with your bag on one shoulder, and the young man behind the desk is smiling at you.

"The annual plan is much cheaper," he says. "You pay once, or in twelve parts. Either way, you're set for the year."

You like the gym. You like the pool, the quiet hour at lunchtime, the way your head clears after forty lengths.

But your mind has already gone somewhere else. A whole year? You are Tomás, a freelance sound editor. You know what is booked until mid-November. After that there is a podcast series that might happen, a documentary that probably won't, and a long gap in the calendar that looks like fog.

"Let me think about it," you say.

He nods kindly. You leave with a flyer and a small, familiar knot in your stomach.

The question nobody asks out loud

It isn't really about the gym. It's about the fact that you can't answer a simple question: what will next year look like?

You don't mean in a big, dreamy way. You mean in a plain, practical way. Will March be a busy month or a thin one? Can you take two weeks in August without wincing? Is it reasonable to sign up for something that runs for twelve months?

Every month is different. January was fat, February was a trickle, and April paid for itself twice because of one lucky film festival. When someone says "yearly", you feel like a person trying to read a map in the dark.

So you do what many freelancers do. You keep the whole thing in your head. A rough feeling, a vague number, a promise to look at it properly "after this project".

It works, mostly. But it costs you something every day. You say yes to work you don't really want, because you can't tell if you can afford to say no. You don't book the holiday, because you can't tell if it's allowed. You carry a low hum of worry that nobody can see.

The walk back to the studio

On the way home you stop at the bakery for a sandwich and eat it standing in the doorway of your building. It's one of those grey October afternoons.

You think about what an honest answer would sound like. Not "I'm fine" and not "it's all chaos". Something like: "I expect roughly this much to come in, I need roughly this much to go out, and I want these weeks off."

That sentence sounds so reasonable. And yet you can't say it. Not because you're bad with money, but because the pieces live in different places. Some are in your invoices, some in your email, some in a notebook, and the rest sit in the part of your brain that wakes you up at 4 am.

You realise something small and slightly embarrassing. You have never once written down what a good year would look like for you. You've only reacted to the months as they arrived.

The sheet with a few honest rows

That evening you don't start a big project. You open a blank sheet, mostly because you want to stop circling the thought.

You write down what you would like the year to bring in. You write down which weeks you'd like to be away. You add the jobs you're fairly sure about, and next to them the ones that are only hopes. The hopes go in a different colour, so you don't fool yourself.

Nothing dramatic happens. No fireworks.

But something loosens. For the first time, the fog has edges. You can see that spring is thin and that autumn is crowded. You can see that the two weeks in August could actually fit, as long as you plan around them rather than apologise for them afterwards.

You notice that you're breathing differently. A plan doesn't make the future certain. It just means you're no longer facing it empty-handed.

A normal Monday, next year

Now imagine a Monday in March.

You make coffee and open one file. In a few minutes you can see what you hoped to earn by now, what actually arrived, and what's waiting in the pipeline. The gap is small, or it isn't, but either way you know about it. It is a fact on a screen, not a feeling in your chest.

A producer writes to ask if you can take on a rush job next week. You glance at your capacity, see that it's a heavy week, and answer calmly. Maybe yes, with a fair rush rate. Maybe not this time. Either way it's a decision, not a reflex.

At the gym desk, the same young man offers the annual plan again. You smile and think about it for ten seconds, because you know roughly what the year holds.

And the August weeks are already marked. When they come, you actually rest. Nobody has to wonder whether you're allowed.
