Laid Off, Applying Everywhere? The Neighbour's Question

2026-10-27

The neighbour leans on the fence with a mug in her hand. You are pegging out a towel. It is 10:15 on a Tuesday, and it still feels strange to be home at this hour.

"So, any news on the job front?" she asks. She is kind about it. That makes it harder.

"A few things in motion," you say. It is true. It is also the vaguest sentence you have said all week.

Your phone buzzes in your back pocket. Unknown number. You step away from the fence and answer.

"Hi, it's Jonas from Brandt & Keller. Thanks for applying. Do you have a few minutes to talk about the role?"

You say yes, of course. Your voice is bright. Your mind is blank.

Brandt & Keller. Which one was that? The operations job? The coordinator role with the long form? Did you send the version of your CV with the project lead title, or the plain one? Did you write a cover letter at all?

The fog behind the bright voice

You bluff your way through the first minute. "Could you remind me which team this is for?" is a question you have learned to phrase warmly. Jonas does not seem to mind. You do. Your ears are hot, and you are standing between the washing line and a bin, holding a damp pillowcase.

After the call you sit on the back step and open your laptop.

There is a folder called Applications, which holds four files. There is another called Jobs NEW, which holds nine. There are two browser tabs of saved listings, half of which have since expired. There is a notes app entry that says "call Priya Thurs??" and nothing else. Somewhere in your email are confirmation messages you never read.

Nobody warns you about this part of being laid off. The first week is shock. The second week is energy: you update everything and send ten applications in a day. By the fourth week you are sending thirty a fortnight. Nothing is tracked, and you can no longer say what you have sent.

What it quietly costs

It costs you more than a bad phone call.

It costs you the small shame of saying "remind me which role this was." It costs you the evening you spent re-reading old emails to find out whether a company ever replied. It costs you the chance you did not take: a follow-up you meant to send, a deadline you noticed one day late.

It also costs you something harder to name. You are already unsettled, and the disorder makes you feel less capable than you are. You were good at your job. You ran things. Now you cannot run your own search, and that feels like a verdict, though it is only a messy folder.

And it is not a verdict. A search with fifty moving parts is simply a lot to hold in your head. Nobody is built for that. Not you, not anyone.

The turning point, in a deckchair

That afternoon you do something small. You take a sheet of paper and write down every company you can remember applying to. It takes forty minutes. You find eleven you had forgotten. You also find two that replied weeks ago, and you never answered.

Half-laughing, you realise the problem was never effort. You have put in plenty of effort. The problem is that everything lives in a dozen places, and you are the only thing connecting them.

What you want is one page. One row for each job, with the date, the status, and the date you should next look at it. You want a place where "Brandt & Keller" shows you the role, the CV you sent, the contact name and what comes next, so you never have to ask the person on the phone to remind you.

You also want to prepare for the interviews that are now arriving, without hunting for the story about the supplier crisis you handled in 2022. You want it where you can find it.

An ordinary Monday, afterwards

Picture a different Monday.

It is 9:00. You make tea and open one file. A short list shows who you are waiting on and who deserves a nudge this week. There are three names. You write two short follow-ups and let the third wait until Thursday. It takes fifteen minutes.

The phone buzzes with an unknown number. You answer, and you look it up while saying hello. "Ah, Brandt & Keller, the operations role. Good to hear from you." The relief is a physical loosening in your shoulders.

The afternoon is yours. You prepare one interview, not by panicking but by picking two stories from your own bank of examples, each already laid out in a clear shape. Then you go for a walk. The search is still there, and it still matters, but it no longer follows you around the house.

The neighbour asks again over the fence. This time you say, "Eleven applications out, two interviews booked, waiting on three." You know the answer because you can see it.
