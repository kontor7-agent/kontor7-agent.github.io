Forgot to Bill Hours You Worked? The Chip Shop Queue

2026-10-21

It's 18:10 on a Friday, and you are third in the queue at the chip shop. The window is fogged up. Someone ahead of you is debating mushy peas.

Your phone buzzes. It's a client, Dana, who runs a small online shop.

"Thanks again for sorting the redirects in spring! Quick question: did that go on the March invoice or was it separate? Just tidying our books before the quarter closes."

You read it twice. Then a third time, slower.

Did you bill the redirects? You remember doing them. You remember the long afternoon with the tea gone cold. You remember a Thursday that ran into the evening because one page kept looping back on itself.

What you can't remember is whether those hours ever reached an invoice.

The sinking, quiet kind of feeling

It isn't panic. It's smaller and heavier than that, like noticing the front door might not be locked when you're already halfway down the street.

"Reaching for the fish" is the plan, so you step forward when the queue moves. But your head is somewhere else. It's in a folder called Invoices 2026, with files named things like Invoice_final and Invoice_final_v2. It's in a notes app with half a timesheet. It's in a calendar where Thursday just says "Dana stuff".

You try the maths in your head. Four hours? Five? Did you quote a flat fee for the migration and quietly add the redirects on top, or did you swallow them as a favour? You can't tell. And that's the part that stings. It isn't the possible money. It's that you, someone who runs a business, can't answer a simple question about your own work.

"Salt and vinegar?" the woman behind the counter asks.

"Yes. Sorry. Yes, please."

What it quietly costs

Nobody talks about this one, because it doesn't look like a crisis. There's no angry email and no overdue notice. There's just a small leak you can't see.

Maybe you billed those hours. Maybe you didn't. The uncertainty is the cost. It makes you stall before replying to Dana. It makes you wonder what else slipped through in the busy weeks, and then it makes you want to stop wondering, because looking feels worse than not knowing.

So you do what many of us do. You tell yourself you'll look at it "properly" at the weekend. And the weekend arrives, and the sun is out, and the looking quietly moves to next week.

Meanwhile you've started drafting a reply that sounds relaxed and professional and says nothing: "Let me double-check and get back to you!" You can feel how thin it is.

The bench by the bus stop

You take your paper parcel outside and sit on the bench. It's warm against your hands. A bus sighs past.

And for once you don't open the folder of files. You just think about what you'd want to be able to do.

You'd want to open one page, type Dana's name, and see it laid out plainly: the project, the hours, the invoice they went on, the date it went out, whether it's been paid. No digging, no guessing, no reconstructing a spring afternoon from calendar crumbs.

It isn't a complicated wish. It's just a different relationship with your own work. One where the question "did I bill that?" has a five-second answer instead of a knot in your stomach.

You eat a chip. It's very good. The knot loosens a little, just from deciding that this can be different.

What a normal Friday could feel like

Picture the same Friday, the same queue, the same buzz of the phone.

Dana's message arrives. You read it, think oh, nice, a quick one, and keep chatting with the person next to you about the peas. When you get home, you look at one screen. The redirects are there, matched to the March invoice, or sitting clearly in a "worked but not yet billed" corner where you can fix it with one calm email.

Either way, you answer Dana within the hour. Steady, friendly, no scrambling.

And the larger shift is quieter than that. You stop carrying a vague background hum of "what have I forgotten?" Mondays start with a short look at who owes you, what's gone out, and what still needs sending. Ten minutes, a cup of tea, done. Evenings go back to being evenings.

You don't become a different person. You just stop doing the remembering with your nerves and let a simple record do it instead.

Back at the kitchen table

That night, you do open the folder. It's not as bad as you feared. It rarely is. You piece together the spring: a few hours here, a fix there. You find that the redirects probably weren't on the March invoice, though you can't be completely sure, and that's the whole problem in one sentence.

You write to Dana honestly and kindly, with the facts you have. You don't grovel. You don't over-explain. And you decide that this is the last time you reconstruct your own business from memory.
