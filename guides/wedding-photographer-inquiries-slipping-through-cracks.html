Wedding Inquiries Slipping Through? The Crew Meal Table

2026-11-04

The vendor meal arrives at 18:40: a plate of schnitzel and potato salad on a folding table beside the kitchen door. The band is still setting up. The couple is eating their starter, and you have eleven minutes before the speeches.

Mara props her camera bag against her shin and opens her phone. She tells herself she is only checking the time.

Then she sees it. An Instagram message, nine days old: Hi! We're getting married next June at a farmhouse near the lake. Are you free on the 14th?

She did not answer. She is almost sure she meant to. She opened it on the tram, thought I'll reply properly when I'm home, and then a Tuesday happened.

The schnitzel goes cold while she scrolls.

The inbox that lives in four places

There is also an email from a couple who want to know about packages. That one she did answer, she thinks, but the reply isn't in her sent folder. Maybe she wrote it in a notes app. Maybe she only wrote it in her head.

There is a WhatsApp from a bride's sister who asked for "prices, just roughly". A contact-form message from a venue coordinator. A voice note she hasn't listened to, because she was in the car.

Nothing here is dramatic. Nobody is angry. That is what makes it so quiet and so awful. Each inquiry is a person who is, right now, deciding whether the photographer they liked is also the one who answers.

Mara knows how it feels from the other side. She booked a florist once and waited six days for a reply. By day four she had already written to someone else.

What it quietly costs

It isn't only the lost bookings, though those are real, and the worst part is that she will never know how many. You can't count the replies that never got sent.

It is the low hum in the back of her head all season. The feeling that somewhere a message is waiting. The way she opens her inbox with her shoulders already raised. The Sunday afternoons spent "catching up" instead of resting after a Saturday on her feet for ten hours.

It is the small shame, too. She photographs one of the most important days of people's lives, and she can't say with certainty who she has answered this week. That doesn't feel like a real business. It feels like winging it with good lighting.

The band starts a soundcheck. Someone drops a cymbal. Mara types a quick reply to the lake couple, apologises for the delay, and says she'll send details tomorrow. Then she sits there with her thumb hovering, because she has just made a promise on top of a pile of other promises, and she doesn't know where any of them are written down.

The moment it clicked

The turning point isn't a big one. It happens a few weeks later, on a grey Monday morning, and it's mostly boredom.

She is standing in the supermarket queue, trolley half full, when she realises what she has been doing wrong. She has been trying to remember. Every inquiry lives in her memory, and her memory is also carrying the timeline for Saturday, the gallery for last month's couple, and what to buy for dinner.

The problem was never that she is careless. She was asking her head to do a filing cabinet's job.

An inquiry doesn't need to be remembered. It needs one place to sit, with a name, a date, and a single honest word beside it: answered, waiting, booked, not this time. A place she can look at in thirty seconds and know.

That is all. No grand system. Just somewhere other than her nerves for it to live.

A different Monday

Picture the same week afterwards.

Mara opens one page with her coffee. Three people have written since Friday. Two already have a reply and a small note about when she'll follow up. One is new, so she answers it before the kettle clicks off, while the couple's names are still fresh and the date is still easy to check.

She can see which enquiries are waiting on her and which are waiting on them. That alone is a relief, because for months those two had felt like the same heavy thing.

A couple she met three weeks ago has gone quiet. Instead of dreading that, she sees a gentle follow-up due today. She writes two warm sentences. It doesn't feel pushy. It feels like what a professional does.

By 9:30 she has closed the laptop and is walking to the park with her camera bag empty and her shoulders down.

Nobody is checking her phone at a wedding meal table anymore, hoping nothing is hiding in there. If a message comes in on a Saturday, she can read it, smile, and let it wait, because she knows where it will land.

The lake couple, by the way, are booked. She answered them properly the next day, with a clear date and a clear next step.
