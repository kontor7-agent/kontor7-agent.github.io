Too Many Freelance Spreadsheets? The Monday Kettle Moment

2026-10-13

It is 8:20 on a Monday, and Jonas is standing in the shared kitchen of his coworking space, waiting for the kettle.

He has a freelance design practice, a laptop bag on his shoulder, and a question in his head that arrived before the coffee did: Did Katrin ever pay that second invoice?

He knows the answer is somewhere. He just doesn't know which file it lives in.

This is an illustrative scene, but you may know it well.

The file you opened first

At his desk, Jonas opens the invoice list. It was a good one, once. He built it in a calm week last spring, with coloured columns and a tidy header.

Katrin's second invoice is there. The status says sent. That was true six weeks ago.

So he opens the hours sheet, because maybe he logged the payment there. He didn't. He opens the rates file, the one with the quote calculations. Nothing about Katrin. Then a folder called Admin_NEW, which sits next to a folder called Admin_old.

Four files are open now. None of them agrees with the others.

Nothing is on fire. That is the odd part. No one is angry, and no deadline has been missed. But Jonas feels the small, familiar tightening under his ribs, the one that says: a real business would know this by now.

The quiet cost of patching things together

He didn't set out to build a maze. Each piece made sense on its own day.

The invoice list came when a client paid late. The hours sheet came after a project that felt endless. The pricing file came after a quote he regretted. Each fix solved one painful moment, and each one landed in its own file, with its own logic and its own idea of what a "client" is called.

So "Katrin" is also "Katrin M." and "K. Meier Studio". When Jonas wants the full picture of one client, he plays detective.

The cost doesn't show up as a number. It shows up as:

  • the half hour on Monday morning that isn't design work
  • the email to a client that he drafts, then doesn't send, because he isn't sure he's right
  • the rate he quotes a little lower than he wanted, because he can't see what the last project actually earned him
  • the thin, constant feeling of winging it

Nobody talks about that last one. From the outside, Jonas looks organised. Inside, he is holding six small things in his head so they don't fall.

The sentence that changed the question

At 9:05, a message from another freelancer in the coworking space pops up while he's still digging. She has seen him frowning at the screen. She says, kindly, "You look like I did last year. Are your files not talking to each other?"

They aren't. And that is the real discovery, said so simply.

He had always asked, Which spreadsheet is the best? But the problem was never one spreadsheet. The problem was that every tool was built on its own, in a different mood, at a different time, to rescue a different bad week.

What if the tools were made to sit together? The same calm look, the same way of entering a client, and everything in one place instead of scattered across folders named final and final2.

He doesn't need a clever system. He needs a set of simple ones that belong to each other.

The same Monday, a few weeks later

Picture the kettle again. Same kitchen, same hum of the fridge.

This time Jonas isn't asking himself anything. He pours the water, carries his mug to the desk and opens one folder. In it, the things he needs are where he expects them: who owes him, what he billed, what a project really earned, what he might charge next time.

It takes a few minutes. He spots one invoice that has gone quiet and sends a short, friendly note. He doesn't rewrite it nine times, because he knows exactly what he is talking about.

Then he starts the actual work, the part he became a freelancer for.

At six he closes the laptop. The evening is his, with no vague guilt about "admin I should have done".

Nothing about his business changed overnight. What changed is that he can see it. That alone loosens the knot under the ribs.

If you recognise the kettle moment

Maybe your version isn't a kettle. It might be a train seat, a school gate or a lunch break with one eye on your phone. The feeling is the same: the information exists, but it's scattered, and you carry the gaps around with you.

Building it all yourself, one rescue file at a time, is a perfectly human way to get here. It is also something you are allowed to stop doing.
