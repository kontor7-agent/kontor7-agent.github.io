Backup Drive Full? The Bakery Question at 6:30 am

2026-10-15

"We're backed up, right?"

It is 6:30 on a Tuesday morning and the back room of the bakery smells of warm flour and coffee. Ines is standing between a rack of rolls and a shelf of flour sacks, laptop balanced on a crate.

She built the bakery's little online shop two years ago. She also looks after its tiny server, because nobody else does. Today the owner has just wiped his hands on his apron and asked, lightly, over his shoulder: "We're backed up, right?"

"Of course," she says.

The word comes out before the thought does.

The folder that says nothing

She opens the backup drive. It is full. Not almost full. Full.

There is a script somewhere. She wrote it on a quiet afternoon and felt very sensible about it. She scrolls to find the last good copy. The dates thin out, then stop, in what looks like early summer.

Next to the backups sits a log file with a size that makes her blink. It has been growing quietly, line after line, for months. Nobody ever told it to stop. Nobody ever read it either.

She does not know whether last night's run worked. She does not know whether any run worked after the disk filled up. The honest answer to the owner's question is "I think so," and that is not an answer.

Behind her, the first tray comes out of the oven. The owner hums. He trusts her completely, and that is the heavy part.

What it quietly costs

Nothing has gone wrong. That is what makes this kind of worry so hard to talk about. No lost files, no angry phone call, no drama. Just a low hum in the back of your head.

It sits there on the train. It sits there on a Sunday walk. It surfaces when a client mentions "our data" in a meeting and you nod a little too quickly.

You tell yourself you will sort it out properly next week. You will check the schedule, clear the old copies, and look at the logs. But next week has invoices in it, and a client who wants one small change, and a laptop update that restarts at the wrong moment.

So the backup remains a thing you believe in rather than a thing you know. And you feel a little unprofessional about it, which is exactly the feeling nobody puts on a to-do list.

The part where you stop guessing

Ines sits down on an upturned bucket and does the thing she has been avoiding. She writes down what she actually wants, in plain words.

She wants a copy that only adds what changed, so it doesn't swallow the disk. She wants old copies to disappear on their own after a sensible while. She wants the logs to tidy themselves. And she wants one small text file per run that says what happened, so she can read it in ten seconds instead of wondering.

She also wants to be able to ask the whole thing, "What would you do right now?" and get an answer without anything being touched.

None of that is exotic. It is just the boring, steady care that nobody has time to build between two paying jobs. The relief is in realising you don't have to invent it from scratch on a crate at 6:30 in the morning.

A different kind of Tuesday

Picture the same bakery a few weeks later.

It is 6:30 again. The rolls are cooling. Ines arrives, makes her coffee, and opens one short log from the night before. It says the run started, what it copied, what it cleaned up, and that it finished. Nothing red. She reads it standing up, the way you glance at a clock.

The owner asks, "We're backed up, right?"

"Yes," she says. And this time the word has something behind it.

She doesn't think about the drive again until the next morning. The laptop at home does the same quiet routine on its own schedule. When a client's folder is deleted by accident in the middle of a busy week, it is a ten-minute errand and not a cold feeling in the stomach.

That is all it is. Not a heroic fix. Just the absence of a small, constant worry, and a bit more room in your head for the work you actually like.
