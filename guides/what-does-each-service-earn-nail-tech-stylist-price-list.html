What Does Each Service Earn? The Nail Tech at the Wholesaler Till

2026-10-14

The card machine at the beauty wholesaler says 187.40. You hold your phone over it and wait for the beep.

It's Thursday, 12:50. You have forty minutes before your 1:30. A basket sits at your feet with gel polish, three boxes of tips, cuticle oil and the good top coat that finally stopped lifting.

The beep comes. The cashier hands you a receipt as long as your arm.

And a quiet thought arrives, the same one as last month: Which of my services paid for all this?

You don't know. You really don't.

The price list you made in a hurry

You sit in the car with the receipt on your knee. On your phone is the price list you made two years ago. Pretty font, soft pink background, pinned to your Instagram. Full set, fill, gel removal, nail art per nail.

You picked those prices by looking at the studio down the road and subtracting a little. You wanted to be "fair". You wanted people to say yes.

Since then, the polish got dearer. The lamp died and you replaced it. Your rent went up in spring. Clients started asking for chrome and foils and tiny hand-painted flowers, and you said "of course" every time, because saying no feels like losing someone.

But the number on the list never moved.

The sum you do in your head

You are good at your craft. You can feel a bad fill from across the room. But when someone asks, "Is the chrome worth doing at that price?", you only have a feeling.

So you do the sum in your head, the way you always do. Roughly this much for product. Roughly that much for the afternoon. Something for the rent. The numbers slide around like wet polish.

It always ends in the same place: a shrug and a sentence you've said to yourself many times.

It's probably fine.

But it nags. Because your Saturdays are full and your account still doesn't look like it. Because a client booked a two-hour set last week and you went home more tired than usual, with no idea whether that set earned more than a quick fill or less.

No one at the wholesaler sees this. Only you, in the car, with the receipt.

What it quietly costs

It isn't dramatic. That's why it lasts.

It's the small flinch when a regular says, "Can you add the glitter, same price?" You say yes before you've thought, because you can't say what it costs you in a calm voice. All you have is a vague sense that it costs something.

It's the Sunday evening when you tell yourself you'll sort the prices "next week".

It's the faint feeling that other people running a chair or a studio have it figured out, and you are somehow winging it. Everyone else seems to have a system.

Mostly it's the money you never see leave. A bit on the long service. A bit on the product you use generously because you like the result. A bit on the rent, spread over hours you never counted.

The moment it turns

That afternoon your 1:30 is a woman who has come to you for years. She's early. While you prepare, she says, almost kindly, "You should charge more, you know. I'd pay it."

You laugh and say thanks.

But the sentence stays with you through the whole appointment, the way a stone stays in a shoe. I'd pay it. The problem is not that you don't believe her. It's that you don't know how much more, or for which service. Raise everything? Raise the art only? You have no answer, so you do nothing, again.

And somewhere in that hour it becomes clear that this is not a courage problem. You've had plenty of courage. You've just never had the picture: one clear view of what each service brings in once the colour, the time and your share of the rent are taken out.

Without the picture, every price is a guess. With it, a price is just a fact.

A different Monday

Picture a Monday morning a few weeks on. The studio is quiet before the first client. The kettle is on. You have your coffee and one sheet open on the laptop.

You look at your services in a row. Full set. Fill. Removal. Chrome. Each with a calm little number beside it: what it actually earns for you.

One line surprises you, in a good way. Another makes you wince a bit, and then you feel something close to relief, because now you know. It isn't a vague worry in your chest anymore. It's a line on a page, and a line on a page can be changed.

So you change it. Not a dramatic leap, just a nudge. You update the price list, the same pretty pink one, with prices that finally match the work.

The next time someone asks for glitter at no extra charge, you smile and say, "That one's a small add-on." Your voice is even. You are not apologising and you are not defending. You just know.

At six you lock the door and the day feels like it earned what it should.
