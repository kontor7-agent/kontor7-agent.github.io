Worried About 2027 Freelance Income? The Lease Renewal

2026-11-01

The letter is on the hallway shelf under a takeaway menu. You see it at 8:10 on a Monday, coat half on, keys in your hand.

Renewal of tenancy, 2027. Please confirm by the end of the month.

It is a perfectly friendly letter. One page. One signature line.

You stand there longer than you meant to. Your coat is still half on.

The question underneath it isn't about the flat. It is: Do you know what next year looks like?

You don't. You are a freelance illustrator, call her Hanna if you like, and you have a feeling about next year. The feeling is mostly fog with a few bright patches. A magazine job that might repeat. A publisher who said "let's talk in January." A client who has paid reliably for two years and has just gone quiet.

The answer you give yourself

On the tram you try to do the sum in your head. It goes badly.

This year had two huge months and three thin ones. You remember the thin ones better. You remember the week in spring when you refreshed your inbox between sentences, pretending to work.

So you do what you always do. You pick the most cautious number you can imagine, squint at it, and feel your stomach tighten. Then you pick a different number, a hopeful one, and feel faintly guilty for it.

Neither number is wrong. Neither one is yours. They are both just moods.

The tram stops. A woman beside you is eating a pastry out of a paper bag, and for some reason that makes you want to cry a little. She looks so unbothered.

What the fog costs

Nobody tells you that unpredictable income has a running cost, and it isn't only money.

It is the project you say yes to at a rate you don't love, because who knows what January brings.

It is the holiday you keep "pencilling in" and never book, because you can't tell if you're allowed.

It is the quiet resentment when a friend with a salary says, "Just take the week off, no?"

And it is this particular Monday feeling. A one-page letter that should take a minute, and instead you carry it around all morning like a stone in your pocket.

You don't need a crystal ball. You are not asking for certainty. You are asking for something much smaller: a way of looking at the year that doesn't depend on your mood that day.

The small turn

At lunch, in the café under your studio, you open your laptop and don't open your inbox.

Instead you write three plain lines on a blank page.

Roughly how many weeks do I actually want to work? What do I need to bring in, month by month, to feel okay? What is already likely, and what is only hoped for?

That's it. No grand strategy.

And something shifts, quietly. The third line does most of the work. When you separate likely from hoped for, the fog doesn't vanish, but it gets edges. You can see where the solid ground is. You can see the gaps too, and a gap you can look at is a very different thing from a gap you only feel.

You realise the weeks off were never the problem. They were just invisible. Once they have a place on the page, they stop being guilt and start being part of the plan.

You also realise you don't want to keep doing this on loose scraps and half a memory. You want one calm place where the year lives: what you aim for, what's coming, what has actually landed, and when you're away.

A different kind of Monday

Picture a Monday in February.

You make tea. You open one file. It takes about ten minutes.

January came in a little under target and you can see exactly how much. It doesn't feel like a verdict. It feels like a weather report. The publisher call is sitting in the "possible" column, not in your head at 3 a.m. Two weeks in August are blocked out, and nothing in the numbers says you can't have them.

When a client offers a project at a rate that's too low, you can check what the quarter looks like before you answer. Maybe you still say yes. Maybe you say, calmly, "That doesn't fit my rate." Either way, the answer comes from the page and not from panic.

Then you close the file and get on with the actual work, which is drawing.

That's all. Not wealth, not certainty. Just a year with a shape.

Back at the hallway shelf

That evening you pick up the letter again. It's lighter than it was this morning, though nothing about it has changed.

You don't need to know everything about 2027. You just need to be able to look at it without flinching.
