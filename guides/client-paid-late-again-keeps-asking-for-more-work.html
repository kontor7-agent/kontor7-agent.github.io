Client Paid Late Again and Wants More Work? The Tile Wall

2026-10-30

You are standing in front of a wall of white tiles. Forty small squares, each a slightly different white. Your partner holds two of them up to the light and asks, "Warm or cold?"

Your phone buzzes in your jacket pocket. You glance down.

"Hi Mara! Great news, the board loved the concept. Can you start on phase two this week? We'd like the first drafts by Friday."

There is no "sorry for the delay." There is no "your invoice is on its way." Invoice one has been open for thirty-eight days. You know the number without looking, because it sits in your chest like a stone.

Warm or cold

"Mara?" Your partner is still holding the tiles.

"Warm," you say. You have no idea which one is warm.

You smile, and you put the phone back in your pocket. Inside, a small argument has already started. They said the board loved it. If you push now about the money, you might lose phase two. Maybe they're just busy. Maybe it's in the system. Maybe you're being petty.

Then the other voice, the quieter one: You paid for your own software this month. You covered the rent from the buffer. You are about to choose tiles for a kitchen with money that someone else is holding.

Nobody warns you about this part of working for yourself. It isn't the late payment itself. It's the way the client's silence turns into your guilt. They are late, and you are the one rehearsing apologies.

The reply you almost sent

In the car park, you sit with the engine off and type a reply.

"Of course! Happy to get started. Just a quick note about the invoice, no rush, whenever you get a chance..."

You read it back. No rush. You delete it. You type a firmer version, and it sounds cold, so you soften it, and now it sounds like begging again. Three drafts later, you have said nothing at all.

Here is what this quietly costs, and you know it. It isn't only the money. It's the Sunday evening dread before you open your inbox. It's the way you start a new job for someone already behind on the last one, a little less generous, a little more tired. It's the feeling that you are not quite a real business, because a real business would simply know what to say.

You don't need a sharper personality. You need the words to already exist before the awkward moment arrives. And you need to know, calmly, which step you are on.

What you actually wanted

Let's be honest about what you wanted, standing at the tile wall. You did not want a fight. You did not want to write a stern letter or to threaten anyone.

You wanted to be able to say, in a friendly voice: "I'm glad the board liked it. I'll start phase two as soon as invoice one is settled. Can you tell me when I can expect it?"

That is all. Warm and clear, with no apology and no drama. You wanted to be the kind of person who says it on a Tuesday morning without a racing heart, and then goes back to drawing.

The problem was never that you couldn't write the sentence. It was that every time, you wrote it from scratch, in the worst possible mood, with the stone in your chest.

A different Thursday

So imagine the same message arriving again some other week.

You are in the same kind of place, the supermarket queue, a tile shop, anywhere. The phone buzzes. "Can you start phase two?"

The stone doesn't appear. You already know where this client stands, because you looked at one page on Monday and saw it plainly: invoice one, overdue, second reminder already sent, next step due today. Nothing is hiding in your head.

When you get home, you open a calm, friendly message you prepared for exactly this moment. You change the client's name and one sentence, and you press send. It takes four minutes. It does not sound desperate, because it was written on a good day, by someone who wasn't desperate.

Then you do something small and quietly radical. You carry on with your evening. You eat dinner. You look at the tile samples without hearing a ticking clock.

The client may answer quickly or may not. Either way, you are no longer waiting in a fog. You know the next step, and you know the one after that, and you know you are allowed to take them. A reminder is not an attack. It is part of running a business.

The kitchen, finally

In the end you chose the tile that was neither warm nor cold. It looked like early morning, a little grey, a little gold. Your partner liked it.

What you liked, though, was the thought that money owed to you could become a plain fact on a plain page, handled in ten minutes a week, instead of a feeling that follows you through the shops.
