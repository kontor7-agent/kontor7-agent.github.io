Client Wants to Start Tomorrow, No Contract? Cable Car

2026-10-19

The message arrives at 16:42, just as the cable car queue shuffles forward.

"We're so excited!! Can you start tomorrow? Saturday is fine for us."

It's from Martin, the new client. The one who said "let's just get going" on Wednesday's call. You said yes then, because he was friendly and the project was good, and because yes feels better than "hold on."

Now you're standing in hiking boots with your sister and two cousins. It's the first day off you've had in weeks, and your phone is warm in your hand.

There's no contract. There's no written scope. You don't know who sends the files, who signs things off, or what "done" means. There is a one-line email that says "logo, website, some social stuff, you know!"

You do not know.

The reply you start four times

You type "Sure, happy to start!" and delete it.

You type "Could we sort a few things first?" and delete that too. It sounds like you're making trouble for someone who just said they're excited.

The queue moves again. Your cousin points at the mountain and says something about the view. You nod and don't see it.

What you're really thinking is quieter. A real business would have a process for this. A real business wouldn't be standing in a queue, making it up on the way up.

That thought is the heavy one. It isn't about Martin. It's about whether you're allowed to ask for a clear start, or whether asking makes you look difficult.

So you do what many freelancers do. You send "Great, talk soon!" and put the phone away.

The cable car glides up. The valley falls away, and you smile at the right moments.

The view you didn't see

At the top there is a terrace, a hot chocolate, and a sky that stops conversations.

You take a photo. Then you check the phone again, because it's been twenty minutes and your stomach is asking.

Two more messages from Martin. One has a link to a shared folder with 214 files, named things like "final_NEW2". The other says, "Also, can you do the packaging too? Should be quick."

There it is. The project has grown while you were looking at a mountain.

It doesn't feel like a crisis. It's a slow, familiar tightening, the sense that you've said yes to something you can't yet describe. The more you picture Saturday morning, the clearer the questions get. Who decides? How many rounds of changes? When does he pay? What did you actually agree to?

You had answers on Wednesday. They were in your head, and his head had different ones.

Somewhere between the hot chocolate and the walk back to the lower station, you notice the cost. It isn't money yet. It's the Saturday you'll spend half-working and half-worrying, and the Sunday you'll spend guessing what he meant.

What if the start had a shape?

On the ride down, your sister asks, "You're quiet. Work?"

You say it's nothing. Then you say, "A client wants to start tomorrow and I haven't got anything written down."

She shrugs. "So send him the thing you'd send anyone. The first-day thing."

"I don't have a first-day thing."

The words sit there in the cabin. I don't have a first-day thing.

It's not a failure. You've just been inventing every beginning from scratch, one anxious email at a time. The thing that would let you say "here's how we start" calmly, without sounding stiff or suspicious, simply doesn't exist yet. You've been carrying it around in your head instead.

And you realise that clients who are excited usually like a clear start. A short, friendly page that says here's what we're doing, here's what I need from you, here's how we'll check in doesn't read as distrust. It reads as someone who has done this before.

The next morning, you decide, you won't start by guessing. You'll start by asking, kindly and in writing.

A Monday that begins on purpose

Picture a different version of this.

A new client says yes. Within the hour, they get a warm welcome email and a short form. It asks what they want, what finished looks like, who gives feedback and which logins you'll need. You don't draft any of it at 16:42 in a queue. It's already there.

The client fills it in over a coffee. A kickoff call has a simple agenda, so nobody wonders what it's for. Scope is confirmed in plain words that both of you can point to later. Access is sorted before the first hour of work, not hunted down in hour six.

On Monday morning you open one tracker and see where each new client stands. Nothing is half-remembered. Nothing sits in a chat thread you'll never find again.

The work is the same work. But you begin it with your shoulders down. The project feels like a project, not a favour you owe.

And your Saturday stays yours, with or without a mountain in it.

Back at the lower station

The cabin bumps into the platform. Your cousins pile out, arguing about lunch.

You stay a second longer with the phone. This time you write something short and kind: that you're glad to start, that you'd like to confirm a few basics first so you can do your best work, and that you'll send a quick outline in the morning. Then you let it go.

It won't be perfect. But for the first time that day, you've chosen how the project begins, and it feels lighter.
