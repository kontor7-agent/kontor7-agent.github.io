Client Hasn't Paid My Invoice: The Email You Rewrote Nine Times

2026-10-13

You are standing outside a café with your bike lock in one hand and your phone in the other. It is Monday, 9:05. The coffee can wait.

On the screen is an email draft you have been rewriting since Friday. Hi Anna, just a quick, friendly nudge, no stress at all, I'm sure it just slipped through...

You delete "no stress at all." It is a lie. You are stressed. You put it back, because it sounds nicer than the truth.

The invoice was for a website refresh. A good project, a nice client, a fair price. And now the money has not arrived, and you are not sure for how long.

The question underneath the question

You typed client hasn't paid my invoice, what do I do into a search bar last night. The results were full of stern letter templates and words that sounded like a courtroom. You closed the tab. That is not who you want to be with a client you still like.

But the real knot is not the email. It is smaller and more embarrassing.

You don't know exactly when the invoice was due.

You sent it sometime in the spring. Or was it early summer? Was it 14 days or 30? You think you wrote the terms on the invoice, but the file is in a folder called Invoices_final_NEW, and the hours you worked are in a notebook, and the notebook is in your other bag.

So the nudge you want to send feels unsafe. What if you say "three weeks overdue" and it is really one? What if they reply, politely, with a date, and you have to say, "Oh. Let me check."

That is the moment you feel like someone winging it, not a business owner.

What waiting quietly costs

Nobody talks about this part. The unpaid invoice is not just a number missing from your account. It is a low hum in the background of your whole week.

You check your banking app while the kettle boils. You check it again at the bakery counter. You say "no, no, take your time" to a client in one email and quietly resent them in the next thought.

You take a smaller job than you wanted because the month feels thin. You postpone buying the monitor. You tell yourself you are fine, and you are, mostly. But a slow payment makes every slow month feel like it might be the one.

And chasing money feels like begging. Even though it is the opposite. It is asking for something you already earned.

The email you finally send

You lock the bike. You go in, order the coffee, and sit by the window. You open the folder.

Ten minutes of digging. Invoice number found. Date found. Terms: 14 days. It is 38 days since you sent it. Not a disaster. Not a drama. Just a fact.

You delete the apologies. You write three sentences: which invoice, when it was due, and a polite request for a payment date. You read it once and press send.

Nothing explodes. The café carries on. A man at the next table is laughing at something on his laptop.

And you notice something odd: the relief was not about the reply. It came the moment you knew the date. The fog was never the client. It was not being able to see your own numbers.

The turning point

Walking home, you think of the other invoices. Is there one from March that you never looked at again? Did the translator you pass on work to ever pay you for the extra hours? You honestly cannot say.

And you think: this should not need a café-window detective job.

What if there were one quiet page where every invoice sat with its number, its amount and its due date, and a status that simply said open, paid or overdue? What if the hours you worked sat next to the invoice instead of in a notebook in the wrong bag? You would not need to remember anything. You would just look.

An ordinary Monday, later

Imagine the same Monday, a few weeks on.

You sit down with your coffee. You open one file. Two minutes later you know that one invoice is overdue by nine days, two are open and not yet due, and the rest are paid. The overdue one is highlighted. There is nothing to reconstruct.

You write the reminder in one go, calm and friendly, with the date and the number in it. You do not apologise for existing. You send it and close the laptop.

The hum in the background is quieter. Not gone, because clients will always pay a little late sometimes. But it is no longer a mystery. You know where you stand, and you can say so in a normal voice.

That is what being a real business feels like. It is not bigger. It is just visible.
