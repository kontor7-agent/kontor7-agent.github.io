Refund Request on a Finished Project? The Minigolf Queue

2026-10-27

The email arrives while you're standing at hole seven.

It's a Saturday, mid-afternoon. Your niece is lining up a putt past a plastic windmill. Your sister is holding the scorecard and a half-melted ice lolly. You glance at your phone only because it buzzed in your pocket, and then you can't unsee it.

Hi Jonas, we've been thinking about the website. It's not quite what we hoped. We'd like to discuss a refund.

The project finished three weeks ago. The final invoice was paid. They said "love it" in a message you still have. You remember, because you screenshotted it on a good day.

Your stomach does the thing. Your face stays polite.

The reply you write in your head

"Your turn," says your niece.

You putt. It goes nowhere near the hole. Nobody comments.

By hole eight you've written four replies. One is furious: do you know how many hours I put into this? One is apologetic: of course, let me see what I can do. One is a long, defensive list of everything you delivered, with dates. One is just "Sure", because it would end the feeling fastest.

None of them is the email you'd send if you were calm. And you're not calm. You're holding a tiny pencil and wondering whether this makes you a bad designer.

That's the part nobody talks about. Not the money itself, though the money matters. It's the sudden doubt about the whole thing. Was the work good? Did you miss something? Are you the kind of person who gets refund requests? A single email, and your sense of being a real business wobbles.

What it quietly costs

The weekend goes grey. You laugh at the right moments at lunch, but part of you is somewhere else, composing paragraphs.

You think about replying right now, from the car park, just to get it over with. You think about waiting until Monday, which means two days of carrying it. You think about offering a partial refund before they've even said what's wrong, because that would feel kind, and fast, and it would stop the knot.

And that's the real danger. Not the client. The speed at which you'd give away money to escape an uncomfortable feeling.

Sunday night you're in bed, rereading the email again. It still says the same thing. It doesn't say what exactly was wrong. It doesn't mention your contract or the approved designs. It just says "not quite what we hoped", which could mean anything from a colour they've changed their mind about to a real problem you'd want to fix.

You realise you've been answering a question nobody has actually asked yet.

The small shift

Monday morning, you make coffee and sit at the table instead of the desk. It's an odd little ritual you've started: no laptop for the first five minutes.

And something loosens, because a thought arrives, plain and almost boring: You don't have to decide anything today. You only have to reply.

A reply isn't a verdict. A reply can be warm and short. It can thank them for telling you, say you'd like to understand what's not working, and ask them to point to the specifics. It doesn't admit anything. It doesn't fight. It just opens the door a little and lets you see what's on the other side.

What you need isn't a perfect argument. It's the first sentence, then the second. A tone you can stand behind. A version that sounds like you on a good day, not you at hole seven.

It's a different kind of problem than you thought. You're not defending your worth. You're writing an email.

A different Monday

Picture the same email arriving on a calmer version of this week.

You read it once. You feel the small drop in your chest, because you're human. Then you open the folder where you keep your wording for exactly these moments: the polite no, the "let me understand", the "here's what we agreed", the gentle close. You pick the one that fits, change two details, and read it aloud once.

It sounds steady. Not cold, not grovelling.

You check your contract and your notes on what was approved, because the facts belong in the conversation, and if it turns into a real dispute, a legal professional is the right person to ask, not a blog. But you don't need to panic first. You send the reply before ten, close the laptop, and go for a walk.

Maybe the client explains something fair, and you offer to fix it. Maybe they push, and you hold your position kindly. Maybe it turns out to be a misunderstanding about a colour. You don't know yet. What you do know is that you handled the first move like someone who runs a proper business.

That's the relief: not winning, just not being ambushed by your own nerves.
