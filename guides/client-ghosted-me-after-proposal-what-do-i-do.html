Client Ghosted Me After the Proposal: What Do I Do? The Tuesday Queue

2026-10-14

You are standing in the supermarket queue, a basket of pasta, lemons and oat milk on your arm. The woman ahead of you is counting coins. You pull out your phone.

No new email. You check anyway. Then you open the sent folder, as if the proposal might have gone missing on its own.

It did not. It is right there. Sent ten days ago, 14:12, with a friendly line at the end: Happy to talk through any questions!

No questions came. No yes, no no. Just a silence the size of a room.

The call that went so well

That is the part that stings. The call was lovely. The client nodded, laughed at your joke about the old website, said, "This is exactly what we need." You walked home lighter than you had felt in weeks.

You even cleared a gap in your calendar. Three weeks in November, pencilled in with their name. You turned down a smaller job because of it. A small, quiet decision, made on the strength of a nod.

Now the pencil mark just sits there.

The checkout beeps. You pay, you bag the lemons, and the thought arrives, right on schedule: Was my price too high? Did I say something wrong? Did they find someone cheaper?

What the silence does to your week

Nobody tells you that being ghosted is not one bad moment. It is a hundred small ones.

It is the way you glance at your phone during lunch. The way you hesitate to say yes to another enquiry, in case the big one comes back. The way you draft a follow-up in your head while brushing your teeth, then decide it sounds needy.

Because that is the real knot, isn't it? Not the lost job. The feeling that writing again would be begging. That a professional would simply wait, with a calm face, and you are not calm at all.

So you do nothing. Day eleven. Day twelve. The gap in your calendar stays empty, and the not-knowing costs more than a no ever would.

The quiet reasons people go silent

Here is something worth holding onto while you wait. Silence after a proposal is very often not a verdict on you.

Sometimes the budget owner is on holiday. Sometimes the decision sits with someone you have never met. Sometimes your email slid under forty others and the client feels a little guilty about it, which makes it harder to answer, not easier. People avoid replies they feel bad about.

You cannot know which of these it is. But you do know this: a short, kind message costs you almost nothing, and it gives them an easy way back in.

The trouble is not that you don't know what to say. It is that you know too many versions. The breezy one. The apologetic one. The one with a deadline that sounds like a threat. You write three, delete three, and close the laptop.

The turn

It happens on the bus home, almost by accident. You are not trying to solve anything. You just think: what would I say if this were a friend's problem?

Something short. Something like: "Hi Marta, I wanted to check whether the proposal reached you, and whether anything in it needs adjusting. No pressure either way. I am planning my November this week, so a quick yes or no would help me."

No grovelling. No drama. A clear, honest reason to reply, and a door left open.

And you realise the awkwardness was never about the words. It was about starting from a blank page, alone, at the exact moment your confidence was at its lowest. The email itself is five lines. The dread around it was the heavy part.

You also realise that a calm follow-up is simply part of how a proposal works. It is not a favour you are asking. It is the next step in the process, the same as sending the proposal was.

A different Tuesday

Picture the same situation a few months from now.

Proposal goes out on a Monday. You note a date in your calendar: follow up Thursday. You do not think about it again. You eat lunch without your phone on the table.

Thursday comes. You open a message you already know how to write, adjust two details, and send it in under two minutes. It sounds like you on a good day: warm, clear, unbothered.

Maybe they reply within the hour. Maybe they say the budget fell through, and you feel a small disappointment and then, strangely, relief, because now you know, and you can fill that November gap with something real. Maybe they stay silent, and you send one last friendly note a week later, then let it go without a knot in your stomach.

Either way, the answer stops living in your head. The pencil mark gets rubbed out or inked in. The evening is yours again.
