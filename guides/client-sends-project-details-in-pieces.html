Project Details Arrive in Pieces? The Flat-Pack Shelf

2026-11-03

It's Saturday, a quarter past ten, and Mira is on the floor of her new flat with an Allen key.

She is a freelance web designer. Today she is not designing anything. She is building a bookshelf, the kind that comes in a flat box with a picture of a smiling couple on the front.

Her phone buzzes on the carpet beside her. She knows who it is before she looks.

Page four of the instructions

The shelf is half built. Two sides are up, and the back panel is on. Then she turns to page four and reads: Before fixing the back panel, insert the middle dowels.

She looks at the back panel, which is already nailed in place.

She sits back on her heels and laughs, a short, tired laugh. It sounds almost exactly like the one she did on Thursday.

On Thursday a client had written: "Oh, I forgot to say, we also need the site in German." She had already built the navigation. The menu labels were fixed. The page widths had been chosen for English.

The week before that, it was the logo files. "Sorry, I just found the old ones." The week before that, it was the login for the hosting account, which arrived on day six in a message that began "Hope this helps!"

None of it is dramatic. Nobody has shouted at her. That's the odd part.

The quiet cost of one more thing

The messages come in small pieces, each one polite. A PDF here, a voice note there, a "quick question" on a Sunday. Each piece, taken alone, is reasonable.

But Mira has started to feel something she doesn't say out loud. She feels like a bad builder. She started before she had the whole picture, and now every new piece feels like her fault.

So she absorbs it. She rebuilds the menu on her own time. She replies "No problem at all!" with a smile she doesn't feel. Then she looks at the hours and wonders why a fixed-price project is quietly turning into a hobby with a deadline.

She doesn't want to sound difficult. The client is lovely. She would just like, once, to begin a project knowing what she is building.

What the shelf was trying to tell her

She pulls out the back panel, which takes twenty minutes and one bent nail. She puts the dowels in. She slides the panel back.

And it fits. It was always going to fit, in the right order.

She stays on the floor a moment with the instruction sheet in her lap. Nothing was wrong with the shelf. Nothing was wrong with her hands. The only thing missing had been the order, and a moment at the start when someone says, this is everything we need before we begin.

She thinks about her own projects. She has always been good at the work. What she has never had is a proper first page: one calm place where the client says who they are, what they want, what they'll send and by when, and where she says what happens next.

Until now the start of a project has been a handshake, a few emails and hope.

A different Monday

She imagines it for a moment, the way you imagine a holiday.

A new client says yes. Within the hour they receive a friendly welcome and a short form to fill in. It asks for logins, files, languages and who signs things off. There is a kickoff call with an agenda, so nobody wonders what it's for. Afterwards there is a short note: here is what we agreed.

Then, in week two, when the client says, "Oh, we also need German," Mira doesn't flinch. She doesn't absorb it or apologise. She says, warmly and without drama, "Good idea. That wasn't in the scope we confirmed, so let me send you a quick quote for it."

There is no awkward standoff, because the page was already there.

In that Monday she opens her laptop at nine, with coffee, and knows what is in the project and what isn't. By six the laptop is closed.

Nothing about it is clever. It is just the instructions arriving before the nails.

The shelf, finished

By noon the bookshelf stands straight against the wall. Mira fills the bottom row with design books she hasn't opened since the move, and steps back.

Her phone buzzes again. The client has sent a new logo file, with "sorry, last one, promise!"

She smiles and doesn't answer yet. She makes tea instead. She will reply properly on Monday, and this time she will also write herself a note. It says the next project will start with page one.
