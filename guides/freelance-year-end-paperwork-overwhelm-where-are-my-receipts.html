Receipts Everywhere at Year-End? The Boxing Day Car Park

2026-10-13

It's 10:20 on the 27th of December, and Jonas is sitting in his parked car outside his parents' house.

He told everyone he needed to "grab something from the boot." He did not need anything from the boot. He needed ten minutes where nobody asked whether he wanted more potato salad.

The laptop is balanced on the steering wheel. The tethered phone signal is weak but good enough. On the screen is a folder called Receipts 2026, and inside it are eleven files. Eleven. He has been freelancing as a conference interpreter all year.

He knows there should be hundreds.

The holiday that isn't one

You know this feeling if you work for yourself. The calendar says time off. The shops are closed, the inbox is quiet, and the whole house is in pyjamas. Everyone else is resting.

Your brain, meanwhile, has opened a second tab: year-end.

Somewhere there is a train ticket from March. A hotel invoice from the job in Vienna. A printer cartridge, a headset, a software subscription that renews quietly in the background. Some of it is in your email, some in a photo you took and never named, some in a coat pocket, and some, you suspect, nowhere at all.

You are not lazy. You are not bad at this. You were busy being good at your actual work.

But sitting there, you feel it anyway. A small, hot embarrassment. A real business would know where its receipts are.

What it quietly costs

Jonas scrolls through his inbox and types "receipt" into the search bar. Four hundred and twelve results. He opens one. It's a newsletter from a stationery shop.

He closes it and opens another. A booking confirmation, no amount visible. He closes that too.

The cost isn't dramatic. That's what makes it hard to name. It's the half hour of searching that turns into two hours. It's the low hum of dread under the Christmas dinner. It's the thought, while someone is telling a perfectly nice story about the neighbours, I should be doing that.

And it's the quiet guess. When you can't find the proof of something, you start wondering whether it's worth the trouble. Some of it you'll skip, and you'll never know how much.

Then comes January. Everyone who has ever worked alone knows the shape of that month: the dread, the shoebox, the appointment you booked weeks ago and now wish you could move.

Jonas's sister knocks on the car window. She's holding two mugs.

"You're doing it again," she says. "The work face."

"It's not work," he says. "It's the opposite of work. It's finding out I don't know where my own year went."

She hands him a mug through the door and goes back inside. She doesn't ask more. She doesn't need to.

The sentence that stuck

He sits there a while longer, tea steaming up the windscreen.

What sticks with him is a throwaway line from a colleague at a booth break in autumn. She'd been complaining about nothing in particular, and said, almost bored: "I don't hunt for receipts anymore. I just have one place where they go."

One place. That's all she'd said. He hadn't asked her what she meant, because at the time his own year still felt manageable.

Now the sentence sits in his head like a pebble in a shoe. One place where they go.

Not a better memory. Not a heroic weekend of sorting. Not a new personality. Just a place, and a habit so small it barely counts as a habit: something arrives, it goes there, and it's done.

He realises that the problem was never that he's disorganised. Nothing was waiting for the receipts. There was no home for them, so they landed wherever he happened to be standing.

It doesn't have to be like this. It almost makes him laugh.

A different kind of December

Picture the same week a year from now.

It's the 27th. You're on the sofa, or still in bed, or walking along a grey beach with a flask. The laptop is closed. Not hidden, just closed.

You know that the receipts from this year are in one place, because you put them there as the year went by, a minute at a time, usually while waiting for a coffee. You know what's still open on your list, and what's finished. There's a short row of dates, and none of them is a surprise.

When the thought year-end drifts through your head, it doesn't land as a punch. It lands as a small, almost boring fact: mostly done.

And in January, the appointment with your tax professional is just an appointment. You bring what you have, neatly, and you let them do the part that is theirs. You don't apologise for a folder called Misc. You don't open with "I know, I know."

The evenings are yours. The potato salad is yours. The neighbours' story, finally, is something you can listen to all the way to the end.
