Drowning in Writing Tasks Every Week? The Car Park Hour

2026-10-23

It is Thursday, 14:05, and you are parked outside the orthodontist.

Your daughter is inside for forty minutes. The laptop sits on the steering wheel, a little crooked, the way it always does. The rain taps the roof. You have, finally, a free hour.

You know exactly what it is for.

Three product descriptions for the new autumn range. A reply to the customer who asked where her parcel is. A note to the supplier about the delayed delivery. And the job ad for the part-time helper you have been meaning to hire since August.

The cursor blinks in the first empty document. You type a sentence. You delete it.

The writing that never ends

Nobody told you that running a small business would mean so much writing.

You thought you would be making things, selling things, looking after people. Instead you are a one-person publishing house. Every week it is the same kinds of texts: a little warmer for customers, a little firmer for suppliers, a little more polished for the shop page.

You have written all of them before. That is the strange part. Somewhere in your sent folder there is a perfectly good supplier email from March. You just cannot find it, and even if you could, it would need changing, and changing it takes as long as writing it fresh.

So you start from zero. Again.

What it quietly costs

The cost is not dramatic. That is why it is so easy to ignore.

It is the reply to a customer that sits in drafts for two days because you want to get the tone right. It is the product description you rush at the end of a long day, and it reads like it. It is the job ad you keep postponing, because starting from a blank page feels heavier than the job itself.

It is a Sunday evening when you tell yourself you will "just quickly" write the week's messages, and an hour later you are still at it.

And under all of that sits a small, nagging thought: a proper business would have this sorted by now.

It would not. Most small business owners you know are doing exactly what you are doing. They just do not talk about it.

The window tap

At 14:31 someone raps on your car window. A man in a hi-vis jacket, pointing at the barrier ticket on your dashboard. You wave, mouth "sorry", and shift the car forward half a metre.

Back to the screen. The supplier email is still two lines long.

You think about asking an AI tool for help. You have tried before. You typed something like "write a supplier email" and got a stiff, generic paragraph that sounded like nobody you know. You spent longer fixing it than you would have spent writing it. So you closed the tab and told yourself it was not for you.

But the problem was not the tool. It was the empty box. You were handing it nothing, so it handed you nothing back.

The moment it clicks

What if the box was not empty?

What if, for each of these jobs, you already had the question half-asked? The product, the tone, the customer's mood, the one detail that matters, all waiting in little brackets for you to fill in. You would type in your own words, and the result would sound like you.

Not a new way of working. Just a way of not starting from zero every single time.

That is the whole shift. You stop being the person who writes everything from scratch. You become the person who reads, adjusts and sends.

A different Thursday

Picture the same car park, a few weeks on.

The rain is still on the roof. The ticket is still on the dashboard. But you open the laptop and the autumn descriptions take ten minutes, because you know what to give and what to check. The parcel reply goes out before the kettle would have boiled. The supplier note is polite and clear, and it says what you actually mean.

Then you open the job ad. It is no longer the thing you have dodged since August. It is a draft on the screen, and you are simply improving it.

You read it all through yourself. You change a line here and there, because it is your business and your name at the bottom. The tool gives you a first draft; the decisions stay with you.

When your daughter comes out with her new retainer case, you close the laptop and ask what she wants for dinner. And you actually mean the question, because your head is not still back in the inbox.

On Sunday evening, there is nothing to "just quickly" write.
