Renegotiating Price After the Project? The Tower Stairs

2026-10-24

Lena is on the ninth landing of a lookout tower when her phone buzzes against her hip.

It is Saturday, just after eleven. Her sister's family is somewhere above her, arguing happily about who gets the good view. Lena is a brand designer. She delivered a full identity package to a client nine days ago: logo, colours, a small guideline PDF. Everyone said it was lovely. The invoice went out on Monday.

She stops on the landing, one hand on the cold railing, and reads the preview.

"Hi Lena, we've been looking at the numbers again and the total is a bit more than we'd imagined. Could we talk about adjusting the price?"

The view is enormous. She doesn't see any of it.

The sentence that takes the air out

This is the part nobody warns you about. Difficult clients during a project are one thing. You can see them coming. But after? After the files are sent, the work is live on their website, and the price was agreed in writing?

Your stomach drops anyway.

Lena's thoughts arrive in a rush, one on top of the other.

Maybe I did quote too high. Maybe I should have asked more questions. They've been so nice. If I say no, will they stop recommending me? Is ten percent off really so terrible?

She knows what the number was. She knows what it cost her: three weeks of mornings, two rounds of revisions that were not in the original plan, and one Sunday she never got back. But standing on a metal staircase with her family's footsteps clanging above, none of that feels like an argument. It feels like a bill she is about to apologise for.

Two replies she doesn't send

She types the first one with her thumb. Of course, no problem, I can do 10% off! It is warm, quick and easy. She looks at it for a moment and deletes it, because she can already feel the next invoice and the next conversation, all quietly shaped by this one.

She types the second one. As per our agreement, the price was confirmed on the 3rd. It sounds like a locked door. She deletes that too. She doesn't want a fight. She wants to stay someone these people like, and also someone who gets paid what she said she would.

Both of those feel like the only two options. That is the trap.

"Are you coming?" her nephew shouts from above.

"Two minutes," she calls back, and sits down on the step.

What she is actually afraid of

It isn't really the money, or not only. It is the fear that she is "not a real business" after all. A real business, she thinks, would have a sentence ready. A real business wouldn't be sitting on a staircase with her heart pounding over a polite email.

She thinks about the agency down the road. They would never answer from a stairwell. Someone there would take a breath, open a template, and send something steady and kind that neither gave in nor slammed the door.

She doesn't have a someone. She has herself, a phone and a nephew counting stairs.

Then a smaller thought arrives, quieter than the others: I don't have to answer right now.

It is such a small thought. The email isn't a fire. The client wrote on a Saturday, and nothing in it says "reply within the hour." Nobody is standing at her elbow. The panic was telling her it was urgent. It wasn't.

She types four words, Thanks, I'll reply Monday, and sends them. Then she puts the phone in her pocket and climbs the last three flights.

The view from the top is, in fact, enormous. She sees it this time.

Monday, without the knot

Here is the version of Monday she'd like to have. It is a bit like the one she has had before on better weeks.

She sits down with coffee and the original quote beside her. She reads the client's email again and notices it is mostly a question, not a demand. She is allowed to answer a question calmly. She can thank them for raising it. She can say what the price covered, say that the project is complete, and leave the door open to talk about something that actually suits them, such as a smaller scope next time. The voice she uses isn't defensive and isn't pleading. It is the voice of someone who knows what her work cost her and says so without raising the temperature.

She doesn't have to invent that voice from nothing at 11 a.m. on a staircase. She can start from words that already sound like her, then change a line or two until they fit this client and this project.

That is the whole difference. The conversation might still be a little awkward, but she isn't alone with it. She sends the email before lunch. Then she goes back to the actual work, and the afternoon is hers.

Whatever the client says next, she will know what she said and why. That is a different feeling from hoping it blows over.
