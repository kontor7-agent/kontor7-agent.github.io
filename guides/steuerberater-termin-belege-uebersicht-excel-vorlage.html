Steuerberater Appointment Next Week? The Folder Called 'Misc'

2026-10-13

The email arrives at 8:12 on a Tuesday morning, while the kettle is still ticking.

Friendly reminder: please send your documents for the annual statement by the end of the month.

You read it standing up. Then you sit down, because your knees have decided that's the better plan.

Let's call her Mira. She is a freelance illustrator, six years in. Illustrative, of course. But if you work alone, you may know exactly how she feels.

The folder called 'Misc'

Mira opens her laptop and looks for the receipts. There is a folder on the desktop called Misc. Inside it: a photo of a receipt for a graphics tablet pen, taken at an angle, half a thumb in the frame. A PDF named Rechnung_final_2.pdf. A screenshot of a software subscription. Three files called IMG_4471.

There is also a paper pile. It lives in a shoebox on the shelf, next to the printer, and it has been growing quietly since February.

She doesn't open the box. She just looks at it. The way you look at a dog you know is going to need a walk in the rain.

The thought in her head is not I'm bad at this. It is smaller and meaner: a real business would have this sorted.

What it quietly costs

Nothing dramatic happens. That's the thing. No one shouts. The sky stays where it is.

But the cost is there, in small coins.

A Saturday morning that turns into "just quickly sorting receipts" and eats the whole afternoon. The low hum of worry whenever the tax advisor's name appears in your inbox. The suspicion that you paid for things you never wrote down, and that some of it is simply lost. The awkward feeling of handing over a messy pile and hoping the person on the other side is kind about it.

And under all of it, the question you rarely say out loud: Am I even earning what I think I'm earning this year?

Mira doesn't know. She has a feeling. A feeling is not a number you can say in a meeting.

The call that didn't happen

At eleven she is supposed to call the advisor's office to confirm a date. She looks at the phone. She puts the phone face down.

She tells herself she will call once she has "a bit more order". This is the sentence that has been moving the call back since September.

She makes a second coffee. She answers two client emails, quickly and well, because that part of the job she is good at. Then she goes back to Misc, and the cursor blinks, and the morning slides toward noon.

The moment it tips

Around one o'clock, a friend texts her. Another self-employed person, a photographer. Just a casual message: Done with my year-end stuff for once. Weirdly boring. Coffee?

Boring. Mira stares at the word.

She had always assumed that people who had it together had some secret talent she lacked. A gift for filing. A personality built for drawers. But the way the friend says it, there's no talent in it at all. Just a place where each receipt goes, a look at the month once it ends, and a short list at the end of the year so nothing is left to memory.

Not a heroic system. A small, dull, kind one.

That is the turning point, and it's quiet. It isn't "I must become a different person." It's "oh. It was never supposed to be this hard."

A different Tuesday

Picture the same morning, a year from now.

The email from the advisor's office comes in at 8:12. Mira reads it with the kettle still ticking, and she smiles a little, because she knows what's behind it. Receipts are in one list, entered as they came in, month by month. She can see, at a glance, what each month looked like and how the year is shaping up. The checklist for the end of the year has most of its boxes ticked already.

She doesn't open a shoebox. She opens one file, checks it over with a cup of tea, and sends it on.

Then she calls the advisor's office and confirms a date. The call takes ninety seconds. She spends the rest of the morning drawing, which is what she actually does for a living.

Nothing about it is glamorous. The relief is in what doesn't happen: no Saturday swallowed, no hum of dread, no hand-over of a pile she's embarrassed by. Just a calm sense that this part of the business is hers, and in order.

And the number she wasn't sure about? She knows it now. Not because she did anything clever, but because she can finally see it. What she does with it is still up to her and her advisor, as it should be. A tidy overview doesn't replace their judgement. It just means you arrive at the conversation knowing your own year.
