Billed the Wrong Hours? The Meatball Queue Panic

2026-10-19

The tray is already warm in your hands when your phone buzzes.

It's 12:34 on a Saturday. You're in a furniture-store restaurant, third in the meatball queue, a flat-pack bookshelf waiting in the car. The preview on your screen says: Hi Jonas, quick question about the hours on your invoice…

That's all you can see. The rest is cut off.

Your thumb hovers. You know that tone. Quick question is never quick.

The sentence you can't un-read

You open it right there, between the gravy and the lingonberry jam.

The invoice shows 42 hours for the website rebuild. I thought we'd agreed on roughly 30. Could you send me a breakdown?

Nothing in it is rude. That makes it worse. Your mind starts running anyway.

Did you bill 42? You think you remember 42. But did you count the revision week twice? Did the call on the 9th end up in there, and the one on the 10th too? Were those even the same call?

The person ahead of you says, "Next, please." You say, "Sorry, just the small plate," and pay without looking at the total.

What you don't have

You sit by the window with a plate you don't want.

You realise what the problem actually is. It isn't that you cheated anyone. You know you didn't. The problem is that you can't prove it in the next five minutes.

Your hours live in three places. Some are in a notes app, some in a calendar, and a few are in your head, labelled "Tuesday-ish." The invoice was written on a tired Thursday from a mix of all three.

An honest mistake and a sloppy system look exactly the same from the outside. Right now they feel the same from the inside too.

You type a reply and delete it. Sorry, I'll check! sounds like panic. The hours are correct sounds like a lie you can't back up yet.

So you type nothing. The meatballs go cold. The knot in your stomach does what it always does: it grows.

The Saturday afternoon you didn't plan

Back home, the bookshelf stays in its box.

You spend three hours on the floor with your laptop, a calendar export and a notes file. You rebuild the project day by day, slowly, the way you should have been able to see it from the start.

Here is what you find. Most of the 42 hours are real. Four of them are a call you logged twice, once in the calendar and once in your notes. They were the same call.

Four hours. Not a scandal, but not nothing either. It's the kind of error that makes a client wonder what else is off.

You write the email carefully, without grovelling and without excuses:

Thanks for flagging this. I went through my records and found one call counted twice, so the correct total is 38 hours. I've attached a corrected invoice and a day-by-day breakdown. Sorry for the confusion, and thank you for asking.

You read it once. You send it.

The client answers within the hour: Thanks, that makes sense. Appreciate the quick fix.

Your shoulders drop an inch. Then another.

The relief is real, but it's a little hollow, because you know how close it was. Next time the question might arrive while you're abroad, or mid-deadline, or on a day when the notes are missing.

The part nobody tells you about trust

Here's what stays with you that evening. A client asking about hours isn't an attack. It's a perfectly reasonable thing to do. What hurts is not being able to answer calmly.

A professional, you think, is someone who can say, "Of course, here's the breakdown," before the coffee gets cold. Not someone who needs a Saturday afternoon and a pile of exports to find out what they did last month.

You don't need to become a different person. You don't need a complicated system. You just need your hours, your projects and your invoices to sit in one place, so that one look gives you the answer.

A Monday that feels different

Picture the next time that email comes.

It's a Monday, 9:15. You have a coffee, and the preview says: Quick question about the hours…

You open the email. You open one file. The project is there, with its days and hours, and the invoice it belongs to sits right beside it. You see at a glance what was billed and what wasn't.

You reply in five minutes: Of course, here's the breakdown. No sweat on your neck, no dread, no Saturday lost.

Then you get on with your day.

That's the whole difference. You haven't done anything clever. You've just stopped relying on memory for something memory is bad at.
