Tax Return Week: Your Invoices and Hours Won't Add Up

It is Thursday, a little after ten in the morning, and the email from your tax adviser's office has been open since Tuesday.

Three short lines. Please send an overview of your invoices for the year. If possible, with the hours behind them.

You made coffee. You carried it to your desk. You have not drunk it.

On the left screen: a folder called "Rechnungen 2025". Some PDFs are named with a number, some with a client, one is called "final_neu_2". On the right screen: a notes app, a calendar, and a spreadsheet you started in February with great intentions and abandoned in May.

You think: it can't be that hard. I did the work. I sent the invoices.

Then you try to prove it.

The numbers that almost match

You open the first invoice. A web design project, 38 hours. You search for those hours. The calendar shows a few blocks. The notes app has a line that says "Fri – layout, ca. 5h?" The question mark does a lot of work.

You open the next one. The number jumps: after 2025-014 comes 2025-016. Where is 015? Did you cancel it? Did you never send it? Is it sitting in a draft somewhere, quietly unpaid?

You are not a careless person. You are the person who delivers on time, who remembers the client's dog's name. But there is no back office here. There is only you, and the year is long, and memory is a poor filing cabinet.

So you do what many self-employed people do. You start reconstructing. Bank statement open in a third window. A highlighter, on a screen, in your head. This payment, that invoice. This week, those hours.

An hour passes. Your coffee is cold.

What it quietly costs

The worst part is not the time, though it is a Thursday you meant to spend on a client's project. The worst part is the small, private feeling underneath.

The feeling of not being a real business.

You would never say it out loud. But you wonder what the adviser will think when the overview arrives with gaps. You wonder whether you billed everything, or whether three hours here and a half day there simply evaporated. Money you earned and never asked for. You wonder about the invoice that may still be open, from a client you are too embarrassed to ask.

None of that is a tax question. It is a can I see my own year? question. And right now, the honest answer is: only in pieces.

Around noon you send the adviser a message. "I'll need a few more days, sorry." You add a smiley so it sounds relaxed. It does not feel relaxed.

The sentence that turns it

That afternoon you call a friend who has been freelancing for nine years. She is a translator. You expect sympathy. Instead she laughs, kindly, and says something that stays with you:

"I don't reconstruct my year anymore. I just write things down once, in the same place, as they happen. Hours, invoice, due date. That's it."

Once. In the same place.

It sounds almost too simple. But you realise you have never had a place. You have had habits, scraps, good intentions and a lot of searching.

The problem was never that you are disorganised. It was that every piece of the year lived somewhere different, and nothing connected the hours to the invoice to the payment.

Nothing about that has to stay as it is.

A different Thursday

Picture the same email arriving next year.

Three short lines from the adviser's office. You read them with your coffee, and you drink it while it is warm.

You open one file. The hours sit next to the invoices they belong to. The invoice numbers run in order, no mystery gap. Each one shows whether it is open, paid or overdue, and you can see at a glance that one client is a week late. You will send a friendly reminder on Monday, without the knot in your stomach, because it is simply a line on a list.

You export what the adviser asked for. You write a two-line reply. Done by half past ten.

The rest of the morning belongs to the actual work you love. At six you close the laptop, and the evening is yours.

Nothing dramatic changed. You just stopped relying on memory to hold a whole year together.
