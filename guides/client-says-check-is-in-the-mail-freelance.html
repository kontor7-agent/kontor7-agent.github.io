The Check Is in the Mail Again? A Freelancer's Wait

2026-10-18

You stand at the row of mailboxes in the hallway of your building, Saturday, just after eleven. The key is already in the lock. You know what you will find. Pizza flyers. A letter from the energy company. Nothing with a handwritten address and a cheque inside.

On Tuesday, the client wrote: "The check is in the mail, should be with you by end of week." On Thursday you believed it. Now it is Saturday, and you are holding a flyer for a gutter-cleaning service.

You close the little metal door a bit too gently, as if that could help.

The sentence that means nothing

Here is the odd part. You do not know whether the client is lying. Maybe the check really is in a postbox somewhere. Maybe it is on someone's desk waiting for a signature. Maybe nobody has touched it since the invoice landed.

That is exactly what makes it so hard. "The check is in the mail" cannot be argued with. It is polite. It is vague. It buys another week without costing the sender anything.

You climb the stairs and think about the reply you could send. Could you tell me when it was posted? Which address did you use? Do you have a tracking number? Every version sounds like an accusation. You imagine the client reading it with raised eyebrows. So you send nothing, and the waiting continues.

What the waiting costs

Nobody talks about this part. The money is late, yes. But the money is not the only thing that is late.

You check the mailbox on the way to the bakery. You check it again on the way back. You open your banking app while the kettle boils, then close it, then open it again. You agree to a smaller grocery shop than you wanted. You tell yourself it is fine.

And you have a quiet, unfair thought: maybe I should not have started the work before the deposit. Which is not really about the deposit. It is about feeling like someone who has to ask for what they already earned.

Chasing money can feel like begging. So you wait, and the waiting quietly turns into a small, constant weight.

Monday, 9:05

On Monday you sit down with coffee and look at the invoice. Sent three weeks ago. Due two weeks ago. There was a first reminder, sort of. A friendly line at the end of another email. Then "the check is in the mail."

You realise something that is a little embarrassing. You do not have a next step. You have a feeling, and the feeling says wait a bit longer. But there is no plan behind it, no date on which waiting turns into asking more clearly, no wording that you already trust.

Every time this happens, you start from zero. You write the email, delete it, rewrite it, and then leave it in drafts until Wednesday.

Then a thought arrives, quietly, over the rim of the coffee cup: this does not have to be a new decision every time. A calm sequence could already exist. A first gentle note on day one after the due date. A clearer one a week later. A firm but friendly one after that. Each with words you already checked and a date that tells you when to send it.

Not a threat. Not a performance. Just an order of steps, so that you are not improvising while your stomach tightens.

The same Monday, a different way

Picture the same morning with that in place.

You open one sheet. The overdue invoice sits in a row, and next to it a small note says what comes next and when. Today is the day for the second reminder. You do not debate it. You copy the message, add the client's name and the invoice number, and read it once. It is polite, specific, and asks one clear thing: please let me know the date the payment was sent.

It takes four minutes. You press send.

Then you do something unusual. You close the tab. You do not check the mailbox at lunch. You work on the project that actually pays you next month, and the afternoon is yours again.

If the client answers with a date, you note it. If they go quiet, you already know the step after this one. Either way, you are no longer standing at the mailboxes hoping. You are running a business that has a way of dealing with late money.

That is what changes. Not that every client pays on time. They will not. It is that a late payment stops being a private drama and becomes an ordinary item on a list.
