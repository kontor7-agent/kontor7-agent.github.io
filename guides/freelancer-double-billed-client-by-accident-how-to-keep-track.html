Double Billed a Client by Accident? The Monday Phone Call

2026-10-15

The phone buzzes on the hallway shelf at 9:07 on Monday morning. The display says Brandt & Keller – Accounts.

You are standing there in socks, one hand around a mug that is still too hot to drink. Accounts never call to say thank you.

Let's call you Jonas. You edit video for small companies: product films, training clips, the odd conference recap. You pick up, and a friendly woman says, "Hi, quick question. We have two invoices from you for the October recap film. Different numbers, same amount. Is that on purpose?"

It is not on purpose.

The sentence your stomach heard first

Your mouth says, "Let me check, one moment." Your stomach has already understood.

You remember it now, in a rush. Three weeks ago you were on the train with a patchy connection. You thought the first invoice had never left your outbox, so you sent it again. Then the first one had gone out. You just never saw the confirmation.

Two emails. Two numbers. One film.

She is kind about it. She says it happens, that she'll hold one back, and that you shouldn't worry. You thank her three times, which is two too many. You hang up and stand there while the mug goes lukewarm.

Nothing terrible has happened. Nobody was charged twice. But that is not what the knot in your chest is about.

What it quietly costs

The knot is about the question underneath: what else don't I know?

If you can double-bill one client, what about the others? Is there an invoice you sent that nobody has opened? A project you finished and never billed at all? You have a vague picture of your own business, and the picture has just shown a crack.

There is also the small, hot embarrassment. You wanted Brandt & Keller to see someone calm and professional. Instead they saw someone who lost track of his own paperwork. The woman from accounts was lovely, and it still stings.

So you open your laptop at the kitchen table and start to check. You scroll through the sent folder. You open a spreadsheet you started in January, with a tab called Rechnungen neu next to another called Rechnungen final. You open the bank app. You search your inbox for "invoice" and get four hundred results.

Fifty minutes later you have answers, and you also have a ruined start to the week. The film editing you planned for the morning has slid to the afternoon. You are tired before the real work has started.

The thought that arrives with the second coffee

Here is what you notice, somewhere around 10:30.

The mistake was never about carelessness. You are careful with frames, with audio levels, with a client's logo being exactly two pixels off. The mistake happened because the answer to "did I already bill this?" lived in three places at once: your outbox, your memory, and a spreadsheet you stopped trusting in spring.

No single place said, plainly: this project, this invoice, sent on this day, due on this day, paid or not.

You realise you don't need to become a different person. You don't need to be more vigilant or work harder at remembering. You need one quiet place where each project has one line, each line has one invoice, and a glance tells you which ones are out, which are paid and which are still waiting to be written.

That is all. A calm, boring, single source of truth.

A different Monday

Picture the same morning a few weeks later.

The phone buzzes at 9:07. It is a friend asking about lunch on Thursday.

You are at the kitchen table with your coffee, and you open one file. You see this week the way you see a clean timeline: who has been invoiced, who still owes you, what falls due on Friday. The October recap film has one line, one number and one date. If you ever wondered whether you had billed it, you would not search anywhere. You would look, and it would be there.

You spend about ten minutes on it. You close it and go and edit. The morning belongs to the work, and the coffee is still hot when you finish it.

There is a smaller change too, and it matters more than it sounds. When a client asks, "Did you send that invoice?", you can say, "Yes, on the ninth, due on the twenty-third," without opening six tabs. Your voice is steady because the facts are in front of you. You sound like what you are: someone who runs a real business.

If you recognise yourself

Maybe you have not double-billed anyone. Maybe it was the other way round: the invoice that never went out, or the one you could not find when the client said they'd paid. The feeling is the same. It is the dull worry that your own records might not be telling you the truth.
