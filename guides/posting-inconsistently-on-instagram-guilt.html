Posting Inconsistently on Instagram? The Checkout Queue

2026-10-23

It's 10:15 on a Tuesday, and you're third in the queue at the wholesale cash-and-carry. The trolley holds twenty kilos of flour, butter, and a box of piping bags you didn't really need.

Your phone buzzes. It's a message from an old friend.

"Hey! Are you still doing cakes? Your Instagram has gone really quiet."

You read it once. Then you open the app, just to check.

The last post is from nineteen days ago. It's a lemon drizzle on a cake stand, with a caption you wrote in a hurry. Before that there's a lovely run of seven posts in eight days. Before that, a gap. Before that, another burst.

You look at the grid and see a heartbeat that can't decide how fast to go.

The guilt that arrives with the receipt

The cashier scans the flour. Beep. Beep.

You're not thinking about flour. You're thinking, I'm not a real business. Real businesses post on Mondays.

Nobody has said that to you. But the thought is familiar, and it comes with its own small routine. You promise yourself a fresh start. This week you'll post every day, and you'll finally do those Reels. You'll batch everything on Sunday.

Then a big order comes in on Wednesday. Your hands are covered in buttercream until nine. By Thursday the plan has quietly slipped away, and the guilt moves in to fill the space.

It costs you more than you'd admit. It's the little pang each time you open the app, and the Sunday evening you spend staring at your camera roll, wondering which photo is "good enough." It's the faint feeling that people are watching you fall behind.

You carry the trolley to the car. The boot is full, and your head is fuller.

What the quiet actually looked like

Here's something you notice in the car park, with the engine off.

The nineteen quiet days weren't empty. You delivered a three-tier wedding cake and answered forty-one messages. You tested a new ginger sponge and finally raised your prices. You were running a business.

The silence on Instagram wasn't laziness. It was a business owner with two hands, doing the work in front of her.

And the posting? It never had a home. The ideas lived in your notes app, in half-finished captions, in photos you meant to use, and in a voice memo you recorded at a red light. Every time you sat down to post, you started from nothing. You had to decide what to say, which photo to use, whether it was too salesy, and whether you'd posted the same thing last week.

No wonder it happened in bursts. Starting from zero is exhausting, and nobody does it gladly twice a week.

The turning point, in a car park

You write back to your friend.

"Still baking! Just quiet online. Orders are full until the 14th."

She replies with three heart emojis and: "Oh good!! I'll order for my sister's birthday."

You look at the message for a moment. One person asked, and one person is about to order.

It doesn't mean Instagram doesn't matter. People do check, and a quiet page can make a lovely business look closed. But it makes you wonder whether you've been punishing yourself for the wrong thing.

Maybe the problem was never discipline. Maybe it was that every post began in a fog, and you'd never had one calm place where the whole month could sit and be seen.

It doesn't have to be elaborate. Just somewhere you can look at the weeks ahead, see what's planned, and see what's already written, so you stop deciding everything on the day.

A Monday that doesn't ambush you

Picture a different Monday, a few weeks from now.

You're standing at the counter with your first coffee while the oven warms up. You open one file. The month is laid out in front of you, calm and quiet. Thursday has a post marked ready. Saturday has a half-written caption, and a note in the corner says photo of the ginger sponge, cut slice. Next week has gaps, and that's fine. Nobody expected you to fill them yet.

You don't feel like you're falling behind, because you can see exactly where you stand.

A big order lands on Wednesday and your hands disappear into buttercream until nine. But the Thursday post goes out anyway, because Thursday was already waiting. You don't have to be brilliant at 9 p.m., because the brilliant part happened on an unhurried Sunday and was parked somewhere safe.

You won't suddenly post every day. You probably don't want to. But the rhythm becomes steady, a plain, kind rhythm, and the guilt has nothing left to hold on to.

Sunday evening is yours again. You can put your phone face-down and watch something dull and lovely.
