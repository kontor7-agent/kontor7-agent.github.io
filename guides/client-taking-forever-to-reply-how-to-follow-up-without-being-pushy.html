Client Taking Forever to Reply? Following Up Without Pushy

2026-10-14

It is Monday, 8:40 in the morning, and the tram is stuck behind a delivery van.

You are holding a coffee in one hand and your phone in the other. You open your inbox. Not for anything new. You open it to check, again, whether Marta has answered.

She has not.

You sent her the revised brand guidelines eleven days ago. Her last message said, "Looks great, will get back to you this week." That was two weeks ago.

So you do what you always do. You look at the thread. You look at the little line that says Sent. You close the app. You open it again.

The draft you wrote on the tram

You type: Hi Marta, just following up on my last email.

You delete it. It sounds like a sigh.

You type: Hope you're well! Just circling back, no pressure at all! Too many exclamation marks. Too much apology. You can almost hear yourself begging.

You type: As per my previous email. You delete that one fast. That is the sentence of someone who is angry, and you are not angry. You are just tired of waiting.

The tram lurches forward. You put the phone in your pocket and tell yourself you will do it later.

What the silence costs

Here is the thing nobody says out loud. The waiting itself is work.

You cannot fully start the next project, because maybe Marta's feedback will arrive and blow up your week. You cannot fully relax, because there is a small hum in the back of your head. Is she unhappy? Did the email land in spam? Did she find someone cheaper? Did you say something wrong?

Meanwhile an invoice depends on her approval. The rent does not care that Marta is busy.

So the quiet costs you concentration at your desk, a little sleep, and a small piece of your confidence. You start to feel like the person who is always asking for something. Not like someone running a real business with a real process.

The call that changed the tone

Around eleven you are on a call with Ines, a friend who runs a small translation practice. You are only half-listening, still thinking about Marta.

She mentions, almost in passing, that she had a client go silent last month. She says it so lightly that you stop stirring your tea.

"How did you handle it?" you ask.

"Oh, I just sent my second nudge," she says. "It's basically the same friendly paragraph every time. I change the name and the date."

"You don't agonise?"

She laughs. "I used to. For a whole year, probably. Then I stopped treating each email like a letter to the court. It's just a message. A clear one. Short, warm, and it makes the next step easy to take."

That last part stays with you. It makes the next step easy. You realise your drafts never did that. They apologised, hinted, or defended. None of them simply said what you needed and made it easy to say yes.

The shape of a calm follow-up

Later that afternoon you try again. This time you do not ask whether she has read it. You write as someone who assumes the client is busy, not careless, and who has a plan either way.

A few warm lines. One clear question. A small, friendly deadline that is real, not invented. No sigh, no scolding.

You read it back. It sounds like you on a good day. Professional. Kind. Not pushy.

You send it at 14:10 and, for once, you do not refresh the inbox.

At 16:25 a reply arrives. "So sorry, this week has been chaos! Approved, send the invoice whenever."

There was no drama. Marta was never upset. She was just buried.

A normal Monday, afterwards

Imagine the version of this Monday where the knot never forms.

You open your inbox and see that two clients have not answered. You are not alarmed. You know what you will send, and roughly when. You have a gentle first nudge, a clearer second one, and a friendly last one that names the next step. You adjust the name, add a detail, and press send before your coffee has cooled.

Then you get on with your day. The tram arrives, you look out of the window, and the thread stays in your inbox where it belongs, not in your head.

The silence of a client is still a little uncomfortable. But it stops being a verdict on you.
