Busy Etsy Shop, No Money Left After Fees? The Toast

2026-10-25

Saturday, 15:40. A garden strung with paper lanterns, somebody's fortieth birthday. Marta holds a glass of elderflower something and a small plate she has stopped eating from.

Her friend Jonas taps a fork against his glass. He wants to say a few words, and then he says them. Mostly about the birthday girl. Then he turns and says, "And to Marta, whose Etsy shop is absolutely flying. Look at those notifications!"

Everyone laughs and raises a glass. Marta smiles. Her phone, face down in her pocket, buzzes again. Another order.

She should feel proud. Instead, a small cold thought sits behind the smile: Then where is the money?

The busy that doesn't show up

Marta makes linen tea towels and small block prints. People like them. Reviews come in with little hearts. Last month she packed parcels at the kitchen table until her fingers smelled of cardboard.

But her business account looks the way it did a year ago. Thin. Sometimes thinner.

She knows some of the reasons, in a vague way. There are fees. There are postage labels. There is the linen, the ink, the little kraft tags, the tape that always runs out on a Sunday. She has a feeling that it all adds up to something bigger than she wants to look at.

So she doesn't look. She packs the next parcel instead. Busy is easier to feel than worth it.

The question under the toast

Later, by the table with the cake, Jonas's sister asks her kindly, "So is it going well? Could you do it full-time?"

There is a true answer and a polite answer. The polite one is, "Yes, it's growing!" Marta says the polite one.

Walking to the tram afterwards, she runs the true answer in her head. I don't know. I honestly don't know whether the bestseller earns me anything. I don't know if I'm paid for the hours or if I'm paying to work.

That is the part nobody tells you about a shop that sells. Selling feels like proof. It isn't proof of anything except that people want the thing at the price you chose. And you chose that price once, a long time ago, on a hopeful evening, maybe by looking at what others charged.

She thinks of the rush orders, the free-shipping weekend she ran because it seemed generous, the sale she gave herself a pat on the back for. She isn't sure any of them were kind to her.

The quiet shame

The worst of it is not the money. It is the small private embarrassment of running something real and not knowing its numbers. Marta would never tell a customer, "I'm not sure what this costs me." Yet that is exactly what she'd tell herself, if she were honest.

It makes her feel like a hobbyist with a very busy hobby. Not a business owner. Someone winging it, with nice photos.

And the guilt is odd. She is not lazy. She works hard. It's just that hard work and a clear picture are two different things, and she has only been doing one of them.

The moment it turned

It did not come as a big realisation. It came on Monday morning, with the kettle clicking off and her laptop open on the same kitchen table.

She thought: I don't need to feel better about this. I need to see it. One product, in plain view. What the linen costs, what the platform takes, what the postage and packaging come to, and what is left for her. Not a vague worry. Just a clear answer, even if the answer is uncomfortable.

Because an uncomfortable answer can be acted on. A foggy worry can only be carried around.

She realised that a number she could see might even be a relief. Maybe the tea towels were fine, and the little prints were the quiet leak. Maybe the opposite. Either way she would stop guessing at the bakery counter of her own life.

A different Monday

Picture the same kitchen a while later. Same kettle, same laptop, same pile of linen on the chair.

But when the order notifications buzz, they sound different. Each one is a sale she understands. She knows which products carry the month and which ones only look busy. When someone asks for a custom size, she can name a price without that tight feeling in her chest, because the price comes from something real.

The market stall on Saturday and the online orders live in one picture, not two notebooks and a memory. At the end of the month she can say a sentence she has never quite managed before: "This is what I actually earned."

And at the next birthday, when somebody asks if she could do it full-time, she gives an answer that is neither polite nor panicky. It is just true. Maybe "not yet." Maybe "closer than I thought." Both are better than not knowing.
