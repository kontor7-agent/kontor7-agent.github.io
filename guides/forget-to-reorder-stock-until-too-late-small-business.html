Forgot to Reorder Stock Until Too Late? The Deli Call

2026-10-22

It is Wednesday, 11:15, and your phone lights up with a number you don't know.

Imagine Mara. She makes chilli oil in a rented kitchen unit, forty bottles at a time. She is standing at the sink, rinsing a pan, when the call comes in.

It is a buyer from a small chain of delis. She tasted the oil at a market in the spring. She wants 200 bottles for the autumn shelves, delivered in nine days.

Mara says, "Of course, that should be fine." Her voice sounds calm. Her stomach does not.

The shelf in the back

She hangs up and walks to the storage shelf. She already knows what she will find, but she looks anyway.

There are 60 empty bottles. There are no caps at all. The label roll is down to a cardboard tube with a few sad strips of paper.

The caps ran out last week. She remembers thinking, I'll order those tomorrow. Then tomorrow was a market day, and the day after that was a day of parcels. The thought slipped away like a coin down the back of a sofa.

The supplier says five working days for caps. The label printer says a week. The deli wants everything in nine days.

Mara stands there with a wet pan in her hand. She does the maths in her head, the way you do it when the numbers won't come out right. Then she does it again, hoping for a different answer.

What it quietly costs

Here is the part nobody posts about. It isn't the one missing box of caps. It's the low hum behind everything you do.

You open the cupboard and wonder what is about to run out. You lie awake and try to picture the shelf from memory. You are never sure whether the number in your head is from today or from three weeks ago.

So you over-order "just in case." Cash sits in boxes you won't need until spring. Or you under-order, the shelf goes empty, and you start writing the apology email before anyone has complained.

You are good at your craft. But a small, stubborn part of you thinks, a real business would have this under control.

That thought is not true. It is just tired.

The call she almost didn't make

Mara does what she can. She phones the supplier and pays extra for faster delivery. She asks the label printer for a favour and gets half of it. It works, barely, and she delivers on day nine with a dry mouth and a very full car.

The deli buyer is delighted. Mara drives home feeling like someone who has just crossed a road without looking.

That night she doesn't celebrate. She sits at the kitchen table and thinks, This worked because I got lucky. I can't build a business on luck.

She doesn't need a bigger warehouse or a new system with a hundred settings. She needs one quiet place where the answer already sits: how many caps are left, how fast they go, and when to order before it gets tight.

She needs to stop being the only place where that information lives.

A different Wednesday

Now picture the same phone call, a few months later.

The number is unknown. The buyer says 200 bottles, nine days. Mara says, "Let me check, I'll call you back in ten minutes."

She opens one file. Bottles, caps and labels are all on one screen, each with a plain mark beside it: fine, or time to order. She sees what she has and what a batch of 200 would use. She sees that the labels need a nudge, and that the caps are already on their way, because she ordered them two weeks ago on a perfectly ordinary Monday.

She calls back in eight minutes. "Yes. Nine days works."

Then she dries her hands and goes back to the pan. Nothing in her chest has tightened. Her afternoon is still hers.

That is the whole difference. The shelf stops surprising her. It isn't magic. It is a number she can trust, in the one place she always looks.
