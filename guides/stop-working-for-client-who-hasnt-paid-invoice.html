Should I Stop Working for a Client Who Hasn't Paid?

2026-10-28

The call starts at 9:05, and Jonas is already holding his breath.

He is a freelance web developer. He is standing at the kitchen counter with the laptop propped on a cookbook, coffee going cold beside it. On the screen, his client's project lead is smiling and sharing a slide titled Phase 2.

"So, can you start this week? We'd love to keep the momentum."

Jonas says, "Sure, sounds great."

He hears himself say it. Meanwhile one thought runs underneath the whole conversation, like a fridge hum you only notice when it stops: Invoice 14 is still open. Day 41.

The yes that comes out on its own

You know this moment, even if your trade is different.

The work was good. They said so. The new brief is exciting, and the money is real. If you say no, you might lose the client. If you say yes, you are working for people who haven't paid for the last round.

So your mouth says "sure" while your stomach says something else.

After the call, Jonas closes the laptop and stands there. He is not angry. That is the strange part. He is just tired in a quiet, low way, the way you get tired from carrying a bag you forgot you were holding.

He opens his email. Three reminders, each a little softer than the last, each one rewritten more times than he would admit. No reply. No "sorry, finance is slow." Nothing.

He types a search into his browser, half embarrassed: should I stop working for a client who hasn't paid my invoice.

What the question is really asking

Here is what he finds, and what most of us find. A lot of confident opinions. "Never work for free!" "Don't burn bridges!" Both are true, and neither tells you what to do on a Monday morning with a real person on the other end.

Because the question underneath is rarely "should I stop?" It is more like:

  • Am I being unreasonable?
  • Will they think I'm difficult?
  • If I pause and they pay tomorrow, will I have damaged something for nothing?
  • If I don't pause, am I teaching them that my invoices can wait?

That last one stings, because it is quietly true for many of us. Each week of silence that we absorb without a word becomes a small yes. Not to the work. To the delay.

And it costs more than the amount on the invoice. It costs the focus you bring to phase two, the sleep before the next deadline, the faint resentment that creeps into work you used to enjoy.

The thing nobody tells you about pausing

Jonas calls his friend Elif, a translator who has been self-employed longer than he has. He expects a pep talk.

She laughs gently instead. "You're treating it like a fight or a surrender. It's neither. It's a next step you haven't decided on yet."

Then she says the sentence that stays with him: "The awkward part usually isn't the pause. It's that you're making it up as you go, alone, at 9:05 on a Monday."

He thinks about that for a while.

What he has been doing is improvising. Every reminder was written fresh, in the mood of the day. Every decision about "how long do I wait?" happened inside his own head, with no line drawn anywhere, so every day felt like a new negotiation with himself.

(What you are actually entitled to do depends on your contract and where you live. For that, a professional is the right person to ask. This story is about the other part: the feeling, and the order of things.)

A different Monday

Picture the same call, a few weeks later, with one difference.

The project lead shares the Phase 2 slide. Jonas smiles, because he is not holding his breath anymore.

"That looks good," he says. "I'd love to start. Before I do, I'm waiting on the last invoice, so I'll begin as soon as that's settled. Can you check where it is on your side?"

Calm voice. No apology. No speech.

He is not angry and he isn't begging. He is a professional stating the order in which things happen. The project lead says, "Oh. Of course, I'll look into it today."

Maybe that works. Maybe it takes another reminder. Either way, Jonas already knows what the next message will be, and roughly when he'll send it. He does not wonder about it at night. He knows who is overdue, by how many days, and what comes next. The knot in his stomach doesn't have much to hold on to anymore.

When the call ends, he drinks his coffee while it is still warm. Then he opens the work he actually wants to do.

What changes isn't the client

Notice what changed in that picture. The client is the same. The invoice is the same. What's different is that Jonas stopped deciding everything from scratch under pressure.

A simple system does that. It turns "ugh, how do I word this?" into a message that is already written in your own calm voice. It turns "how long is too long?" into a clear next step on a clear day.

So the question "should I stop working for a client who hasn't paid?" gets smaller. It stops being a verdict on you and your business. It becomes one planned step among several, taken without drama.
