Biggest Client Pays Late, Scared to Lose Them? Kickoff

2026-11-08

It's Thursday, 10:58. Your video call starts in two minutes, and you are doing the thing you always do first: opening your banking app.

Nothing. Invoice 0412 is now 47 days old. Net 30, they said. You wrote it on the quote and they nodded.

The call is with Halden & Co., your biggest client. They are more than half of your income this year. Nobody warned you that "my best client" and "the one I'm most afraid of" can be the same company.

You close the app. You straighten the laptop. You smile at your own face in the small window.

The offer on the call

Their project lead joins, friendly as ever. She talks about the spring campaign. She says the words you used to dream about: "We'd love to book you for the whole series. Can you start in two weeks?"

You hear yourself say, "Of course, that sounds great."

Your stomach does something else. It tightens, quietly, under the table, where nobody on the call can see.

Because here is the arithmetic you never say out loud. If you say yes, you are working for a client who owes you money, on credit, for another three months. If you say anything else, you might lose the one client who keeps the lights on.

So you say yes. You always say yes.

The call ends at 11:34. She types "Talk soon!" in the chat and a little wave appears. You sit there with your hands on the keyboard and the cursor blinking in an empty email draft addressed to accounts.

The email you don't send

You start: Hi, just a quick reminder about invoice 0412…

Delete. Too small. Like you're apologising for existing.

Dear Sir or Madam, I would like to point out that the payment is overdue…

Delete. Who is this person? You've never talked like this in your life.

You try a third version. Then you close the draft and make tea, because tea is a task you can finish.

This is the part nobody sees. It isn't the money itself. It's the weighing. Is it rude to ask? Will they think I'm needy? Will the new project quietly go to someone else? Every late day, the question gets heavier, and every unanswered day makes the next message harder to write.

So you carry it. In the supermarket queue. At the dinner table. At 3 a.m., when you do the maths on how long the account lasts if Halden pays in December instead of October.

You are not bad at business. You are a person working alone, with the biggest decision of your month sitting on a blank page.

The small thing you notice

A week later, you are on the tram, scrolling through an old thread with a different client. A reminder you sent two years ago. Three friendly lines. They paid the next morning with a smiley.

You had forgotten that this was possible.

Something shifts, slowly. The problem was never that you can't chase money. It was that every time, you started from zero. A blank page, a racing heart, nine rewrites. No wording ready. No clear moment that says: now is the day for a first nudge. Now is the day for a firmer one.

And when there is no plan, every email feels like a personal confrontation. When there is a plan, it feels like a Tuesday task.

You think: what if the awkward part were already written? What if someone had already done the sentences for you, polite, clear and still yours? What if you simply looked at a list that said, Invoice 0412, day 47, next step: this one?

A different Monday

Picture it. Monday, 9:10. You open one sheet and it shows who is late, by how many days and what comes next. Two clients are green. One is amber. Halden is amber, too, but you know exactly what to send, and it takes four minutes.

The tone is warm and direct. It doesn't beg and it doesn't threaten. It sounds like a professional who assumes the best and keeps a calendar.

You press send. You close the sheet. You drink your coffee while it's still hot.

And here is the surprising part. A client who values you does not stop valuing you because you asked for what was agreed. If anything, a clear, friendly reminder tells them you run a real business. They have a process on their side too, and sometimes your invoice is simply sitting in someone's inbox. A calm nudge gets it moving.

You can still say yes to the spring series. You can say yes with a line about payment terms, written without a lump in your throat. That is a decision you make, not one you swallow.

By Wednesday the money may be there, or at least an answer. Either way, you are no longer waiting in silence. You are doing the next sensible step.

The knot in your stomach doesn't vanish by magic. It loosens because the question "what do I say now?" already has an answer.
