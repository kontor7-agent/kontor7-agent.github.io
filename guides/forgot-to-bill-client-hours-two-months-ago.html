Forgot to Bill Hours From Two Months Ago? The Jumble Sale

2026-10-22

It is Saturday, 10:40, and you are standing behind a trestle table in a church hall, guarding a box of mismatched teacups.

You only came to help. Your neighbour runs the village jumble sale every October, and you said yes because it felt good to do something with no screen in it.

Then your phone buzzes in your apron pocket. A message from a client, Daria, who runs a small tea brand. It is friendly and short.

"Hi Tomasz! Loved the new labels. Quick thought: could you do a couple more tweaks like in August? Same kind of thing."

Same kind of thing. In August.

A woman asks you how much for the teapot. You say "two euros" without hearing yourself. Your mind has already gone somewhere else.

The August you can half see

You remember August. Heat in the flat, the fan on the floor, three rounds of revisions on the packaging illustrations. Daria changed her mind about the green. Then about the leaf. Then she changed it back.

You remember saying, "Don't worry, I'll count the extra rounds separately."

And then you remember nothing. No invoice. No "sent" email. Only a vague picture of a number somewhere. Was that the deposit? Or the final payment? Or the extra hours?

You are a grown professional, standing next to a box of cassette tapes, and you cannot say whether you were paid for something you did two months ago.

The quiet maths

You start doing sums in your head, which is the worst place to do them.

Fourteen hours? Maybe sixteen. Some of it was in the evenings, some in a weekend you told yourself you'd "just finish." Your rate has gone up since then, so which rate would you even use?

Then the second thought arrives, the one nobody says out loud. What if I bring it up now and she thinks I'm trying to sneak something in?

And the third. What if I don't bring it up, and I just do the new tweaks, and quietly keep eating the cost?

You realise you have been here before. Not this exact corner, but this feeling: the small, hot embarrassment of not knowing your own business. It is not about the money alone. It is about feeling like someone who is winging it.

"Is this one a pound?" a man asks, holding a lamp.

"It's whatever you like," you say, and he laughs, and you realise you meant it about the lamp and not about your August.

What you actually do

There is a lull around noon. You step out the side door to the little car park behind the hall, where someone has propped a bicycle against the wall and a blackbird is complaining in a hedge.

You open your laptop bag, because of course you brought it. Not to fix anything. Just to look.

You find your folders. You find the emails. You find Daria's August messages, with the dates on them, and your own replies. It is all there, scattered across three places, like crumbs.

It takes you twenty minutes, standing in a car park, to rebuild what happened. Rounds one and two were covered. Round three was not. Neither were the two follow-up calls.

It was not sixteen hours. It was closer to nine. Still real. Still yours. And no longer a fog.

The relief is not that the money is huge. The relief is that it has a shape now. A thing with edges is something you can talk about.

The sentence you can say out loud

You write back to Daria right there on the kerb, in plain words:

"Happy to do the new tweaks. I also noticed I never invoiced the extra revision rounds from August. I'll send that over today, with the dates, so it's clear. Then I'll quote the new tweaks separately."

Your thumb hovers a moment. Then you send it.

Nothing explodes. The blackbird keeps complaining. Back inside, someone is arguing happily over a chess set.

Daria's answer comes before the tea urn is empty: "Oh yes, of course, thanks for catching that!"

That is all. Two months of dread for one friendly line.

A different kind of Monday

Here is what you think about on the walk home, carrying a teacup you did not need to buy.

What if every Monday started with a quiet look at one page? Which hours were worked, which of them were billed, what is still open and who owes you. Not a project, just a glance, the way you glance at the weather.

You would not have to trust your memory with money. You would not have to stand in a church hall doing arithmetic with your stomach. When a client says "same as in August," you would simply know what August was.

And chasing, or correcting, or quoting, would feel like what it is: ordinary business. Not begging. Not confessing.

You would close the laptop at six, and it would stay closed.

If this was a little too familiar

If you recognise yourself in the teacups, you are not careless. Most people who work alone carry their whole business in their head, and heads are not built to be ledgers. Things slip, usually the quiet things, usually the hours.
