Repetitive Data Chores Eating Your Week? The Cold Monday Coffee

2026-10-13

Monday, 8:40 am. The coffee was hot when you sat down. Now it has a thin skin on top.

You haven't written a single line of the thing you actually get paid for. Instead, you are on file 61 of 140, renaming it by hand, because the export from the client's system calls everything report_final_NEW (3).csv.

The thought in your head is quiet and a little bitter: I did exactly this last Monday.

Let's call her Mara. She is made up, but you may know her. She builds small dashboards and data fixes for a handful of clients. She is good at it. On paper, she is a developer. In practice, on a lot of mornings, she is a very fast, very tired file clerk.

The chores nobody puts on the invoice

Nobody hires Mara to rename files. Nobody hires her to move columns around, fix dates that flip between day-first and month-first, or delete the same blank rows from the same kind of export.

But it all has to happen before the real work can start. So it happens. Quietly, in small pieces:

  • Ten minutes to sort the downloads into folders.
  • Twenty minutes to clean a spreadsheet that looks almost like last week's.
  • Fifteen minutes to check an endpoint by hand, again, because you don't quite trust your memory of what you checked.

None of it feels big enough to fix. Each chore is small enough to just do. That is the trap.

By Friday, those pieces have eaten a good part of the week. And you can't point at them. There is no line on the invoice that says "the hours I spent getting ready to start".

What it quietly costs

The time is the obvious cost. The other costs are harder to name.

It is the low-grade irritation of doing the same thing twice and knowing it. It is the slight shame of telling a client the work will take until Thursday, when you know part of the reason is a pile of manual tidying.

It is the small fear that you missed something. When you clean forty files by hand, you can't be sure file 27 got the same care as file 3. You become your own unreliable machine.

And it is the feeling that you are not quite the professional you meant to be. You pictured clean work and calm days. You got a Downloads folder and a cold coffee.

The call that changed the tone

Around 10 am, a client rings. She runs a small online shop and wants to know why last month's numbers look different from the month before.

Mara knows why. A date column was read the wrong way round in one batch. She fixed it by hand, but not in the other batch. She can feel the explanation forming, and it is not a good one.

"I'll look into it and send you something today," she says. Her voice is steady. Her stomach is not.

After the call, she sits still for a moment. Then the thought arrives, not dramatic, just clear: This is not a discipline problem. I'm doing a machine's job.

She is not lazy and she is not disorganised. She has simply been the glue between steps for so long that she stopped noticing it was glue.

It doesn't have to be done by hand

The turning point is small. It doesn't come as a big decision. It comes as a question: What if the boring part simply ran, the same way, every time?

Not clever. Not a grand system. Just the chores she keeps repeating, written down once as small, dependable routines. Messy exports go in one end. Tidy, consistent files come out the other. The folder sorts itself. The API check runs the same way before every launch, so she doesn't have to rely on how she feels about it that day.

She doesn't have to invent all of that from scratch on a free Sunday she doesn't have. That is the real relief: someone has already built the dull parts, and she can borrow them.

A different kind of Monday

Picture the same Monday, a few weeks later.

The coffee is still hot at 9:30 because you have only just picked it up for the second time. The exports from the weekend are already in their folders, named sensibly. The dates agree with each other. You glance at the result, the way you'd glance at a clean desk, and nothing jumps out.

The client calls with her question about last month. This time you open the file and the answer is simply there. "Here's what changed, and here's why," you say. It takes four minutes. She says thanks and moves on to her next thing.

You spend the morning on the part of the job you actually like: the thinking, the building, the problem that needed you in particular. At lunch you eat without a second screen open. On Friday, you close the laptop while it is still light outside.

Nothing about your skill changed. You just stopped paying a tax of hours to the same small chores.
