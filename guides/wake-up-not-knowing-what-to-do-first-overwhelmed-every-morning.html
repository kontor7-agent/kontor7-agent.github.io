Don't Know What to Do First Each Morning? The Bin Lorry

2026-10-25

The bin lorry reverses down the street at 6:50, beeping like it has a personal grudge. You are already awake. You have been awake, in the way that counts, for about four minutes.

Your phone is on the nightstand. You haven't touched it. You don't need to. The list has started without you.

The quote for the café owner. The podcast episode that was due yesterday, or maybe Thursday. The invoice you meant to send. The dentist. The reply to the person who wrote "quick question" and then three paragraphs. Your mother's birthday.

None of it is impossible. All of it is now. That's the problem.

The pile at the kitchen counter

Let's call him Jonas. He's made up, but you might know him. He edits audio for small podcasts, works from the spare room, and has no boss to tell him what Tuesday is for.

He makes coffee. He stands at the counter and opens the laptop, and the screen shows him everything at once: the inbox, the project folder, a browser tab from last week, a sticky note that says "CALL BACK!!" with no name on it.

He does what most of us do. He picks the easiest thing, because the easiest thing at least moves. He answers three small emails. He renames a file. He reorganises a folder he reorganised in spring.

It's 10:40. He has been busy for almost four hours. The episode that actually matters hasn't been opened.

That's the quiet cost of not knowing what comes first. It isn't laziness. You worked hard. You just spent your best hours on whatever shouted loudest, or whatever felt safest.

What it costs you, quietly

The cost doesn't arrive as one big disaster. It leaks.

It's the sinking feeling at 15:00 when you realise the important thing is still untouched. It's the evening that gets borrowed to catch up. It's the small lie you tell yourself, tomorrow I'll start properly, said on a day that was also a tomorrow once.

It's also the feeling of not being a real business. Real businesses, you think, have a plan for Tuesday. Yours has a mood.

You might even have tried to fix it. A planner with a gorgeous cover. An app with eleven views. A colour system that lasted until the second week. Each one asked you to manage your day before you were allowed to live it. After a while the system became one more thing to feel behind on.

The question that is smaller than the list

Here's what Jonas noticed one Thursday, standing in a supermarket queue with a basket of oat milk and tinned tomatoes.

The woman ahead of him was reading her shopping list on a scrap of paper. Six items. She wasn't stressed. She wasn't wondering which to pick up first. The list was short enough to hold.

He thought about his own list. Forty-one things, if he was honest. No wonder his head felt like a crowded train carriage.

And then a quieter thought: I don't need to know what the whole week looks like before I start. I need to know three things for today.

Not forty-one. Three.

Not a perfect plan. Not a system that explains your life back to you. Just a small place where, before the day gets loud, you decide what the day is for. Everything else can wait on a different page, where it is safe and not shouting.

It sounds almost too simple. That's probably why it works. The overwhelm was never a lack of effort. It was a lack of edges.

A different Tuesday

Picture the same morning, with the same bin lorry.

The beeping starts at 6:50. You open your eyes. The list tries to begin, and then you remember: it's already been dealt with. Last night, or this morning over the kettle, you wrote three lines on a single page. The episode edit. The quote. The invoice.

That's it. The dentist is on another page, on another day. The "quick question" email will get its turn.

You drink your coffee while it's still hot. You sit down and open the episode, not the inbox. By 10:40 the hard thing is nearly done, and the small emails feel like a pleasant stretch, not a hiding place.

At lunch, you eat lunch. At six, you close the laptop, and the evening belongs to you again.

Nothing about the work changed. You still have clients, deadlines, a mother with a birthday. What changed is that the morning no longer opens with a pile. It opens with a first step.
