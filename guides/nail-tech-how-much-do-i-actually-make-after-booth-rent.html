Nail Tech: How Much Do I Actually Make After Booth Rent?

2026-10-16

The form with one empty box

Thursday, 11:20 am. Your 11:30 hasn't arrived yet, and you're sitting at your station with your phone in one hand and a half-filled rental application in the other.

Name, done. Address, done. Then the box: Average monthly net income.

You look at it for a long time.

You know you're busy. Your book is full most weeks. Your hands ache in the good way, the way that says you worked. But net? After booth rent, after the gel, the files, the tips pot, the cash that went straight into the till at the corner shop for lunch?

You type a number. You delete it. You type a smaller one. You delete that too.

Somewhere between a full week and a real answer, there's a gap. And you've been living in it for a while.

What "busy" hides

Nobody warns you that being booked solid and knowing what you earn are two different skills.

Money reaches you from four directions. Card payments land a day or two later. Cash gets split between the drawer, your pocket and "I'll sort it later." Tips arrive in both. And the rent comes out on the first, whether the month was kind or not.

Then there's the stuff that leaks quietly. The builder gel you reordered twice because you couldn't remember if you'd already ordered it. The no-show on Tuesday at two, the one who texted "so sorry!!" at 2:10, when the slot was already gone. The full set you gave a little off because she's a regular and it felt rude not to.

None of it is dramatic. That's the problem. Every leak is small enough to forgive on its own.

But on a Thursday, with a form in your hand, they add up to a question you can't answer. And that feels worse than it should. You run a real business. You have clients who trust you with their hands, their wedding days, their Friday-night plans. Why can't you say what you take home?

The question you don't say out loud

It isn't really about the rental form.

It's the small knot that shows up on Sunday evenings, when you open your banking app and close it again. It's the way you say "yeah, it's going fine" when your sister asks, and half mean it. It's wondering whether your most popular service, the one everyone books, is the one quietly costing you the most in products and time.

It's the faint suspicion that you might be working hard to stand still. And the fear that if you looked closely, you'd confirm it.

So you don't look. You keep the little notebook, the notes on your phone, the receipts in the drawer under the cuticle oil. You tell yourself you'll sit down properly when it's quieter.

It's never quieter.

The client who arrives at 11:34

She comes in apologising for the traffic, and you wave it away. You settle her hand on the towel, start on the cuticles, and let the rhythm take over.

Halfway through the first coat she says, "Do you ever just sit and think, wow, I actually did this? Own chair, own clients?"

You laugh. "Sometimes."

But it lands. Because you did do this. You built a book from nothing. You learned the work, found the chair, earned the people who come back. The only thing missing isn't skill or nerve. It's a clear view of the numbers sitting underneath all that effort.

And a view is something you can build.

What if the week simply added up?

Imagine a different Sunday evening. Same sofa, same tea going cold.

But this time you spend ten minutes, not an hour of dread. Cash and card from the week sit side by side and match. The no-shows have a price next to them, not a vague feeling of loss, just a figure you can look at calmly. You can see which services genuinely earn their time and which ones you've been doing out of habit.

Nothing about your week changed. You're still the one at the chair. But the fog is gone.

So when a form asks for your monthly income, you don't freeze. You look, you know, you answer. And when a regular asks for another favour on the price, you can decide with a clear head, not a guilty one.

That's what a simple system quietly gives you: not more money overnight, but the ability to see the money you already make. A tracker only organises your own records, though. It's not tax advice, and it doesn't replace a professional when you need one.

The rest of Thursday

Her nails dry under the lamp. She admires them, taps your card reader, and leaves a tip that you, for once, write down straight away.

Then you look at the rental form again. The box is still empty.

This time you don't guess. You put the phone down, and you decide that next week the answer will be sitting there waiting for you, no mental arithmetic needed.

The gap between busy and knowing is smaller than it feels. It just needs a place to live.
