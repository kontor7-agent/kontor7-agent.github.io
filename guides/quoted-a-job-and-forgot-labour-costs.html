Quoted a Job and Forgot Labour Costs? The Wardrobe Doorway Friday

2026-10-14

Friday, 3:40 pm. You are standing in a hallway that smells of fresh sawdust and a little of floor polish.

Marek, an illustrative carpenter, has just fitted the last door on a built-in wardrobe. The customer runs her hand along the edge, opens it, closes it, opens it again. It glides. She smiles.

"It's exactly what you quoted," she says. "I love when that happens."

He smiles back. He says thank you. He means it.

But somewhere behind his ribs, a small cold thought has started to move.

The number on the sheet

He remembers the quote. He wrote it standing in her spare room two weeks ago, tape measure still in his hand. Boards, hinges, runners, handles, paint. He added it up on his phone, rounded it to a friendly figure, and sent it before he left the driveway.

It felt quick. It felt professional.

Now he is looking at that friendly figure in his head, and he cannot find the line for himself. Not the days. Not the Tuesday he lost to a wall that was not straight. Not the two evenings he spent re-cutting a panel because the measurement from the doorway had been off by a finger.

The quote covered the wood. It never covered him.

The tea she hands you

She brings him a mug of tea. It's the good kind, in the nice mug.

He drinks it standing in the hallway because it would be rude not to, and he makes small talk about hinges and soft-close and how the paint will need a week to harden fully. All the while his mind is doing arithmetic it does not want to do.

Six days, give or take. Fuel. Parking. The saw blade that went dull on the second board.

He doesn't say anything. What would he say? "Sorry, I forgot to charge for my work"? She would be kind about it, and that would be worse. She has already told her sister the price. She has already moved the money in her head.

So he nods, takes the payment, and carries his tools back to the van one trip at a time.

What it quietly costs

Here is the part nobody puts on a website.

It is not only the money, though the money is real. It is the drive home with the radio off. It is the Saturday that was supposed to be yours, and the small voice saying you are good with your hands and bad at being a business.

It is the way you start to dread the next quote. You want to be quick, because quick feels confident. But quick is exactly how the labour fell off the page.

And there is the other thing: the next customer will ask for something similar, and you'll quote from memory again, because that is what you did last time and the customer said "exactly what you quoted." It sounded like praise. It was also the sound of the gap closing over your own wages.

None of this makes you a bad tradesperson. It makes you someone who works alone, on site, with a tape measure in one hand and no office behind you.

The Monday that feels different

Imagine another Monday instead.

Same van, same coffee going cold in the cup holder. You pull up outside a new job, a bedroom this time, and you do what you always do. You measure. You look. You tap the wall and make a face.

But when you sit down with the customer, you are not adding things up in your head while she watches. There is a place where the materials sit, a place where your time sits, and a place where the margin shows up on its own. You see, calmly and in one glance, what the job really costs you to do.

You say a number and it does not wobble.

That is the whole difference. Not a cleverer price, just one that includes you. When the customer says "that's exactly what you quoted," you can say "good," and mean it, because the figure had your Tuesdays in it.

On Friday, you drive home with the window down. The week had a shape, and you were paid for all of it.
