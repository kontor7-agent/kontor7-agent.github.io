Awkward Asking a Client About an Unpaid Invoice Again?

2026-10-19

Jonas stands at the edge of the canal lock with a cheese roll in one hand and his phone in the other. It is 12:40 on a Thursday. The screen shows a draft he has opened four times since breakfast.

Hi Katrin, just a quick follow-up on invoice 2024-31...

He deletes "just". He puts it back. "Quick" sounds like he is apologising. "Follow-up" sounds like he is nagging.

This would be the third time. The first email was friendly. The second was friendlier, with a smiley he now regrets. Both got silence. Not a no, not a "we'll pay Friday". Just nothing, which is somehow worse.

The knot you carry on a lunch walk

Jonas edits videos for small companies. He is good at it. Clients tell him so, and then they take five weeks to pay him.

What nobody sees is the small tightening in his stomach each time he opens his banking app. The amount for that job is not a big number. It is rent-sized, though, and he had quietly planned the month around it.

And he has told nobody how it feels to write the third email. It feels like asking for a favour. It feels like admitting he doesn't trust Katrin, who has been nice to him. It feels like standing in the doorway of someone's office, holding out a hand.

So he does what many people in his position do. He waits another few days. He tells himself she is busy. He tells himself that sending a third message would make him "that kind of freelancer".

Meanwhile he works on other projects with half his attention. He checks his inbox more often than he needs to. The job is finished, delivered and praised, and it still sits on his mind like a stone in a shoe.

Watching the water

A narrowboat has drifted into the lock below him. A woman in a fleece closes the gate behind it. Her partner winds a paddle, and the water starts to rise.

Nobody is in a hurry. Nobody apologises to the water. The boat simply lifts, a hand's width at a time, because that is how locks work. There is a sequence, and everyone on the towpath trusts it.

Jonas watches for a while with the cheese roll going cold.

It occurs to him that the lock keepers are not nervous. They don't rehearse in their heads whether it is rude to open the next gate. The step is the step. It is not personal.

And his own situation, he realises, has no step at all. Every time an invoice runs late, he starts from nothing. He invents the tone from scratch, argues with himself about whether it is too soon or too harsh, and then does nothing for a week.

The awkwardness is not really about Katrin. It is about having no plan. Every late payment becomes a fresh emotional decision.

What if the third email was just the third step?

He thinks about it on the walk back, past the bakery and the bike repair shop with its sign for puncture jobs.

What if there were a normal, boring order of things? A friendly note when the due date passes. A clear, plain one a bit later. A firmer one after that, still polite, still professional. Not a mood, just a sequence, the same for every client and every invoice.

Then the third email would not be "begging". It would simply be what comes next. Katrin would receive the same calm message that anyone else would, because that is how his business runs.

He likes that thought more than he expected. It takes the personal sting out of it.

Back at his desk he stops rewriting. He writes four plain sentences, states the invoice number, the amount, the original due date and a clear request for a payment date. He leaves out the smiley and the "just". He sends it at 13:15 and closes the laptop lid halfway, as if he could close the worry too.

Katrin answers the next morning. A short apology, a mix-up in her accounting department, and the payment on Monday. Of course, that does not always happen. But it happens this time, and his main feeling is not triumph. It is a small, embarrassed laugh. All that dread for four sentences.

A different kind of Monday

Imagine the same kind of week a few months later.

It is Monday, 9:10. Jonas has coffee, the window is open, and he opens one overview. Two invoices are due this week, and one is a few days late. Next to the late one it says what comes next, and when. He doesn't have to remember, or work out his tone, or wonder if he is being too soft.

He copies the calm, ready-written message, changes the client's name and sends it. It takes him three minutes. Then he gets on with an edit he actually enjoys.

No knot. No lunchtime walk spent rehearsing sentences. He still cares about his clients, and he still values the relationships. It is just that being paid on time has become a quiet part of the work, not a standing worry.

That is the real change. It isn't harder words. It is that chasing money stops feeling like a personal act and starts feeling like admin you do like any other, with a professional's calm.
