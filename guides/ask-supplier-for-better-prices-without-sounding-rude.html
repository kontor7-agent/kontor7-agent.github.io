Ask a Supplier for Better Prices Without Sounding Rude?

2026-11-07

It is 10:20 on a Tuesday morning and the rain is drumming on the roof of the car.

You are parked outside the trade park, engine off, a half-eaten cheese roll on the passenger seat. On your phone is the new price list from your supplier. Eight pages. Most of the numbers have crept up. Nothing dramatic, just a little here, a little there. The kind of rise you only feel when you add it all together.

Say you're Ines. You run a one-woman screen-print studio: tote bags, posters, the odd run of tea towels for a café that likes your work. The ink supplier has been good to you for four years. They know your name. They once squeezed an urgent order into a Friday van.

And that is exactly the problem.

The draft you keep deleting

You open a new email. Hi Tom, I hope you're well. I was just wondering, and I completely understand if not...

Delete.

Hi Tom, thanks for the new list. Unfortunately the prices are a bit...

Delete.

Because what if it sounds like a complaint? What if Tom takes it personally? What if next time the Friday van is suddenly "fully booked"? You are a tiny customer. You are not a big account with a buyer and a spreadsheet. You are a woman with a cheese roll and a nervous thumb.

So you do what most people do. You close the email, put the phone face down, and tell yourself you'll deal with it later. Later, you will simply pay.

Nobody talks about this bit of running a small business. It isn't glamorous and it isn't dramatic. It's the quiet habit of swallowing a small cost because asking feels rude. One rise at a time, your margin goes thin, and you start to wonder why a busy month doesn't feel like one.

What the quiet yes costs

It's not only money. It's the little sting that arrives every time the invoice comes in. It's the feeling that everyone else seems to negotiate with a calm smile while you rehearse sentences in the car.

And it's the story you tell yourself: real businesses know how to do this. I'm just winging it.

You are not winging it. You have run this studio for four years. You pay on time. You order regularly. That is worth something to a supplier, and politely saying so is not rude. It's ordinary business, the kind both sides expect.

The trouble is the words. Not the thinking, the words. The blank email box at 10:20 with rain on the glass.

The turning point is smaller than you think

Here's what changes things. Not courage. Not a course on negotiation. Just the moment you realise that a good supplier email is mostly made of three plain ingredients: a warm opening that is true, one clear request, and an easy way for the other person to say yes, no, or "let me see what I can do."

That's it. No threats, no apologies, no essay.

When you can see those parts laid out, the email stops being a verdict on your worth and becomes a short note between two people who both want the relationship to continue. Tom would probably rather get a friendly question than quietly lose you to a competitor one cold order at a time.

And if the answer is no? Then you know. You've lost nothing but a few minutes, and you can decide calmly what to do next. The decision stays with you.

A different Tuesday

Picture the same morning with the knot gone.

You park. You open the email. Because the sentences already have a shape, you only have to fill in the gaps: Tom's name, your yearly volume, the one product you'd like a better price on. You read it once, out loud, and it sounds like you on a good day. Kind, steady, clear.

You press send before the roll is finished.

Then you go inside, collect your order, and make small talk with the man at the trade counter about the rain. There is no little voice in the back of your head replaying the email. It's out of your hands, and it was written well enough that you don't need to second-guess it.

Tom replies after lunch. Maybe he offers a small volume discount. Maybe he says the list is fixed but suggests ordering in larger batches twice a year. Maybe he says no, kindly. Whatever the answer, the exchange was polite, and the relationship is intact. You've simply stopped paying for silence.

Back at the studio you hang the first test print on the line, and the afternoon is yours.
