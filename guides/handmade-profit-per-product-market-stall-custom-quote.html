Handmade Profit Per Product? The Custom Quote at a Market Stall

2026-10-14

It is 10:40 on a Saturday, and a woman in a green raincoat is holding one of your mugs up to the light.

"I love these," she says. "I need twelve for my sister's wedding. In this blue, with her initials on the bottom. Could you do that? What would it cost?"

Your heart does a small, happy jump. Twelve. A real order, before lunch.

Then the jump turns into something else.

You don't know the number. You know what the mug sells for on the table. You know roughly what the clay cost. You do not know what the glaze costs per piece, what the initials add in time, or what the firing share is. You don't know what the card reader quietly takes from each sale either.

So you do what you always do. You look thoughtful, you say a figure that sounds fair, and you smile.

"Twelve would be... let's say that much."

She nods. Too fast. You know that nod.

The nod that comes too fast

The rest of the morning, the number sits in your chest like a pebble in a shoe.

You sell a candle holder and a small bowl. A child wants to pay with a note that is far too big for the mug she picked, and you open the cash tin. Your stomach drops again. You started the day with a handful of coins and a vague memory of what you put in last week. Was it enough? You scrape together the change from three different pockets, and the child's dad laughs kindly.

It's fine. Nobody is upset.

But you feel it, that low hum of winging it. The feeling of being a person with a stall, not a person with a business.

And under that hum are the questions you don't say out loud to anyone:

  • Does the bestseller actually earn anything once the fees, the packaging and the pitch fee are taken off?
  • If you count your hours at the wheel, is that mug paying you a wage or a favour?
  • How many of the blue ones are left at home? Is it two, or none?

The woman in the green raincoat did not ask for the answer. You just wished you had it.

What the Sunday notebook knows

On Sunday you sit at the kitchen table with a coffee, the cash tin, a receipt pile and two notebooks. One is for the market. One is for the online orders. They have never once agreed with each other.

You add up the tin. It's close to what you expected, give or take a mystery. You cannot say which products made it. You cannot say what you spent to make them.

The wedding order has been going round your head all night. You decide the number you said was probably too low. You think it might have been much too low. But she has been told, she nodded, and now backing out feels worse than losing money. So you will make twelve blue mugs with initials and quietly resent every one.

That is the part nobody warns you about. It isn't only the money. It's the small sourness that creeps into work you used to love.

The moment it doesn't have to be like this

Imagine a different Saturday.

The woman in the green raincoat holds up the mug and asks her question. And you say, "Give me a minute," and you glance at one page you already trust. Your cost per piece is there. Your fees are there. Your time is there, counted as real time, because it is. You tell her a price, calmly, and the price has a reason behind it.

She might say yes. She might say that's more than she hoped. Either way you are not holding your breath. You are simply a maker telling someone what the work costs.

The cash tin is the same story. Before you left home, you counted the float and wrote it down. At closing, the tin and the sales line up, or they don't, and if they don't you know by how much. The child with the big note is no longer a small crisis.

And on Sunday, the kitchen table is just a table. One place holds the sales from the stall and the shop together. You can see which product earns its keep and which one is a lovely hobby. You see the blue glaze is down to two pieces before you promise twelve of anything.

Nothing dramatic. No spreadsheets spread across the floor. Just the quiet of knowing.

A normal Monday, after

Monday morning. You open the studio, switch on the kiln, and take out the clay.

You are not wondering whether the weekend worked. You already know. You know which three things to make more of, which one to stop making in that size, and which price to nudge up by a little, without the guilt, because the numbers say so and you can say so too.

The pebble in your shoe is gone.

You used to think the stress was part of being a maker. It turns out much of it was just not being able to see.
