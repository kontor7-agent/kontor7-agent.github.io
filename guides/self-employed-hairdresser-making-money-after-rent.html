Self-Employed Hairdresser: Making Money After Rent?

2026-10-23

It's 1:20 on a Thursday. Imogen is sitting on the back step of the salon, a cold sandwich on her knee, a colour processing for another twenty-five minutes.

Her phone is in her hand. The banking app is open. The rent left the account this morning, the way it does on the first of every month, and the number underneath looks a bit smaller than she expected.

She tells herself it's fine. The chair has been busy. The diary has been full since September. Her feet ache in the way that feels like proof.

But busy and making money are two different sentences. She has quietly stopped being sure they mean the same thing.

The sum you do with your thumb

She tries the sum on the step, the way she always does. Tips, plus card, plus the cash from the jar. Minus rent. Minus colour and foils and the towels she keeps meaning to buy in bulk. Minus whatever the card machine takes.

It doesn't come out. It never comes out the same way twice.

Some of the cash went on a coffee for a client's birthday. Some of it went into her coat pocket and then into the weekly shop. A tip came by transfer, and she isn't sure which Saturday that was. Somewhere in the middle of all that sits a number she can't name, and it is supposed to be her wage.

Nobody warns you about this part of working for yourself. You can be good at the work and still not know whether the work is paying you. It feels a little embarrassing, like a professional who can't read her own watch.

The gap on Tuesday

The sandwich is dry. She chews anyway and scrolls back through her diary instead.

Tuesday at 10:15 there is a pencilled name with a line through it. A no-show. She had texted twice and got no answer. She used the gap to restock the shelf, sweep, and answer two messages, and told herself it was a useful hour.

But it wasn't free. The chair was paid for whether anyone sat in it or not. She had known that in a vague way, and never once put a figure beside it.

How many of those lines were there in September? Three? Five? She honestly couldn't say. And that's the quiet cost. It's not just the money that slipped away, it's that she can't see how much, so it can't be fixed and it can't be raised calmly with anyone.

There's a second question underneath it, the one she hasn't said out loud to her sister or anyone else. Are the services she does most actually the ones that pay her best? The long balayage days leave her wrung out. The quick fringe trims fly by. Which of them is carrying her? She has a feeling. A feeling is all she has.

What the knot is made of

It isn't really about the rent. She would cope with a hard number. What sits in her stomach is not knowing, and the way the not-knowing leaks into everything:

  • She says yes to a Sunday booking she doesn't want, just in case.
  • She keeps her prices where they are because she can't prove they should move.
  • She puts off opening her post, and feels vaguely guilty about it every time she sees the pile.

She is not bad with money. She is simply doing a business's worth of sums in her head, between clients, with foil in her other hand.

The timer buzzes in her pocket. Colour's ready.

A different Thursday

Here is the version she doesn't know exists yet, and it is not dramatic at all.

On a Sunday, with a tea, she spends ten quiet minutes with the week in front of her. What came in as cash. What came in as card. What the no-shows cost, written as a plain number instead of a mood. Which services paid for their time and which ones, honestly, didn't.

Then she closes the laptop, and that's all.

So next Thursday at 1:20 she is on the same back step, with the same dry sandwich. The rent has gone out again. But when she glances at the app there is no flinch, because she already knows where the month stands. She isn't hoping, she's just aware, and awareness is calmer than hope.

She can say to a client who has missed twice, evenly and kindly, "I'm going to ask for a small deposit from now on." She doesn't have to dread it, because she knows exactly why it matters.

She can look at her most tiring service and decide on purpose what it should cost. Not out of panic, and not out of guilt, just a decision, made with the page open in front of her.

The chair stays full, but now it feels earned. She can finish the day, lock the door and walk home without running the sum in her head the whole way.
