Staff Keep Asking the Same Questions? The Third Roundabout

2026-10-31

The phone buzzes in its holder on the dashboard as you reach the third roundabout.

You don't need to look. You look anyway.

Jonas: Quick one, what do we tell people who want a loan bike while theirs is in?

You have answered this. Last week. And the week before, standing at the workbench with a wheel in your hands. Probably also in March.

You are on your way to the parts wholesaler, the one errand you do alone, and the one forty-minute stretch where nobody needs anything from you. Apparently that was optimistic.

The question isn't the problem

Let's call you Ines. This is an illustration, not a real person. You run a small bike workshop with two people helping you. You like them. Jonas is careful and good with customers. Priya is quick and never complains.

That's what makes it hard to say what's wrong.

Nobody is being lazy. Nobody is asking because they don't care. They ask because the answer lives in exactly one place: your head. How to word the "your bike will be ready Thursday" message. What to say when a supplier ships the wrong tyres. How you handle a customer who wants a discount on a repair already done. Which price you quote for a basic service, and why not lower.

So every day, in small pieces, you hand it over again. A sentence here. A text there. Each one takes thirty seconds.

The cost doesn't show up in thirty seconds. It shows up at 5:40 pm, when you realise you haven't done a single thing you planned. It shows up on Sunday, when you catch yourself rehearsing tomorrow's answers in the shower.

What it quietly costs

You're not tired from the work. You like the work. You're tired from being the only place the answers exist.

You can't really switch off, because if you do, somebody waits. Or worse, somebody guesses. And a guess made on your behalf, in your shop, with your name on the door, is a small knot in your stomach you carry around all day.

You've thought, more than once, it would be faster to just do it myself. Then you remember you already do. That's the whole problem.

There's a quieter worry underneath. If you can't step away for a morning, what kind of business is this? Not one you could take a week's holiday from. Not one that feels like a real company, with a handbook and a calm way of doing things. More like a very busy habit with your name on it.

You don't say that out loud. You answer the text instead.

The turning point, at a red light

You reply with your thumb at the red light, a bit curt, and you hate that. Jonas didn't deserve curt. He asked a fair question.

Then something small happens. He writes back: Sorry, I know you've said this before. I just don't want to get it wrong.

You read it twice.

He doesn't want to get it wrong. He's not testing your patience. He's asking for something to hold on to, and you've been handing it to him one breath at a time instead of once, in writing, where he could find it himself.

The light turns green. You drive on, and for the first time in weeks the thought isn't why do they keep asking? It's why have I never written this down?

The honest answer is dull. Writing things down feels like a big project. You imagine a binder, a weekend, a blank page. So you postpone it and keep answering, because answering is easy and the binder is not.

But maybe it doesn't have to be a binder. Maybe it's just the next ten answers, said once, in your own voice, in a form someone else can pick up. A starting point instead of a blank page. Something that gets you from "I know this" to "it's on paper" without needing a free weekend you'll never have.

A different Monday

Picture the same shop in a few weeks.

It's Monday, 8:45. The shutter goes up. The kettle is on. Priya is already at the bench.

A customer asks for a loan bike. Jonas doesn't text you. He opens the page, reads how you like it handled, and says it the way you would. Warm, clear, no apology. You're across the room fitting a brake cable and you don't even look up.

A supplier email comes in with the wrong delivery date. Jonas drafts a reply from a starting point you gave him. You skim it at lunch, change one line, and that's it.

At 4 pm you notice something odd. Your phone hasn't buzzed all afternoon. You check it anyway, half expecting it to be broken.

It isn't. Things are just going on without you. Not badly. Not carelessly. Just without your voice having to be in the room every time.

That's the relief. Not more free time on a spreadsheet, but the knot loosening. The sense that the business knows some of its own answers.
