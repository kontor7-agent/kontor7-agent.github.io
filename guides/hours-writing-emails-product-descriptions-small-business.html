Hours on Emails and Product Descriptions? The Kiln Timer

2026-10-23

The kiln timer says 2 hours 40 minutes until it is safe to open the lid. Mara has decided to use the time well.

It is Monday, 9:10. She is in her small ceramics studio with a mug of tea going lukewarm and her laptop balanced on a wedging table. Clay dust is already settling on the keyboard.

On the screen are three tabs. A product page for a new speckled bowl set with the description box still empty. A reply to a customer asking whether the milk jug is dishwasher safe. A half-written email to the glaze supplier about a delivery that is a week late.

She types: Handmade with love. She deletes it. It sounds like every other shop on the internet.

The writing nobody counted

Mara became a potter to work with her hands. Nobody mentioned that a good part of the job would be words.

There are product descriptions, each needing to sound like her and not like a catalogue. There are customer replies: the polite ones, the "where is my parcel" ones, the "can you make it in blue" ones. There are supplier emails, a note for the market stall, a short ad for the Saturday helper she needs.

Individually, none of it is hard. That is the trap. A ten-minute email here, forty minutes on a description there, and by Friday you cannot point to where the week went. You only know the pots you meant to photograph are still on the shelf.

The cost is quiet. It is the Tuesday evening you spend polishing a sentence about a mug. It is the reply you put off because you cannot find the right tone, so the customer waits, and you feel a bit unprofessional. It is the nagging thought that other people seem to write this stuff in five minutes.

The blank-page tax

Mara has tried the obvious things. She opened an AI chat once, typed write a product description for a ceramic bowl, and got three paragraphs that could have described any bowl, anywhere. Smooth, generic, a little too excited. She spent longer fixing it than she would have spent writing it herself, so she closed the tab and told herself it was not for her.

What she did not see was that the problem was not the tool. It was the question. A vague request gets a vague answer. And when you are tired at 9:10 on a Monday, vague is the only kind of request you have the energy to make.

The real cost was not the writing itself. It was starting from nothing, every single time.

The moment it clicks

The turning point is small, as they usually are.

A friend who runs a plant stall sends her a message while the kiln ticks away. It is just a screenshot of something she had saved: a prompt with gaps in square brackets. [product], [who it is for], [three real details], [tone]. Fill in the blanks, paste it in, and what comes back sounds like someone who has actually met the product.

Mara looks at it for a moment. Nothing clever is going on. It is just a good question, already written, waiting for her details.

She fills in the bowl set. Speckled stoneware, made in small batches, holds a proper portion of soup, a slightly uneven rim because it is thrown by hand. She adds that she wants it warm and plain, no exclamation marks.

What comes back is not perfect. She changes two words and cuts one line. But it is 80% there, and it sounds like her on a good day. It took four minutes.

She sits back and notices something odd. The knot between her shoulders has loosened a little.

A different kind of Monday

Imagine the same studio a few weeks later.

The kiln is ticking again. Mara has a stack of questions she already knows how to ask. The dishwasher reply takes a minute, because she checks it against what she knows is true and sends it in her own voice. The supplier email goes out polite and clear, with the delivery date in the first line. The helper ad, which she had postponed for a fortnight, is drafted before the tea goes cold.

She still reads everything. She still decides what goes out under her name. That part is hers, and it should be.

But the blank page is gone. By ten o'clock the words are done, and she is wrapping bowls instead of staring at a cursor. When the kiln finally clicks off, she has time to take the photographs she kept putting off, in good morning light.

The evening, when it comes, belongs to her.
