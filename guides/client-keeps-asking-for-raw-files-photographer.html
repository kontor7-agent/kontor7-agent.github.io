Client Keeps Asking for Raw Files? The Chestnut Stand

2026-11-08

Saturday, 14:10. The Christmas market is packed and smells of cinnamon and woodsmoke. Mara has been standing in the chestnut queue for ten minutes, gloves off, phone in hand.

She is an illustrative character, a portrait and small-event photographer. She is not a real person.

The message on her screen is from a client whose shoot she delivered three weeks ago.

"Hi Mara, just checking, could you also send over the raw files? Would be great to have them just in case. Thanks!"

It is the third time this has come up. The first time, she said she'd think about it. The second time, she changed the subject. Now it is back, friendly and casual, and her stomach does the small drop it always does.

The question under the question

The request itself isn't what bothers her. Sometimes raw files are a fair thing to ask for, and sometimes they're a different job with a different price. What bothers her is that she can't answer on the spot.

What did she promise this client? Ten edited images? Twenty? Was there a line about originals anywhere? Was the deposit paid? She remembers sending a gallery link. Did the final invoice go out, or did it stay in drafts because the week got away from her?

The answers are somewhere. A quote email from September. A WhatsApp voice note. A spreadsheet tab she hasn't opened since the summer weddings. A folder called Final_final_2.

The woman in front of her buys a paper cone of chestnuts. The queue moves one step.

Mara types a reply. Deletes it. Types a friendlier one that says yes, of course. Deletes that too, because a yes she hasn't thought through is how she ended up giving away three weekends last year.

What it quietly costs

Nobody warns you about this part of the work. The camera is the easy bit. The hard bit is the fog afterwards.

It shows up as small things. A reply you postpone for two days because you don't know what you'd be replying to. A faint dread when a client's name appears in your inbox. A "sure, no problem" that you resent by Monday.

And there's the awkward feeling underneath: that a professional would just know. That a real studio would have the answer on one screen, and you are standing in the cold with a phone, guessing.

Mara isn't bad at her job. Her pictures are good, her clients like her. But her business lives in six places, and on days like this it shows.

The shift

She gets her chestnuts. They're too hot to hold, so she tucks the cone under her arm and leans on a railing by the mulled wine stall.

A friend of hers, a wedding photographer, once said something offhand: "I don't decide anything in my inbox anymore. I look at the client's line first." One line per client. Shoot date, what was agreed, deposit, gallery, invoice. Nothing clever, just all in one place.

Mara had nodded and gone back to her folders.

Standing there with warm paper against her ribs, she finally sees what has been missing. She doesn't need a better way to say no. She needs to know where she stands before she says anything at all. The words come easily once the facts are in front of her.

The Monday after

Picture a different Monday for her.

She opens one file with her coffee. There is a row for every client: the shoot, the date, the deposit received, the gallery deadline, the invoice and its status. A glance tells her which shoots are delivered, which galleries are still open, and who owes her a final payment.

The raw-files message arrives. She finds the client. The agreement is right there, in her own words, from the day she wrote it. She can answer in two calm sentences, whatever the answer is. Maybe: here is what's included, and if you'd like originals as well, here is how I'd handle that. A friendly yes, a friendly no, or a friendly "let me quote that." Her choice, made with the facts in front of her, not squeezed out of her at a market stall.

Then she closes the laptop and goes for a walk. The client doesn't sulk. Clients mostly respect a person who knows her own terms.

That is what a simple system changes. It doesn't make you tougher. It makes you steady.
