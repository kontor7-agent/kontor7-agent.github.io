Tuesday, 9:40 am: The Client Call Where Your Script Failed

2026-10-12

The call has been running for eleven minutes when your client says the sentence you were hoping to avoid.

"Could you just show me quickly how you got to these numbers?"

You share your screen. Your cursor hovers over a folder called final_scripts_NEW. Inside it: clean_v2.py, clean_v2_fixed.py, clean_v3_USE_THIS.py. A small cold feeling spreads through your chest.

You are, let's say, a freelance analyst named Mara. She is made up, but you may know her. She is good at her job. She just happens to hold it together with a pile of scripts she wrote at odd hours, each one solving exactly one problem on exactly one day.

The script that worked yesterday

You pick clean_v3_USE_THIS.py and run it. For a second, nothing happens. Then a red wall of text appears. Something about a column that no longer exists. The client's export has a new header, and nobody told you.

"No worries, take your time," the client says, in the voice people use when they are already worrying a little.

You say something calm about "a small format change on their side." It's even true. But what you are thinking is: I should have this solved by now. Why am I still rebuilding the same thing every month?

That is the quiet part nobody mentions. It isn't the hard problems that wear you down. It's the small, repeating ones: the same messy export, the same pile of files that need renaming and sorting, the same API you need to look over before you can say "yes, this is fine to connect." Each one takes only an hour. But they come back, and you meet them as strangers every time.

What it quietly costs

Nobody sends an invoice for this part of the work.

It costs the Thursday afternoon you spent hunting for the script you wrote in spring. It costs the slight hesitation before you say "sure, I can turn that around by tomorrow," because you're not certain which version of your own tools is the good one. It costs a little of the confidence you want to carry into a call.

And it costs the way you review things. When you check an API in a hurry, you check what you remember to check. On a good day that's plenty. On a Tuesday with three deadlines, you are relying on your memory to be complete, and you know, somewhere underneath, that it might not be.

None of this is a disaster. That's what makes it so easy to live with for years.

The sentence you say to yourself afterwards

The call ends. You fix the header problem in four minutes, which somehow makes it worse. Four minutes. The whole awkward moment could have been avoided by a tool that expected the data to be a bit untidy.

You make coffee and stand at the window. Across the street a delivery driver is carrying three boxes up a staircase, and you think about how he almost certainly doesn't reinvent the stairs each morning.

Then the thought arrives, plain and without drama: It doesn't have to be like this.

Not a bigger project. Not a grand system. Just a small set of dependable tools, written by someone who has already met these problems, that behave the same way each time. Tools that you open, point at a file, and trust.

A different Monday

Picture it a few weeks later.

It's Monday, 8:15. The radiator ticks. A new export has landed from a client, the usual tangle of duplicates, odd dates, and stray spaces. You don't brace yourself. You run the cleaning kit, and while it works you open the post, answer one message, and refill your cup.

The downloads folder, which used to be a small daily embarrassment, is sorted by the time you sit down. The API you were asked to review has a checklist beside it, so you aren't relying on memory. You note what you found, you write it up plainly, and you move on.

At 9:40 the client call starts. "Could you show me how you got to these numbers?"

"Of course," you say, and you mean it. You share your screen. The folder is tidy. The names make sense. The run finishes without a single red line.

Nobody on the call notices anything special, which is exactly the point. The work simply looks like what it is: careful, steady, professional. And you feel something you hadn't noticed was missing for a long time. You feel unhurried.

What actually changes

It isn't that the problems vanish. Clients will still change headers without telling you. Folders will still fill up. What changes is that you stop meeting each problem for the first time. The repeating parts become quiet background, and your attention goes back to the part only you can do: thinking.

That is worth more than the hours, although the hours are nice too.
