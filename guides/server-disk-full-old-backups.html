Server Disk Full From Old Backups? The Hut on the Hill

2026-10-15

The soup has just arrived. It is Thursday, 12:40, and you are in a mountain hut with a view that deserves your full attention.

You took the day off on purpose. Three weeks ago you wrote it in the calendar, and you told two clients you would be offline.

Then your phone buzzes on the wooden bench. One bar of signal. An email from the monitoring service: Disk usage 100%.

Let's call you Marta. You are a freelance web developer, and you look after a handful of small sites on one rented server. A florist's shop. A physio's booking page. A photographer's gallery. Nothing huge, but all of it is yours to keep running.

You read the subject line twice. The soup goes lukewarm.

The thought that arrives before the panic

It is not "what broke?" It is quieter and worse: "I think I know exactly what this is, and I should have dealt with it months ago."

Because you remember. Two years ago you set up a nightly backup in one tired afternoon. It worked. You saw the first file appear, felt a small glow of professionalism, and moved on.

Nobody ever told it to stop.

So every night since then, a fresh copy has landed in the same folder. Quietly. Faithfully. Seven hundred nights of faithful.

The florist's webshop is taking orders for Friday deliveries. If the disk is full, the database can't write. A customer clicks "order" and gets an error page.

You hold the phone up toward the window, hoping for a second bar.

What it costs when nobody cleans up

The hut is lovely, but you are no longer in it. You are in a terminal, in your head, scrolling through folders with names like backup_2024-11-03_final and backup_old_DONT_DELETE.

You don't remember which of those you wrote. You don't remember why one says DONT_DELETE. Deleting the wrong thing on a server with clients' data is not a mistake you want to make from a bench with 3% battery.

So you do what many of us do. You find a spot at the edge of the terrace where the signal holds, log in on a tiny screen, and delete the oldest files by hand, one careful command at a time, your thumb hovering over every Enter.

It works. The disk drops to 91%. The shop is fine. Nobody noticed.

But the day is gone. The soup is cold, your friends have finished theirs, and someone asks if everything is okay. You say "just a small thing at work" and smile.

That is the real cost. Not the gigabytes, but the feeling that your day off was only on loan.

The walk down

On the way down the valley, a thought keeps circling. It is not "I'm bad at this." It is gentler: "Why is this still a thing I have to remember?"

Backups should be the most boring part of your week. They exist so you can stop worrying. And yet here the backup itself became the worry, simply because it grew without anyone watching.

Somewhere around the third switchback you realise the missing piece was never effort. It was a rule. Something that says, calmly and in advance: keep this much, let the rest go, and tell me what you did.

Not a clever system. Just a small, predictable habit that runs without you, and leaves a note behind.

A different Monday

Picture the same week, with that rule in place.

Monday, 8:30. Coffee. You open one small logfile, a plain text note from last night's run. It says what was saved, what was tidied away, and how much room is left. Thirty seconds. You nod.

There is no scrolling through mystery folders. No guessing which copy matters. If you ever want to change what gets removed, you can first ask for a trial run that only shows what would happen and touches nothing. Then you decide.

Thursday comes. You take the day off. The soup is hot, the view is the point, and your phone stays in your pocket, because nothing in it needs you.

That is all. No drama, no heroics. The server just stays quietly in order, the way you wish the rest of your admin would.
