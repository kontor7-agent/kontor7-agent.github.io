How Long Keep Receipts Self-Employed? The Shredder Aisle

2026-11-07

It is 12:40 on a Thursday, and Mara is standing in the office-supply aisle holding a shredder she doesn't want to buy.

It is £59. It has a little window so you can see the paper go in. She is a freelance copywriter, six years in, and she has come here on her lunch break with one thought looping in her head.

Can I throw any of it away?

The box is in her boot. It is a printer-paper box, the lid soft at the corners. Inside: thermal slips gone the colour of weak tea, card statements, a train ticket from a client workshop, a screwed-up receipt for a monitor arm. Some of it is from this year. Some of it is older than her current laptop.

She does not know which pieces matter. Nobody told her there would be a test.

The question nobody asks out loud

On her phone, in the queue for the till, she types: how long do I have to keep receipts self employed.

The answers are a fog. One site says a number of years. Another says it depends. A forum thread has six people disagreeing, and one of them is angry.

She puts the shredder back on the shelf.

The truth is, the answer is not the same everywhere. It depends on your country and your situation, and it is the kind of thing a tax professional or your tax authority can answer for you properly. A forum cannot. That alone is a small relief. The fog isn't a sign you've failed. The question really does have more than one answer.

But the real knot is underneath, and it isn't about years.

The knot is: am I even keeping the right things?

What the box quietly costs

Mara drives home with the box on the back seat, and the box does what it always does. It follows her around.

It is there on Sunday evenings, when the week should be winding down. It is there when a friend says "how's business?" and she says "great" with her mouth while the rest of her thinks about the slip she might have lost. It is there every January, when she spends a whole weekend on the floor with piles named things like maybe and definitely and what is this.

None of that shows up on an invoice. But it is real.

It is the feeling of not being a proper business. Proper businesses, she assumes, have a drawer. A system. A calm voice that says that one goes here.

She has a box and a vague dread.

The turning point is smaller than you'd think

That evening she doesn't buy anything. She doesn't make a grand plan. She tips the box onto the kitchen table and does one thing differently.

She doesn't sort by year. She doesn't try to decide what to bin. She just picks up the first slip, the monitor arm, and asks a smaller question than the one that has haunted her for months.

What was this, and what job was it for?

It takes about twenty seconds. She writes it down in one row: date, what it was, what it was for, where the paper lives. Then she picks up the next.

By the time the kettle has boiled twice, she has forty rows and a strange, light feeling in her chest. It is not the feeling of being finished. It is the feeling of being able to see.

The shredder question changes shape. It stops being can I throw it away? and becomes what do I know about each of these? Once every slip has a row, a name and a home, the keeping rules are something she can ask her tax professional about with a tidy list in hand, not a box and an apology.

A normal Thursday, a few months on

Imagine the same Thursday, but different.

At 12:40 she is not in a shop aisle. She is eating soup at her desk. A receipt from the morning's printer ink is next to the keyboard, and she logs it before the soup cools. Date, amount, what it was for. Ten seconds. The slip goes into a labelled folder, and the folder goes back on the shelf.

There is a short list on the side of her screen that says what a year-end needs from her, and a little bar showing how much of it is already done. Most of it is already done. Nothing about it is dramatic.

When her accountant sends the usual email in January, Mara reads it with a coffee, not a stomach drop. She does not clear a weekend. She does not sit on the floor.

And when she wonders how long she should hold on to the old papers, she has a clean record to take to someone qualified to answer. She isn't guessing at a kitchen table. She is a person with a real answer coming and a tidy folder to hand over.

The box is gone. Not shredded in a panic. Just emptied, row by row, into something she can see.

If you recognise the box

Maybe yours is a drawer, a carrier bag, a camera roll of blurry photos, or an email folder called receipts???. The shape doesn't matter. The feeling does: the quiet suspicion that you are keeping the wrong things, or the right things in the wrong place, and that it will all come due at once.

It doesn't have to. You don't need to learn bookkeeping this week. You need a place where each slip becomes one calm row.
