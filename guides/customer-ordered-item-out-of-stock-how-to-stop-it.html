Customer Ordered What You Ran Out Of? The Caravan Kettle

2026-10-24

The kettle clicks off. You pour the water over the teabag, and your phone buzzes on the little fold-out table.

New order: 2 × Eucalyptus Door Wreath, large.

You smile. Then the smile stops halfway.

You are in a rented caravan on the coast, the first proper weekend away in months. The rain is tapping on the roof. Someone two pitches down is frying bacon. And somewhere in your head, a small cold voice says: Do I even have two large ones?

You think of the shelf in the spare room. You think of the last large wreath. Wasn't that the one you sent to Hamburg on Wednesday? Or was it the one with the bent twig that you kept back?

You don't know. That is the worst part. Not the empty shelf, but the not knowing.

The Holiday That Isn't One

You open the laptop on your knees. Your shop dashboard says "in stock". Of course it does. It says what you last told it, three weeks ago, on a good day.

So you open the notebook photo on your phone. Then the spreadsheet you started in spring, with the column called "Maybe?". Then your sent-orders folder, counting backwards, tea going cold beside you.

Twenty minutes later you have an answer. One large wreath. The customer ordered two.

You type a message. Delete it. Type it again.

Hi, so sorry, there's been a mix-up with my stock…

You hate the word mix-up. It sounds like someone who doesn't know their own business. And that is exactly how you feel, sitting in a caravan, in borrowed slippers, apologising to a stranger for something you could have seen coming.

The weekend doesn't ruin itself. It just goes grey at the edges. You walk on the beach and keep composing sentences. You wonder how many other orders are sitting there, waiting for things you no longer have.

What It Quietly Costs

Nobody warns you how small this problem looks and how much space it takes.

It takes your Sunday afternoon, when you could be somewhere else in your head. It takes a little of the trust people placed in you when they typed in their card number. It takes the pleasant surprise of a new order, which now arrives with a flinch.

And it takes your sense of being a real shop. You make beautiful things, and you answer every message kindly. Yet some mornings you feel like a person guessing, with a lot of good taste and a bad memory.

You tell yourself it's just how it is when you're small. You tell yourself you'll sort it "when things calm down". But things don't calm down by themselves. They calm down when you can see them.

The Thought on the Drive Home

On the drive back, the windscreen wipers swish and your partner hums along to the radio. You're not really thinking about stock. You're thinking about a friend who runs a flower stall and always seems unbothered. Once, she said something offhand: I just glance at one page on Monday. It tells me what's getting low.

One page. One glance.

It lands differently now. You realise you've been treating this as a memory problem, as if you simply ought to try harder to remember. But it isn't about remembering. You were never supposed to carry the whole shelf in your head. A small, quiet system could hold it for you. It could say, gently, this is running low, and this is the day to reorder, long before a customer finds out first.

It doesn't need to be clever. It needs to be there, and it needs to be true.

A Normal Monday, Later

Picture a Monday a few weeks from now.

You come downstairs. The kitchen smells of toast. You open one sheet while the kettle heats, the same kettle, only now it's just a kettle.

Two items are marked as low. You already knew about one of them, and the other you'd forgotten. You send a supplier message in under a minute: no panic, no guessing, no counting boxes in the spare room with your phone torch.

Then a new order comes in. You glance at the screen, and instead of the flinch, there is a small, ordinary good. You have it. It's on the shelf. It's going out Tuesday.

The shelf stops surprising you. That's the whole change, and it is bigger than it sounds. You answer customers faster, because you aren't dreading what you'll find. You order a bit earlier and a bit calmer. You plan a weekend away and actually take the weekend.

The next time you sit in a caravan with a cup of tea, the phone buzzes and it's just a lovely order, and you can drink your tea while it's still warm.
