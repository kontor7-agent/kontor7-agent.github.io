Forgot Which Jobs You Applied To? The Tram Stop Voicemail

2026-10-16

The tram is four minutes away, and it is raining sideways.

You are standing under the glass shelter with a coffee going cold in your hand when the phone buzzes. A number you don't know. You answer, and a warm voice says, "Hi Jonas, it's Petra from Haldane & Co. I'm following up on your application for the content lead role. Do you have a minute?"

You do not have a minute. You have a blank.

Haldane. Haldane. Was that the one with the job ad that mentioned a four-day week? Or the one where you spent a whole Sunday rewriting the cover letter? Was it three weeks ago, or six? Did you already follow up, or were you just about to?

"Of course," you say, in the bright voice you keep for moments like this. "Great to hear from you."

You have no idea what you wrote to them.

The sheet you meant to keep

You hold the call together for six minutes. Petra is kind. She asks what drew you to the role, and you give an answer that is vaguely true and mostly improvised. When the tram arrives you let it go by, because you can't hear over the brakes.

Afterwards you stand there, a little hot in the face, and think: this is what it costs.

Not the rejection. You've made peace with rejection, or you're working on it. It's the not knowing. The nagging feeling that somewhere in your inbox is a reply you missed, a deadline that passed on Thursday, a friendly "just checking in" you were supposed to send nine days ago and didn't, because you couldn't remember if you'd already sent one.

You do have a system, technically. There's a note on your phone. There's a half-finished table in a document called "jobs_NEW_final2." There are about forty browser tabs, and a folder in your email called "Applied?" with a question mark in the name, which says everything.

None of it agrees with the others.

The quiet arithmetic

That evening you sit down to sort it out, and it takes ninety minutes. You scroll sent mail. You search for company names. You find an application you'd completely forgotten and a reply from a recruiter, two weeks old, asking for your availability. You answer it with a stomach full of small stones.

It is not that you're careless. You've been careful in a dozen other ways: tailoring each letter, reading each company's website, noting who the hiring manager was. But all that care leaks away if it has nowhere to land.

And there's a second cost, the one nobody mentions. Every time you open your laptop to look for work, there's a low hum of dread, because you know you'll first have to reconstruct where things stand. So you put it off. You tell yourself you'll do it tomorrow, when you're fresher.

The search gets slower because the admin around it is heavy.

The part where it gets lighter

The turning point isn't dramatic. It's a Wednesday, and you are watching a friend, a translator who has nothing to do with your world, tidy her own pipeline on a laptop at the next table in a library café. She has one page. Names on the left, dates in the middle, a column that says what happens next.

"That's it?" you ask.

"That's it," she says. "I just look at it in the morning. It tells me who to nudge."

You look at her screen for a moment longer than is polite. Nothing fancy. Just everything in one place, and a calm look on her face.

It occurs to you that the problem was never your memory. Memory was just the wrong place to keep it. A search like this is a dozen small conversations running in parallel, and nobody is built to hold a dozen conversations in their head. You were using your brain as a filing cabinet and then blaming it for losing papers.

What a normal Monday could feel like

Picture it a few weeks on.

It's Monday, 8:40. The kettle is clicking off. You open one file and the day tells you what it needs: two follow-ups are due this week, one interview is on Thursday, and one application has gone quiet long enough that a short, polite nudge makes sense. You write the nudge. It takes three minutes, because you can see exactly what you sent before and when.

For Thursday's interview, you aren't improvising answers on the tram. You've got your stories ready, the project that went sideways and what you learned, the time you disagreed with a client and kept the relationship. They're written down in a shape you can rehearse, not hunted for in your own head under pressure.

Then the phone buzzes on the table. An unknown number.

You pick up. "Hi, this is Jonas."

"Hi Jonas, it's Petra from Haldane & Co..."

And you know exactly who she is, what you applied for, and what you said last time. You say something like, "Petra, good to hear from you. I was just looking at the role again." And it's true.

That's the whole difference. Not a better applicant, just a calmer one. The same person, with the knot in the stomach loosened.
