Hundreds of Files With Random Names? The Photographer's Handover

2026-10-14

The client's face is small in the corner of your screen. Behind it, your own desktop, shared with everyone on the call.

"Could you just pull up the final selection?" she says.

You open the folder. Three hundred and twelve files. IMG_4471.jpg. DSC00093 (2).jpg. scan_final2.pdf. Untitled-7_copy.png. a8f3c1e0-b2.jpg, which you have never seen before in your life.

You scroll. The silence on the call is only a few seconds long. It feels like a week.

"Sorry, one moment. I'm just finding it."

You are not just finding it. You are hoping it will appear.

The folder nobody sees

Most people who work alone have one of these. A folder that grew by itself, one rushed save at a time. The camera named things one way. The client's upload tool named them another. The scanner had its own opinion. Your own tired Friday-self added _final, then _final_v2, then _USE_THIS_ONE.

Nobody warns you about this part of being self-employed. There is no one to say, "let's agree on a naming scheme." It is just you, the files, and the quiet belief that you will sort it out later.

Later never has a calendar slot.

So the cost hides in small places. Five minutes looking for a file before a call. A deliverable sent in the wrong version, and the careful apology afterwards. The faint feeling, when someone says "can you send me the originals," that you are not quite the professional you present yourself as.

It is not a disaster. That is exactly why it never gets fixed.

The afternoon you decide to deal with it

After the call, you sit with the folder open. You think: right, I'll rename them. All of them. Today.

You right-click the first file. F2. Type a name. Enter. Next one.

By file twelve, you notice you are already guessing. Was this the one from the harbour shoot, or the one from the market? You open it to check. Then you have to find your place again. Twenty files in, you have renamed two shoots into the wrong pattern and you can't tell which.

You also feel a particular kind of dread. What if you rename something and break a link? What if two files end up with the same name and one quietly overwrites the other? You close the window.

The folder stays as it was. You tell yourself you'll do it on a quiet day.

There is no quiet day. You know that.

The thought that changes the shape of it

A week later, a friend who builds websites is on your sofa, watching you hunt for an old invoice scan. She doesn't say anything clever. She just asks:

"Why are you doing that by hand?"

You start to explain that it's complicated, that the files are all different, that you'd have to check each one.

"No," she says. "I mean, why are you the one doing the repeating?"

It lands strangely. You had thought of this as a discipline problem. Something you ought to be better at. Not as a job a machine could do while you make tea.

The repeating part, the same rule applied three hundred times, was never meant for your hands. Your part is the deciding. What should a good name look like? Which folder should an invoice live in? Where should the old duplicates go?

The doing can be handed off. And, you learn, it can be handed off gently: shown to you first as a preview, nothing touched until you say so, with a record of every change so that any of it can be taken back.

That last detail is what lets your shoulders drop. The fear was never the work. It was the one wrong click you couldn't reverse.

An ordinary Monday, afterwards

Picture it a few weeks on.

It is Monday, 9:05. The coffee is still too hot. Your downloads folder holds exactly what you downloaded since Friday, and nothing from March.

The client call comes in at half past. "Could you pull up the final selection?"

You type three letters into the folder search. The file is there, named with the date, the project and the version, in plain words that make sense to a person. You share it. It opens. Nobody waits.

"Perfect," she says, and moves on to the next point.

That's the whole scene. Nothing dramatic. No one praises your folder structure. But something small in your chest, the thing that used to tighten when someone said "can you just send me", stays loose.

The duplicates are gone, tucked into an archive rather than deleted. The scans have a home. The random-letter files have names you can read. And the folder tidies itself on a schedule, so the mess never gets the chance to become a project again.

You didn't become a more organised person. You just stopped being the only one doing the sorting.
