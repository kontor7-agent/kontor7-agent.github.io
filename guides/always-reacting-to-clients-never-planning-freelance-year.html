Always Reacting to Clients? The Garage Wall Calendar

2026-11-07

You are sitting in the waiting area of a garage, third plastic chair from the door. The car is up on the ramp for its annual check. Your laptop is open on your knees, because of course it is.

There are four unread messages from one client. The first says quick question. The second says sorry, one more thing. The third is just a screenshot with a red circle on it.

You answer all of them. It takes twenty-five minutes. Nobody thanks you, and nobody should have to. That is the job.

Then you look up.

The calendar on the wall

Behind the reception desk hangs a big paper year planner, the kind with all twelve months on one sheet. It's covered in coloured marker. Blue for MOTs. Green for tyre deliveries. A red box around the second week of August that says CLOSED – Spain!! with two exclamation marks.

The owner, a woman in a grey fleece, is on the phone. She flips a page and says, without looking, "No, I can fit you in the 14th. The 21st I'm away."

She knows where her year is. She knows it the way you know your own front door.

You try to say the same about yours. What are you doing in March? You don't know. Not in a worried way, more in a blank way. March is a place where clients will happen to you.

The week that belongs to whoever wrote last

You have been self-employed long enough to know how it goes. Monday starts with a plan. By ten past nine somebody has an urgent thing, and the plan folds up like a deckchair.

By Wednesday you are working on the loudest request, not the most important one. By Friday you are tired in that specific way. You were busy all week and cannot say with what.

Nobody talks about the quiet cost of this. You haven't sent your own newsletter in five months. You keep meaning to raise your rates, but there's never a calm week to do it in. A holiday is something you'll book once things settle down, and things have not settled down since the spring before last.

It isn't that you are bad at planning. You are good at responding. Clients reward responding. They don't send a card when you protect a Thursday for your own work.

And so the year gets written by other people, one email at a time.

"When are you away?"

The woman in the fleece puts the phone down and catches you looking.

"Sorry," you say. "I like your calendar."

"It's the only reason I'm still sane," she says, and laughs. "I do it every December. Holidays first, then the busy weeks, then everything else goes around them. If I leave it to the year itself, the year eats me."

She goes back to her screen. It was a throwaway line, the kind people say at desks.

But it lands somewhere. Holidays first. Everything else goes around them.

You have always done it the other way round. Work first, then whatever is left over, if anything is.

The car comes back, and so does the thought

The car passes. A small advisory about a tyre, nothing dramatic. You pay, you thank her, you drive home with the radio off.

At a red light you notice you're not thinking about the red-circle screenshot. You're thinking about a blank sheet of paper with twelve months on it, and how it might feel to put the first marker down yourself.

Not a grand plan. Not a five-year vision. Just a few things written in before anyone asks:

  • the week you're away,
  • the days you won't take calls,
  • the month you'll finally send your new rates.

Nothing about it is clever. It just makes your year visible before it fills up.

An ordinary Monday, later

Picture it, a few months on. It's Monday, 8:40. The kettle is on. You open one page, the week, and it already knows what matters. Three things are written in your own handwriting, and one of them is not for a client at all.

An email arrives at 9:12, urgent as ever. You read it and you can see the week. You can see that Thursday is protected, that the invoice follow-up has its own slot, that August has a box with your name on it. So you answer calmly: "I can do that on Wednesday afternoon."

The sentence feels odd in your mouth, and then it feels ordinary.

The client says Wednesday is fine. Most of the time they do.

By Friday you can tell, in plain words, what you did this week. Some of it was reacting, because it always will be. Some of it was yours.

You close the laptop at six. The evening stays where it is.
