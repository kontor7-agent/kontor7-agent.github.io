Black Friday Posts Not Planned? The Parcel Tape Moment

2026-11-04

The parcel tape squeals as Leona pulls it across the box. It is Wednesday, a little after ten in the morning, and the garage smells of cardboard and cold concrete. Three ceramic mugs, wrapped in paper, go in. Then the card with the handwritten thank-you.

Her phone lies face-up on the packing table. It lights up. Another shop she follows has posted a countdown: Black Friday is coming. Sneak peek tomorrow.

She pauses with the tape in her hand.

She has a Black Friday offer, sort of. She has talked about it with herself in the shower. She has typed half a caption in her notes app, somewhere between a shopping list and a reminder to call the glaze supplier. What she does not have is a plan. No dates, no pictures taken, no idea what goes out on which day.

She tells herself there is still time. Then she looks at the date on the phone and the thought changes. Everyone else seems to have done this weeks ago.

The quiet maths of a Wednesday

Here is what nobody says out loud when you run a small shop alone.

Posting is not hard. Deciding is hard. Every time you open the app you have to invent the whole thing again. What do I show? Which picture? Do I mention the offer yet, or is it too early? Is it too late? Do I sound pushy?

So you post when you have the energy, which is never the week you need it. You post three times, vanish for nine days, then post a slightly breathless apology. Then you feel you owe the algorithm a favour.

Leona knows this pattern. She has lived it every November. A blur of late posts, a discount announced at the last minute, a pile of orders she cannot pack fast enough, and a flat feeling in December. She sold things. She cannot say whether it was the right things, or whether she showed up in a way she is proud of.

She tapes the box shut and writes the address. Her mind does not stay in the garage.

What it costs you

It rarely costs a dramatic amount in one go. It costs small things that add up.

It costs the Sunday afternoon you spend scrolling other people's feeds "for inspiration" and end up feeling worse. It costs the coffee that goes cold while you stare at a blank caption box. It costs the feeling that a real business would have this sorted by now.

It is not true, of course. A real business is a person who makes mugs and answers emails. But at ten past ten on a Wednesday, with the countdown post glowing on the table, it feels true.

The sheet of paper her sister slid across the table

That evening Leona's sister Jo comes round for dinner. Jo does not own a business. She works in logistics for a hospital, and she looks at problems the way other people look at furniture: what is in the way and where could it go.

Leona tells her about the countdown post. About the notes app. About the caption she has rewritten four times.

Jo does not say "you should have started earlier." She asks one question.

"If you could see the whole month on one page, what would you want it to show?"

Leona laughs, because nobody has ever asked her that. Then she answers properly, and the answer surprises her. She does not want a clever strategy. She wants to see the days. She wants to know that on the Monday before, there is a quiet post that says what is coming. That the Thursday has a photo of the kiln. That Friday has the offer, written calmly, once. That the Saturday has a thank-you.

She wants to stop deciding every morning.

Jo says, "That sounds like a calendar, not a campaign."

Something in Leona's chest loosens. Not because the work is done. Because it suddenly has edges.

A different Monday

Picture the same garage a week later.

It is Monday morning, a little after eight. The tape squeals again. But the phone on the packing table is quiet, because she does not need it. The posts for the week are already written, in a list she can glance at. Today's photo is in the camera roll, taken last Saturday in good light with a cup of tea beside her.

She does not think about Black Friday while she packs the mugs. She thinks about whether the blue glaze looks more green on screen, which is the kind of problem she actually enjoys.

At lunchtime she posts, in two minutes, and puts the phone away. No spiral. No comparing.

In the evening she is on the sofa, not at the laptop. The month still has its busy days. But they are visible, and what you can see you can carry.

It doesn't have to be a big system

You do not need a marketing degree or a team. For most solo shops and freelancers, the shift is smaller than that: one place where the whole month lives, where each post has a day and a purpose, and where the captions and hashtags you liked last time are waiting instead of lost in a notes app.

That is the difference between panic and a Monday that feels ordinary.
