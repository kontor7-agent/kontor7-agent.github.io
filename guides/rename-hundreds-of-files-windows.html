Renaming Hundreds of Files on Windows? Friday, 4:40 pm

2026-10-20

Friday, 4:40 pm. Marta has one hour before her week off begins.

The suitcase is by the door. The out-of-office is written but not switched on. On her screen is a folder with 340 files, and the client has asked for one last thing before the handover: every file named the way their site team names things.

What she has instead is IMG_4471.jpg. Scan_0032.pdf. Document (3) final.pdf. Document (3) final v2.pdf.

She is an interior designer, and she works alone. Over four months, site photos, supplier sheets and measurement drawings have piled into one folder. Nothing in it is wrong. It just isn't named.

So she does what anyone would do. She clicks the first file, presses F2, types the new name and presses Enter. Then the next one.

The F2 rhythm

After ten files she has found a rhythm. After thirty she has stopped reading what she types. After sixty she catches herself typing Kitchen_South_Wall_07 on a file that is clearly the bathroom.

She undoes it. Then she can't remember whether she undid the right one.

That is the part nobody warns you about. The work isn't hard. It's just the kind of work where one slip stays invisible until someone else finds it. Usually it's the client, usually on a call, usually while you're sharing your screen.

She thinks about that call. "Marta, which one is the south wall again?" The small silence while you scroll.

She types faster.

214 of 340

At 5:25 pm she is on file 214. Her wrist aches. The suitcase is still by the door, and somewhere in the flat a phone buzzes. It's her sister, asking if they are still leaving at seven.

Marta does the maths the way tired people do. A hundred and twenty-six files to go. At this speed, that's another hour at least. Then she has to check them all, because she no longer trusts the ones from the first hour. Then the handover email. Then packing.

The quiet cost isn't the hour. It's what the hour is standing in front of. She wanted to leave this week feeling like someone who runs a proper business. Instead she is leaving the way she always does: with a half-closed laptop, a slightly sick feeling, and a promise to herself to "sort the folders properly when I'm back".

She has made that promise before. The folders have never once been sorted properly.

The thought that arrives sideways

She stops. She stretches her fingers and looks out of the window, where a neighbour is carrying a stack of pizza boxes up the stairs.

And a thought arrives, not dramatic, just plain: Why am I doing this with my hands?

She knows how a name follows a pattern. Room, wall, number. She could say it out loud in one sentence. A person who can say a rule in one sentence shouldn't be typing it 340 times.

It isn't a clever idea. It's more of a loosening, like unclenching a fist you didn't know was closed. This task has a shape, and anything with a shape can be handed to something that never gets tired and never types Kitchen on a bathroom.

But the old worry follows right behind it. What if it renames everything wrong and I can't get it back?

That is the fear that keeps most of us on F2. Doing it by hand is slow, but it is slow and safe. You can see every change. So the real question isn't "can this be automated?" It's "can I look first, and can I take it back?"

The same Friday, a different ending

Imagine the other version.

It's 4:40 pm. The folder is just as messy. But Marta looks at a list of what would change before anything does. Old name on the left, new name on the right, nothing touched yet. She scrolls it the way you'd check a shopping list. Two lines look off. She fixes the rule, not the files, and looks again.

Then she lets it go, and it's done before the kettle has boiled.

And if something still looks wrong on Monday, there's a plain record of every change that she can reverse. No panic. No guessing which file used to be which.

At 5:10 she attaches the folder to the handover email. At 5:15 she switches on the out-of-office. Her sister gets a message: "Early. Come at six if you like."

A normal Monday, later

The part that matters isn't the Friday. It's the Monday three weeks on, when a new client sends 200 photos called IMG_ something and asks for them "tidy by Wednesday".

On that Monday, Marta doesn't feel a drop in her stomach. She doesn't open the folder with the particular sigh people have for jobs they have been putting off. She glances at it the way you glance at a tidy shelf.

The Downloads folder doesn't gather mystery files for months anymore. The duplicates don't breed. Her mornings go to the actual work: the sketches, the calls, the supplier who finally answered.

Nothing about her job changed. What changed is that the small, repeatable chores stopped eating the edges of her day.

That is all most of us are after. Not a perfect system, just a Friday afternoon that ends when it's supposed to.
