Keep Running Out of Stock? The Coffee Morning Remark

2026-10-20

It is Saturday, a little after ten, and you are holding a paper cup of weak tea in the village hall. Someone has brought a lemon cake. The radiator ticks. You are not working, and for twenty minutes you have almost believed it.

Then a woman from your street touches your arm. She is smiling.

"I tried to order your lavender soap for my sister's birthday," she says. "Three weeks ago. It said sold out, so I got her something else. I thought you'd stopped making it!"

You smile back. You say something light, something like oh, thank you for trying, it's been so busy. Your face does the right thing. Your stomach does not.

Three weeks. The lavender soap is your best seller. And you did not know it was gone.

The number you thought you knew

Let's call you Mira. You make soaps and bath salts in a spare room that smells permanently of something nice. You sell online, and now and then at a market. You are good at the making. You are even getting good at the photographing.

What you carry around is a feeling about your stock. I think there's still plenty of lavender oil. I think I have about thirty bars curing. I think the jars are on order.

The word doing all the work is think.

On the walk home you try to rebuild the last month in your head. A busy weekend after a small shout-out online. A box you packed in a hurry. A batch you meant to start on Tuesday and then didn't, because a parcel went missing and you spent the afternoon on hold. Somewhere in there the shelf emptied, and the shop quietly said "sold out" to people while you were busy being a person.

Nobody emailed to complain. That is the odd part. The customers who find an empty page do not write to you. They shrug and buy something else, somewhere else.

What it quietly costs

You get home and open the laptop on the kitchen table, still in your coat.

You don't have a stock problem the way a big shop has one. You have a noticing problem. The information exists, scattered: a notebook by the curing rack, a half-remembered count, three supplier emails, a spreadsheet you started in the spring and stopped trusting in May.

And you start adding up what the not-knowing has cost, without wanting to.

There is the sale you didn't make. Maybe several. There is the birthday present that went elsewhere. There is the small flinch every time you write "back in stock soon" without knowing whether that is true. There is the supplier you always call in a rush, which means you never get to choose the better deal.

And there is the quieter thing. The feeling of not being a real business. Real businesses know what is on their shelves. You know roughly, on a good day, with the right mood and a good memory.

You close the laptop and make a second cup of tea you don't want.

The turning point is smaller than you think

Here is what changes. It is not a dramatic reinvention. It is a Sunday afternoon with the notebook open next to the screen, and a thought that arrives quite gently:

What if I never had to remember this?

Not remember better. Not try harder. Just not have to carry it.

You realise that what you wanted at the coffee morning was not a perfect memory. You wanted a quiet nudge, lavender is getting low, now is the time, arriving while there is still plenty of time to act. A shelf that speaks up before the customer has to.

That is all. One place where your stock lives, and a simple way for it to tell you when something is running down. The rest is just you, doing what you already do well.

An ordinary Monday, afterwards

Picture it a few weeks on.

It is Monday morning, around nine. The kettle is on. You open one file and look at it for about two minutes, the way you might glance at a weather app. Most things are calm. One line has turned a different colour: the shea butter is getting low, and your usual supplier takes about a week to deliver.

So you order it. Today, with no panic and no rush fee, in the middle of your tea.

The lavender soap never goes dark on the shop page. A customer in another town buys it for her sister's birthday, and neither of you thinks about it again. That is the whole point. The best result is a thing that quietly doesn't happen.

At the next coffee morning, someone mentions your soap again. This time it is only to say it smelled lovely. You hold your paper cup, take a bite of whatever cake it is, and for once the tea tastes like tea.
