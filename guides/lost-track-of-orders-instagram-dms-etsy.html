Orders in Instagram DMs and Etsy, Lost Track? The Tape Gun

2026-11-05

It is Saturday, 10:40 in the morning, and the kitchen table is a packing station.

There are twelve padded envelopes, a tape gun that has run out of tape, and a phone propped against the fruit bowl. On the phone, Instagram. On the laptop, Etsy. Between them, a pink sticky note that says "Mara – 2 mugs?? blue?"

You are Ines, in this story. You make small ceramics in a spare room, and you have been busy for weeks. Busy is what you wanted. So why does your stomach feel tight?

Two inboxes, one memory

Etsy orders are easy. They arrive with a name, an address, a neat little receipt. You can see them.

The DMs are different.

Someone writes on Tuesday: "Do you still have the speckled bowl? I'd take two!" You answer, "Yes, lovely, I'll set them aside." Then a market weekend happens. Then a delivery of clay. Then it is Saturday, and you are scrolling up through three hundred messages with your thumb, looking for the word "bowl".

You find one. You are not sure it is the right one. Was that person paid? Did they give an address? Or was that the other bowl person, the one with the heart emoji?

Nobody talks about this part. From the outside, you run a cheerful little shop. From the inside, you carry a list in your head that keeps slipping.

What it quietly costs

It costs small, sharp moments.

The message you send at 9 pm: "Hi! Just checking, did I already send your order?" You hate how it sounds. It sounds like you don't know your own business.

It costs the speckled bowls you promised twice, because the second promise lived in a different app from the first. One person gets a kind apology and a refund. You close the chat and feel your face go warm.

It costs the Sunday afternoon you spend "catching up", which is really re-reading messages and hoping nothing has fallen through.

And it costs a quieter thing. You start to wonder whether a real shop would feel this unsteady. Whether other makers have a trick you missed. Whether you are simply bad at this.

You are not bad at this. You are holding two systems with one head.

The pause over the tape gun

Back at the table, you find a fresh roll of tape and, while you are rummaging in the drawer, you notice something small.

Every order you can actually remember is one that has a line in a single place. The Etsy ones. They have a date, an item, a status. You never lose them, because they live somewhere that holds them for you.

The lost ones are the ones that only exist as a chat, a sticky note, or a hope.

It is not a talent problem. It is a where-does-this-live problem.

You sit down for a moment with your coffee, which has gone lukewarm, and the thought arrives very plainly: it doesn't have to be like this. Not a big app. Not a subscription with forty menus. Just one list. Every order, from every place, in the same few columns: who, what, paid or not, ship by when. And next to it, what is actually on your shelf.

That is all. A place where Tuesday's "I'll set them aside" becomes a line you can see on Saturday.

A different Saturday

Picture it a few weeks later.

You sit down at 10:40 with the same coffee, but it is hot. The DM from Tuesday has already become a line: two speckled bowls, paid, ship by Friday. Someone has been sent a tracking number. Someone else is waiting on a glaze batch, and you know it, and so do they, because you told them a real date.

The tape gun works. The envelopes are labelled in the order you will carry them to the post office. You glance at the screen and it tells you the thing you used to dread to find out: the blue mugs are down to three, so the next firing should include them.

No scrolling. No "just checking". When a customer writes, "Hi, is my order on its way?", you answer in ten seconds, warmly, with the actual answer.

The shop did not get smaller. It got quieter in your head.

By noon you are done. You walk to the post office with a tote bag over your shoulder, and the queue is just a queue. You are thinking about lunch. You are thinking about a new glaze. Not about who ordered what.
