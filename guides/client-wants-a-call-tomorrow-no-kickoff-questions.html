Client Wants a Call Tomorrow, No Kickoff Questions Ready?

2026-10-19

The email arrives while you're standing in the supermarket queue, one hand on a basket with oat milk and a tired lemon.

"Great to meet you! Could we jump on a quick call tomorrow at 10 to get started?"

Your thumb hovers over the screen. You type "Sure, sounds good!" because that is what you type. Then the queue moves, the cashier says something about a bag, and the real thought arrives, late and a little cold:

What on earth do I ask them?

The question you can't answer yet

Imagine Mira, a freelance illustrator. She's been working for herself for three years. She's good. Clients say so. But she has never had a proper way to start a project, only a habit of starting it.

So at the checkout she runs through what she knows. A name. A budget range mentioned once, in passing. A deadline described as "soonish". Nothing about who approves the work, who sends the files, or what "quick call" means to someone who has never hired a freelancer.

She pays. She walks home with the bag hanging a little heavier than it should.

That's the quiet part nobody mentions. It isn't that you can't do the work. It's that the first conversation decides how the whole project feels, and you're about to wing it with a half-remembered list in your head.

What winging it quietly costs

Mira has done this before. She remembers the project where the call went warmly, everyone smiled, and three weeks later she learned the client expected five rounds of changes. She had assumed two. Nobody had lied. Nobody had asked.

She remembers the logo job where the logins arrived on day nine. The one where the person who signed off turned out not to be the person on the call.

None of it was dramatic. It was small friction, repeated: a missing password here, a vague "we'll see" there, an evening spent rewriting something because the real goal surfaced late.

And underneath it, a feeling she'd never quite name. That she was not running a real business. Just being very agreeable, very fast.

Eleven at night, a notes app, and a blank page

That evening she opens a blank document and types Questions for tomorrow. Then nothing. The cursor blinks. She writes "budget?" and deletes it, because asking sounds awkward. She writes "timeline" and stares at it.

She wonders whether other freelancers have some secret list. She suspects they do. She suspects they don't feel like this on the evening before a call.

At midnight she closes the laptop with five scribbled lines and a faint dread. She sleeps badly. In the morning she rehearses sentences in the shower and loses them by the time the kettle clicks off.

The turning point is smaller than you think

Here is the thing Mira realises later, not in a flash, just on an ordinary afternoon: a kickoff call isn't a performance. It's a conversation with a shape.

The shape is always roughly the same. What are we making, and why? What does done look like? Who decides? What do you need from me, and what do I need from you? When do we talk next?

Once a conversation has a shape, you stop inventing it from scratch every time. You stop standing in queues with a rising pulse. You walk in with a page that already holds the questions, and the client, quite often, is relieved. Somebody finally knows how this goes.

That is the whole idea. Not a bigger process. Just a start that doesn't depend on how your week happened to go.

A different Monday

Picture the same email arriving, same supermarket, same tired lemon.

"Could we jump on a call tomorrow at 10?"

This time Mira replies, "Yes, happy to. I'll send a short intake form beforehand so we can use the time well." She does it from the queue. It takes a minute. She puts the phone away and, for once, hears the cashier.

The next morning she opens the call with a calm agenda in front of her. She asks the questions in order. She writes down what she hears. When the client says "soonish", she asks, kindly, "Is there a date that matters?" and a real date appears.

At the end she says what she's understood, in two sentences, and the client says, "Yes, exactly that." Nobody feels pushed. Everyone knows what happens next.

She closes the laptop at ten to eleven with a short list of access she needs and a welcome email already half-written. The afternoon is hers.

Nothing about her talent changed. The start did.
