Made a Mistake on a Client Project? The Car Park Ticket

2026-10-28

The barrier at the hospital car park is stuck, and you are third in the queue.

It is 8:10 on a Tuesday. Your mother's appointment is at half past. She is checking her handbag for the letter. You are checking your phone, because a client's name just appeared in your notifications, and you know the subject line before you open it.

You are Marit, a freelance designer. You are not a real person, but you might be someone you know. Three weeks ago you sent a print-ready file for a bakery's opening-day flyers. Last night the client collected two thousand copies.

The phone number on them is wrong. One digit.

The reply you start four times

You do not open the email at first. You just look at the preview: Hi Marit, quick question about the flyers…

Your stomach does the thing. The knot, then the heat in your face, then the fast, ugly arithmetic. Reprint costs. Whose fault. Whether you checked the proof. Whether they signed off the proof. Whether it matters who signed off the proof.

The barrier lifts. You drive forward and park badly.

Your mother says, "Are you all right?"

"Work thing," you say. "It's fine."

It is not fine, but it is also not a catastrophe, and that is the part your body cannot tell yet.

In the waiting area, under a poster about hand-washing, you open a draft and type Hi Jonas, I'm so, so sorry. You delete so, so. You type Thank you for flagging this. That sounds like a call-centre. You type As you may recall, the proof was approved on the 3rd. You delete the whole line, fast, as if someone might read it over your shoulder.

Four openings. None of them survive.

What the silence costs

Here is the thing nobody says out loud about mistakes when you work alone. The mistake itself is rarely the heavy part. The heavy part is the hours between noticing it and answering.

There is no colleague to glance at. No manager to say, "Send this, it's fine." There is only you, and the little voice that says a real professional would never have made this error.

So you do what many of us do. You over-apologise in your head, you under-answer in the inbox, and the silence stretches. Jonas, who was only mildly annoyed at eight o'clock, starts to wonder by noon why you haven't replied. Your delay starts to look like the bigger problem.

You also lose the rest of the day. You sit with a client's invoice you were about to send, and now it feels wrong to send it. You half-write a discount offer you cannot afford. You rehearse a defence nobody asked for.

And quietly, underneath, you wonder whether this is the email that ends the relationship.

The sentence you were looking for

Your mother is called in. You sit alone with the hand-washing poster and your phone.

And something small shifts. You stop trying to write the perfect apology. You ask yourself a plainer question: what would a calm person say here?

Not a grovelling person. Not a defensive one. A calm one. Someone who names what happened in one sentence, says what they will do about it, and says when. Someone who treats the client as an adult who would rather have a plan than a performance.

You realise you have written that kind of email before. For other people. It was never hard when the mistake was somebody else's. The words came easily when your own stomach was not involved.

The trouble was never that you could not write it. The trouble was writing it while the heat was still in your face.

So you stop typing, breathe, and open a note on your phone. You jot down three plain facts: what went wrong, what you can fix, by when. Nothing else. Within a few minutes, the reply is shorter than any of your four drafts, and a lot kinder to both of you.

You send it before your mother is back.

A Tuesday that goes on

Jonas answers within the hour. He is not delighted, but he is workable. The reprint cost gets talked about like an ordinary business problem, not a verdict. By lunchtime the corrected file is on its way, and you are eating a sandwich from the hospital café without tasting the guilt in it.

What stays with you afterwards is not the mistake. It is how long it took to answer, and how much of the morning it ate.

Imagine a Tuesday where it doesn't. The notification arrives, the knot appears, but you already have somewhere to go. A starting point for the apology. A starting point for the awkward price conversation next month. A steady first line for the invoice reminder you keep postponing. You change the names and the facts, you read it once in your own voice, and you send it. The error stays an error, not a referendum on whether you are a real business.

That is all it takes to turn a bad hour into a bad ten minutes.
