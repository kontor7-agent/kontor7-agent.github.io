Client Pays 45 Days Late Every Month? The Shoe Shop Queue

2026-10-28

It is a Saturday in late August, 11:20, and Ilse is third in the queue at the children's shoe shop.

Her daughter is trying on trainers a size up. The assistant is friendly. The queue moves slowly. Ilse has done this sum in her head four times already: the shoes, the rent on the first, the electricity.

The invoice for the big client went out on the 12th of July. Payment terms: 30 days. It is now the 24th of August, and nothing has arrived.

She knows it will arrive. It always does. Around day 45, give or take two days, every single month. Never on time. Never missing. Just late, in a way that is hard to complain about, because nobody is lying and nothing is wrong enough to make a fuss.

The card reader beeps. She holds her breath for half a second, even though the money is there. It is her money, from the month before last, which is why it is only just there.

The kind of late that has no villain

This is the part nobody warns you about. A client who vanishes is a clear problem. A client who pays 45 days late every month is a fog.

You can't be angry, because they always pay. You can't relax, because you never know the day. So you live in the gap. You plan your month around a date that only exists in your head.

Ilse's gap has a shape. Day 30 passes, and a small knot appears in her stomach. Day 35, she checks the bank app while the kettle boils. Day 40, she checks it again, at the traffic lights. Around day 43 she starts drafting a message in her head, something light and breezy, "just a quick note about the invoice from July!", and by day 44 she has made it so light that it says nothing at all.

Then the money arrives, and she feels a flood of relief, followed by a flicker of something worse: I'll be doing exactly this again in four weeks.

The cost doesn't show up as one big number. It shows up as a quiet tax on everything. She puts off buying a better monitor. She says yes to a small job she doesn't want, because the cash will land sooner. She snaps at her partner over a forgotten pasta order, and the pasta is not the problem.

The call that changed one sentence

Two weeks later, Ilse is on a call with the same client. It is Monday morning, 9:30, and she is half in the conversation and half watching a wasp at the window.

The project manager is cheerful. They love the new illustrations. Could she start the next batch this week?

Ilse hears herself say, "Of course." Then, before she can stop it, she hears the other sentence in her head, the one she has never said: and could we talk about when invoices get paid?

She doesn't say it. Not this time. The wasp finds the open window and leaves.

But after the call she sits for a moment and notices something. She has never actually written the 45 days down anywhere. Not the date she sent each invoice, not the day each payment landed, not the pattern. It has all lived as a feeling. A feeling is very hard to bring to a conversation. A pattern is not.

That is the turning point, and it is small. It is not a big decision or a dramatic email. It is just the thought that this might not be a mood she has to carry. It might be something she can see, and see clearly enough to talk about, calmly, in the same voice she uses to say her rate.

What a Monday feels like when the fog lifts

Picture the same Monday a few months on.

Ilse opens one sheet with her coffee. Not a complicated one. It simply shows what is open, how many days each invoice has been waiting, and what the sensible next step is. Today it says one invoice is at day 31 and a friendly reminder is due. She has the wording ready, polite and clear, so she doesn't have to invent it while her stomach tightens.

She sends it. It takes four minutes. She doesn't rewrite it nine times.

She also sees, in plain numbers, that this client's payments have landed around day 45 for months. That is no longer a worry that visits her at traffic lights. It is a fact she can bring to the next conversation about terms, about timing, about what works for both sides. Whether they change anything is up to them. But she is no longer asking from inside a fog.

And the days between? They are quieter. She doesn't check the bank app at the kettle. The knot at day 30 has loosened, because day 30 now has a plan attached to it.

The shoe shop, in this picture, is just a shoe shop. Her daughter picks the blue ones. The card reader beeps. Ilse doesn't hold her breath.
