Shop Asked for Wholesale Prices? The Hilltop Signal Bar

2026-10-29

The one bar of signal lives by the summit cross. Everyone who walks up here knows it.

Mira stands there with her flask in one hand and her phone in the other. It is a Saturday in late October, the first proper day off in weeks. Her sister is photographing the valley. The wind is cold and the view is the kind you'd put on a postcard.

Then the email loads.

Hello, we love your linen-bound notebooks. We run a small gift shop and would like to stock them. Could you send us your wholesale prices?

She reads it twice. Her stomach does two things at once. One part of her lights up, because a real shop wants her work. The other part goes quiet and tight.

Wholesale prices. She has never written any.

The number she can't see

Mira sells her notebooks at markets and in a small online shop. She knows what people pay for one. She does not know what one costs her.

She knows roughly what the linen cost, and the paper, and the thread. She is much less sure about the platform fees, the packaging, the stall fee she splits across a hundred things, or the hours at the kitchen table with the bone folder.

And now a shop would pay less per piece, and want a few at a time.

Would I make anything?

She does what everyone does on a hilltop with one bar of signal. She does the sum in her head. Half the retail price, minus the materials, and then it blurs. She reaches the hours and the fees and the packaging, and the sum slides away from her.

"Everything okay?" her sister calls.

"Someone wants to buy my notebooks," Mira says. It comes out sounding like bad news.

Two ways to reply, both wrong

On the walk down she runs the two replies she could send.

The first is the quick yes. She names a price that feels fair and doesn't feel greedy. It is the same instinct that sets her market prices, a number that feels okay. She would send it before dinner and feel proud until the first order of fifty arrived, and she saw the stack of linen, the folding, the hours.

The second is the quiet no. Sorry, I'm not set up for wholesale at the moment. It is polite and safe, and it is also a small door closing on something she wanted. She would tell herself it was a business decision. Part of her would know it was fear.

Her sister points at a sheep. Mira nods without seeing it.

What bothers her most is not the shop. It is that she has sold these notebooks for three years, and she cannot say what any one of them earns her. Not roughly. Not honestly.

The turn, somewhere near the stile

At the stile at the bottom of the field, waiting for her sister to climb over, she realises something small.

The email doesn't need an answer today. A shop asking for prices is not a shop waiting by the door. It is a person who will happily read a calm reply on Tuesday.

What she needs first is not a clever price. She needs to see the notebook: what it costs, what the fees take, what is left. She needs that for one product, laid out plainly, so she isn't guessing.

With that, the question would stop being am I allowed to say this number and become this is the number, and here is why. Maybe she'd find room for a wholesale price. Maybe she'd find the retail price was too low all along. Or maybe she'd find that wholesale only works for the bigger notebook, and she could say so kindly.

Any of those is better than a number pulled out of the wind.

She types two words into her phone's notes, with her thumb and her one bar: Reply Tuesday.

It is the first time the tightness lets go.

Tuesday, another kind of morning

Picture that Tuesday.

The kettle is on. The notebooks are stacked on the shelf, the market crate empty by the door. On the screen there's one calm page. It shows her notebook with its materials, its fees, its share of the packaging, and what's left at the end.

She isn't doing sums on a hilltop. She isn't bracing. She looks at what's there, thinks for a minute, and writes a reply in her own voice, short and friendly, with a price she can stand behind.

If the shop says yes, she knows what a bigger order means for her week. If they say it's too much, she won't lie awake wondering whether she should have gone lower. She'll know why she didn't.

That is what she is really buying, more than a wholesale price: the quiet of knowing her own numbers when someone asks for them.
