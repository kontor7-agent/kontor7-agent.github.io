Sent Your First Freelance Invoice? The Gift-Wrap Counter

2026-10-31

Mara is standing at the gift-wrap counter of a department store, 12:20 on a Thursday, holding a scarf she doesn't need to wrap. Her phone is in her other hand. The queue is three people long, which is exactly enough time to open her sent folder and look at it again.

There it is. Sent at 9:07. Subject line: "Invoice". One PDF attached. Her first one.

She wrote the PDF in a word processor, in a font she picked because it looked serious. Her name at the top. The client's name below. A number. A sentence that said "Thank you for your business."

Now, in the queue, with the smell of cinnamon and cardboard, one thought keeps circling: Is that actually what an invoice looks like?

The quiet worry nobody mentions

Nobody tells you about this part. Finding the client was the exciting bit. Doing the work was the proud bit. Then came the small, practical moment where you have to ask for money in a document you've never made before.

Mara had looked at a few examples online. They all looked a little different. One was a table with six columns. One was almost empty. One had a paragraph of small print she didn't understand.

So she did what many of us do. She made something that felt about right and pressed send before she could think too long.

Now the doubts arrive one by one, the way thoughts do when your hands are busy with something else.

Did she give it a number she could find again? Did she say when she expected payment, or only hope they'd guess? Did the total look like a total, or like a favour? What if the client's finance person opens it and thinks, oh, she's new?

That last one stings more than the others. Not because it's likely, but because it touches the real fear underneath: I'm not sure I look like a real business.

The email that made it worse

At 12:31 her phone buzzes. It's the client. Friendly, quick:

"Thanks! Quick question, can you send the due date and which account to pay into? Our system needs it."

Mara's stomach drops, just slightly. Neither was on the page. She had assumed it was obvious. It wasn't obvious to a system that needs a date in a box.

The person ahead of her gets their parcel. The assistant looks up and says, "Next, please."

Mara doesn't move. She says, "Sorry, one second," and steps aside, as though the queue were the problem.

Nothing terrible has happened. The client isn't annoyed. The money isn't lost. But she feels something small and familiar: the sense of winging it. Of being a person with a laptop and a good heart, doing something that looks like business without quite being sure it is.

What she did on the bench outside

She walked out past the perfume counters and sat on a bench near the entrance. She typed a short, warm reply with the missing details. She hit send. Within ten minutes the client wrote back: "Perfect, thank you."

That was it. No drama.

But sitting there with her phone warm in her hand, Mara had a quieter realisation. The problem wasn't that she had done it wrong. The problem was that she had made it up from nothing, alone, in a hurry, and that she'd have to do it again next month. And the month after.

Every invoice from scratch. Every time a small dread about what she'd forgotten.

It doesn't have to work that way. A first invoice can be something you fill in rather than invent. One page, already laid out, where the boxes are in the right order and the total adds itself up. The due date has a place to live. Your name looks like the name of someone who runs a business, because you do.

(Everyone's situation differs, and a template is not a replacement for a tax professional. It simply takes the blank page out of the moment.)

The next one, on an ordinary Monday

Picture four weeks later. It's Monday morning. The kettle is on. Mara finishes a project and, instead of the flutter in her chest, she opens a file she already trusts.

She types in the client. The amount. The date. She watches the total fill in on its own.

She saves it as a PDF, attaches it, and sends it before the kettle clicks off.

She doesn't check her sent folder at lunch. She doesn't rehearse worst cases in a queue. The invoice is simply done, the way a good handshake is done, and she gets on with the actual work she likes.

That's the whole change. Not a new personality. Not a bigger business. Just one thing she no longer has to improvise.

If you recognise yourself in the queue

Maybe your invoice went out this morning and you're feeling that same little wobble. Maybe you haven't sent it yet because you're not sure what it should look like. Either way, you're allowed to be new at this. Nearly everyone who works for themselves has stood somewhere ordinary, phone in hand, wondering if they did it right.
