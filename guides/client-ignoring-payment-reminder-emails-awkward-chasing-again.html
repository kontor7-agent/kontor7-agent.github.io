Client Ignoring Payment Reminders? The Hard Shoulder Wait

2026-10-21

It is 10:20 on a Thursday morning, and Mira is sitting in a car with its hazard lights on.

The engine gave up on the ring road, just after the exit for the retail park. She rolled onto the hard shoulder, called the breakdown service, and was told: forty minutes, maybe fifty.

So now she waits. The car ticks as it cools. Lorries shake the whole frame when they pass. And because there is nothing else to do, she opens her email.

She tells herself it is to check for work. It is not.

The email that is not there

Mira is a freelance illustrator. Three weeks ago she delivered twelve spot illustrations for a client's annual report. The invoice went out the same afternoon. The due date came and went a week and a half ago.

She sent a friendly reminder. Just a quick nudge in case this slipped through! She even used the exclamation mark, the one she uses when she is nervous.

Nothing.

She sent a second one a few days later, a little shorter, a little stiffer. Nothing again. Not even a "we're on it."

Now she scrolls past the empty space where an answer should be, and her stomach does the small, familiar drop. The rent comes out on the first. The car, it turns out, is going to cost something too.

She opens a new draft. Hi Jonas, I wanted to follow up once more…

She deletes it. It sounds like begging. She types it again, harder. Now it sounds like a threat. She deletes that too.

A lorry thunders past and the car shivers.

What the silence costs

Here is the part Mira would not say out loud to anyone.

The money matters, but it is not the whole weight. The weight is the awkwardness. Jonas has been pleasant to work with. She does not want to be "that person." She does not want to turn a good relationship into a complaint. So she waits one more day, and then another, and the waiting becomes its own small job.

It follows her into the supermarket queue, where she does sums in her head. It follows her to the dinner table, where she is only half listening. It shows up at 6 a.m., when she wakes up already composing sentences to someone who is not even awake yet.

And quietly, underneath it all, a thought she does not like: a real business would not have to do this. A real business would have a system.

That thought is unfair. But it is loud.

The pause on the hard shoulder

The breakdown truck is late. Mira has now read her own two old reminders four times.

And something small shifts. Reading them with a cooler head, she notices that neither one says what happens next. There is no date. No clear request. No "here is the amount, here is the day." They are polite, but they are vague, and vague is easy to ignore.

The problem is not that she is too soft or too pushy. The problem is that she is inventing every message from scratch, in the worst possible mood, with no idea what the third one is supposed to sound like. Or the fourth. Nobody ever taught her the order of things.

What if the wording was already there? What if each step had a calm sentence waiting, one for the first nudge, one for the firmer follow-up, one for the "I need a date from you" message? And what if she only had to look at a list and see: this one is eleven days late, this is the next step?

Then it would not be a personal confrontation. It would just be Thursday's routine.

She does not fix anything on the hard shoulder. But the knot loosens by a notch. For the first time in days, she thinks of chasing a payment as something with a shape, not a mood.

A different Monday

Picture the same freelancer a few weeks on.

It is Monday, 9:05. Coffee is still hot. She opens one sheet and sees four invoices: two not yet due, one a few days late, one well past. Next to each, a plain note of what comes next.

For the late one she picks a message that already sounds like her, friendly, specific, with the amount and a date in it. She changes one line, reads it once, and sends it.

No rewriting nine times. No stomach drop. No scrolling the inbox for a reply that is not there.

By 9:15 she is back to the illustration she actually wanted to draw. The money question did not vanish, but it stopped being a cloud. It became a task with a start and an end.

That is the quiet difference. Not that clients suddenly pay faster, because we cannot promise that. It is that you stop carrying it around all day.

Back on the hard shoulder

The truck finally arrives at 11:05. The driver is cheerful, a bit sweaty, and says the fault is something small. Mira laughs, the first laugh of the morning.

In the passenger seat afterwards, she does one thing: she writes down a single line on her phone. Find a better way to chase.
