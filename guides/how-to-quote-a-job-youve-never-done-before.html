How to Quote a Job You've Never Done? The Wall Estimate

2026-11-04

The customer is standing on the lawn with her arms folded against the wind. It's Saturday, 9:15, and she's just pointed at the slope at the back of the garden.

"So, a proper stone wall there. With steps. What would you charge?"

You've laid paving. You've built raised beds. You've never built a retaining wall with steps. Your mouth is already smiling and nodding while your head is racing.

Let's call you Mira. You're a one-person gardener, and this is an illustrative scene, not a real customer story.

The number that comes out of your mouth

She's waiting. The tea she offered you is going cold on the garden table.

You look at the slope. You think of a job that was sort of similar. You think of what the neighbours might pay. You think of the van payment due on the first.

"Probably around eleven hundred," you hear yourself say.

She brightens. "Oh, that's lovely. When can you start?"

And there it is, the small drop in your stomach. You said it too fast. You know it. The number sounded confident, but it was built on a feeling, not on the job.

The part nobody sees

By Sunday afternoon the doubt has a shape.

How many stones is that, really? Is there a delivery charge? Does the slope need drainage behind the wall? Do you need to hire a plate compactor for two days? How long does a first-time job take compared with one you've done twenty times?

You don't know. That's the honest answer. When you've never done a job, every part of the price is a guess, and a guess feels dangerous when your own wages are inside it.

So you do the thing many people working alone do. You quietly promise yourself that you'll make it work. You'll start earlier. You'll skip the lunch break. You'll absorb the extra load of gravel. The job will somehow come out fine, because it has to.

The cost doesn't show up as a bill. It shows up as three evenings of aching shoulders and a week where you realise, scraping mortar off your boots, that you've worked for less than a part-time shop assistant earns. And you can't say anything, because you named the number yourself.

What you'd tell a friend

If your friend rang you in that garden, you'd know exactly what to say. "Don't answer on the spot. Say you'll send the price tomorrow."

It's strange how easy that is to say to someone else.

The truth is that not knowing a job isn't unprofessional. Everyone's first retaining wall, first bathroom, first big tree removal is unfamiliar. What looks unprofessional, even to you, is the panic price. What looks professional is a calm "Let me work this out properly and send it over by Monday."

Most customers don't mind waiting a day. Many trust you more for it.

The same slope, a different Saturday

Now picture the other version.

Same garden. Same folded arms. Same question.

"That's a great project," you say. "I want to give you a number I can stand behind, so I'll measure, check the materials and send you a written quote tomorrow."

You walk the slope with your tape. You note the length, the height, the steps. You take two photos. Then you drive home, not with a knot in your stomach, but with a short list.

On Sunday evening, or Monday morning over coffee, you sit down with one page. The materials go in one place. Your own hours go in another, including the slow first-time hours, because you know the first time takes longer. Hire costs, delivery and a bit of room for surprises have their own lines. And your wages are on the page as a real cost, not whatever happens to be left over.

You can see what the job costs you. You can see what you'd keep. You change the figure until the margin looks like something you'd be glad to earn.

Then you send it. The price may be higher than eleven hundred. It may not. Either way, it's yours, built from things you can point to.

If she says it's more than she hoped, you can talk about it calmly. Maybe a simpler wall, maybe fewer steps. You're negotiating the job, not defending a guess.

After the quote, a quieter month

The other half of this story is what happens when the job starts.

Receipts for stone and gravel go somewhere findable, not into the glove box. When the wall takes two days longer than planned, you notice it while it's happening, not at year-end. When the invoice goes out, you know who has paid and who hasn't.

On a normal Monday after that, you open one file. You see this month's jobs, what you quoted, what you spent, what is still owed. It takes ten minutes. You close the laptop and go back to the van with a plain, rather lovely feeling: nothing is hiding.

It doesn't make you a different person. It just means the next unfamiliar job doesn't have to feel like a bet.
