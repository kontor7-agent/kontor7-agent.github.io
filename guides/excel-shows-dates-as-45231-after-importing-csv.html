Excel Shows Dates as 45231 After CSV Import? Breakfast Room

2026-10-31

The toast has gone cold and you haven't noticed.

It's 7:45 in a hotel breakfast room in a town you'll leave by noon. Your laptop sits between the jam and a little jug of milk. The client wants the order export, cleaned and summarised, before their 9:00 meeting. You opened the CSV two minutes ago.

The column that should say order date says 45231. Then 45232. Then 45232 again. Down the whole screen, five digits, no slashes, no months.

Your first thought is not a technical one. It is: Did I break it?

The number that looks like a typo

You click the cell. You change the format. Now it says 31/10/2023 in one row and something odd in the next. You scroll. Some rows are fine. Some are still numbers. A few have turned into text that refuses to sort.

A man at the next table is reading the news on his phone and eating an egg with enormous calm. You envy him.

You know, somewhere in the back of your head, that this is a known thing. Excel stores dates as counting numbers, and sometimes a file arrives and the number shows up without its costume. You've seen it before. You fixed it before. You just can't remember how, and the fix you remember is for a different file.

So you try the thing you tried last time. It half works. Then you try the thing from a forum post you vaguely recall. It makes the day and the month trade places.

Now it is 8:05, and the problem is no longer the number. It is the clock.

What it quietly costs

This is the part nobody puts in a project quote.

It is not the twenty minutes. It is the way your stomach drops when a simple file turns out not to be simple. It is the small voice that says a professional would have this sorted by now. It is the message you start drafting to the client, running a little late with the export, and then delete because you don't want to be the person who says that.

It is also the second-guessing that follows you after you finally get it right. Are those dates real? Is the 3rd of April really the 3rd of April, or did something flip? You spot-check five rows. You spot-check five more. You send the file with a note that is a little too cheerful.

The work was never hard. The doubt is what eats the morning.

The moment it clicks

At 8:20 you stop fighting the screen and ask a different question: Why am I solving this by hand, again?

Because you have done this before. The weekly export. The other client's file with the odd date column. The one where the zip codes lost their zeros. Each time it feels like a fresh puzzle, and each time you work it out from scratch, with a cooling coffee and a rising pulse.

The puzzle was never new. Only your memory of the answer was missing.

You make a decision, quietly, between the jam and the milk. Today you finish this the slow way. Then you stop doing it the slow way.

The file goes out at 8:48. The meeting starts at 9:00. Nobody on the call knows how close it was.

A different Thursday

Picture the same breakfast room, another week.

The CSV arrives. You open it. Or better, you don't even open it. You drop it into something you set up once, on a calm afternoon, and it comes back with the dates as dates, the duplicates gone and the stray spaces trimmed. You glance at it the way you glance at a clock you trust.

Then you eat the toast while it's still warm.

The man with the egg is still there. You've become him, just for a morning: unhurried, mildly interested in the weather.

The difference is not that the data got cleaner. Exports will keep arriving messy, because that's what exports do. The difference is that the mess no longer arrives in your head at 7:45, when you can least afford it.

What a small system changes

Most of us don't need a grand data platform. We need the boring chores to stop being fresh emergencies. A few tested scripts that already know what a stubborn date column looks like. A folder where they live, so you're not hunting through old projects for the one you wrote in March.

That's all. Less rediscovery, more breakfast.
