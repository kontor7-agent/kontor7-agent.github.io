Overstocked, Cash Tied Up in Stock? The Storage Unit

2026-10-27

It is 8:15 on a grey Monday, and you are standing in front of unit 14 with the padlock in your hand.

You rent this unit for the overflow. Behind the roller door there are forty-one boxes. Some are green notebooks, some are tote bags in a colour that sold brilliantly for one weekend in spring, and some are mugs you ordered because the supplier offered a better price per piece if you took three hundred.

Your phone buzzes in your coat pocket. It is the banking app, politely showing a balance that makes your stomach tighten. Rent for the studio is due Friday. The supplier invoice for the next batch is due the Friday after.

You look at the boxes. Somewhere in there is a lot of money. You just can't spend it.

The kind of tired that isn't about work

Nobody warns you about this part of running a small shop.

You can be busy, even proud of how busy, and still feel short of cash. The money didn't vanish. It turned into cardboard, and the cardboard is sitting in a unit that costs you a monthly fee to keep.

So you do what most of us do. You tell yourself it will sell eventually. You tell yourself the discount was too good to pass up. You tell yourself you'll count it all properly next week, when things calm down.

They don't calm down. The next week has its own orders, its own emails and its own parcels to wrap.

The quiet cost isn't only the money. It's the small flinch every time you open a supplier's email with the words limited-time bulk offer. It's the feeling that your shop is running you, when you thought you were running it.

It's the odd shame of being asked, "How's business?" and answering "busy!" while thinking, I honestly don't know whether I'm doing well.

The count nobody asked for

That Monday, you do something you've avoided for months. You stand in the unit with your phone's torch on and a notebook balanced on a box, and you count.

It takes ninety minutes. Your fingers get cold, and you find a box you had forgotten you owned.

When you're done, the numbers are not a disaster, and this is the surprising part. They are simply true. Some things are nearly gone. Some things have barely moved since March. A few items sit in the middle, selling slowly and steadily, and you had been ordering them as if they were stars.

Standing there, you realise the problem was never that you buy badly. The problem is that you were deciding in the dark. You ordered from memory and from a feeling. The feeling was usually what if I run out?, and that feeling is loud. After enough embarrassing moments of telling customers "sorry, sold out," it's very loud indeed.

So you over-corrected. You bought more, and the unit filled up.

It doesn't have to be a feeling

There's a moment, somewhere around the fortieth box, when the thought arrives quietly: this could just be a list that I look at.

Not a system that takes over your life. Just one honest page that says what you have, what it cost, what's moving and what isn't. A page that tells you when something is truly running low, and also shows you when you're sitting on more than you'll sell in the next half year.

You don't need to be an accountant for that. You only need to stop carrying it all in your head.

A different Monday

Picture the same Monday a few months on.

It's 8:15 and you are at the kitchen table with coffee. The studio rent is already covered, and you didn't have to hold your breath to know it. You open one sheet and look at it for about five minutes.

The green notebooks are fine. The tote bags are marked as slow, so you won't reorder them, and you decide to bundle the last few with something popular. Next to the mugs there's a small flag that says the supplier's three-hundred-piece deal is not a deal for you, because it would tie up cash for most of a year.

You close the laptop and answer two customer emails with a calm "yes, that's in stock, it ships today" and mean it.

The unit is still there, but it is smaller now, and eventually you may not need it at all. You walk past the storage place on your way to the post office and, for once, it is just a building.

The cash sits in your account, where you can use it, rather than on a shelf, where you can only look at it.
