Freelancer Holiday Guilt? Telling Clients: The Auto-Reply

2026-11-06

It's Wednesday, 10:40, and the cursor in the out-of-office box is blinking at you.

The flights have been booked for nine days. Ten days in a rented house with a terrace and a lemon tree in the photos. You haven't told a single client.

So here's Lena, our illustrative freelance copywriter, at her kitchen table. The coffee is cold. The auto-reply field says Subject and Message, and nothing else. She types "I will be out of the office from" and stops. Deletes it. Types it again.

It's an ordinary sentence. It feels like a confession.

The sentence you keep rewriting

You know this one. The thought isn't "I deserve a break." The thought is: what if they think I'm not serious?

What if the big client has a launch coming? What if the small one, the one who pays on time and says thank you, quietly moves to someone who answers at 9 pm? What if ten days away turns out to be the proof that you're not a real business?

Nobody is asking you to justify anything. Yet the draft email grows. First a line about the trip. Then a line about how you'll be "mostly reachable". Then a line promising you'll check messages "just in case". By the fourth version you've apologised twice and nobody has said a word.

What the guilt quietly costs

The cost is rarely the holiday itself. It's what happens around it.

You tell people late, so it lands as a surprise. You say "mostly reachable" and then spend the first three days on the terrace with your phone face-up beside the sunscreen. Every buzz pulls your shoulders up.

You also work double the week before, to be "free". You come back to a swollen inbox and a little dread, and that dread eats the rest of the glow.

And under all of it is the older worry: that you're the only one holding the whole thing together, so you're not allowed to put it down.

Lena knows this. Last year she answered a "quick question" from a hammock. It took four minutes. It also took the afternoon.

The call that changed the tone

That Wednesday, at 11:15, she has a regular call with Mr. Okafor, who runs a small outdoor-gear shop. She's rehearsed an opening in her head. She's going to mention the trip near the end, quickly, like an afterthought.

Then he mentions his week. "We're shut the first week of August," he says. "Same as every year. Family. You should see the sign I put on the door."

He laughs. No guilt. No explanation. Just a sign on a door.

Lena notices something. He never once said "I hope that's okay." He gave dates and a plan, and that was the end of it. The customers live with it. They even like the sign.

She says, "Funnily enough, I'm away the second week of August."

"Oh, nice," he says. "Where to?"

That's the whole conversation. It takes eleven seconds.

The version you can actually send

After the call she sits down again. This time the message comes out in three short lines. The dates. Who to contact for anything urgent, or a plain note that nothing will be answered until she's back. When she'll reply.

No apology. No "I'm so sorry for any inconvenience." The tone is the same one she uses when she says her day rate: warm, level, a professional telling people her schedule.

The odd thing is how much easier it is once the words are right. The guilt wasn't about the holiday. It was about not knowing how to say it. A clear, kind sentence turns out to be most of the cure.

She schedules one email to each client, a few weeks ahead, not the night before. She doesn't hover over "send".

A normal Monday, afterwards

It's the Monday before she leaves. The inbox is quiet in a good way. Two clients wrote back with a "have a lovely time", one with a question that she answers by lunch, and nobody sounded worried.

The auto-reply is already switched on. It reads like her, not like a hostage note.

At 5:30 she closes the laptop. Not because she's run out of time, but because everything that needed saying has been said. The suitcase is half packed on the bed. The kettle is on.

When she's lying on that terrace, her phone is in a drawer indoors. The lemon tree is, as promised, ridiculous. And nobody is waiting on her.
