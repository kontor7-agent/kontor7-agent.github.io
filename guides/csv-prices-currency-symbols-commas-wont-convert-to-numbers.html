CSV Prices With Symbols Won't Convert? The Hardware Till

2026-10-31

The queue at the hardware shop is four people long, and Jonas is holding one shelf bracket.

It is Monday, 9:20. His phone buzzes against the bracket. A client. The one with the supplier price list he promised to summarise by ten.

"Quick one," she says. "What's the average price per category? Just a rough number."

"Sure," he says. "Give me half an hour."

He does not say that the file on his laptop, back in the car, currently adds up to zero.

The sum that says zero

Friday afternoon he had opened the export and felt fine about it. Two thousand rows, clean columns, nothing exotic. Then he asked for a total and got 0.

He looked closer. The prices were not numbers. They were pictures of numbers. "1.299,00 €" in one block. "$1,299.00" in another, from the second supplier. A few with a stray space in front. A few that just said "on request."

To the eye, every one of them was obviously a price. To the machine, they were words.

So he did what everyone does. He replaced the euro sign. Then the dollar sign. Then the commas. Then he noticed that replacing commas turned 1.299,00 into something about a thousand times wrong. Or a hundred times. He could not tell which, and that was the real problem.

He closed the laptop on Friday with a small knot under his ribs. It was still there on Sunday evening, quietly, like a tab left open.

What it costs to not trust a number

The awkward part is not the work. It is the doubt.

A wrong price average does not look wrong. It looks like a number. It goes into a slide, then a decision, then a conversation where someone says, "That seems low, doesn't it?" and you hear your own voice say, "Let me double-check."

When you work alone, nobody reviews your data. You are the review. So every messy file turns into a small test of whether you are a person who can be trusted with numbers. Jonas has never failed that test in public. He has also never stopped feeling it.

At the till, the man in front pays for a bag of screws with exact change, counting coins one by one. Jonas watches the coins and thinks about decimal commas.

The part where it stops being a fight

He gets to the front. The cashier asks if he wants a receipt. He says yes, absent-mindedly, and then looks at it properly for the first time: a clear column of amounts, each with its sign, each adding up to the total at the bottom.

Nobody at that till is confused about what 4,50 € means. The confusion only starts when it lands in a file that has to be read by something that has no idea what a euro sign is.

And then a thought arrives, plain and almost boring: this is not a problem you solve once per file. It is the same problem every time a supplier sends a list. Different symbols, different separators, same Monday-morning knot.

What if the cleaning was not something he did by hand, with his stomach tight? What if it simply happened first, in the same careful way each time, and showed him what it had changed before it touched anything for real?

He stands in the car park with the bracket in a paper bag and feels, for the first time since Friday, a little room in his chest.

A different Monday

Picture the other version of this morning.

The file arrives. He runs one thing. A short report comes back: these 1,874 prices were read as euros, these 112 as dollars, these 14 could not be read and are listed by row so he can look at them himself. Nothing was changed on the original. Nothing was guessed silently.

He reads the report with a coffee. The "on request" rows are exactly the ones he would have wanted to see. He sends the client her average, with a one-line note about the fourteen items that had no price.

No doubt hanging around afterwards. No quiet "let me double-check" in a call three weeks later.

The bracket still needs to go up on the wall. That is the only job left on his list.

If you recognise the zero

Maybe your version is a different file. A shop export with "€12,50" in it. An invoice list from a client system. A spreadsheet from a supplier who writes amounts the way they like to.

The feeling is the same: the data is right there, readable, and still somehow unusable. It is not you. Text that looks like a number is one of the oldest traps in messy exports.
