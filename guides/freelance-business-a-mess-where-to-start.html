Freelance Business a Mess? The Map at the Info Desk

2026-11-01

The tourist information desk smells of laminated paper and sun cream. It is 11:20 on a Tuesday, and Jonas is standing in a queue behind a family arguing about ferry times.

He is supposed to be on holiday. Four days by the sea, booked back in spring. His laptop is in the bag on his shoulder, because "just in case" has become a habit. His phone shows three unread client emails, a calendar reminder he can't decipher, and a note to himself from last week: sort out money stuff.

That is the whole note. Sort out money stuff.

He has read it perhaps twenty times. It has never become anything smaller.

The map on the counter

When it is his turn, the woman behind the desk smiles and slides a folded map across the counter. She draws a circle in pen on the harbour and says, "You are here."

That is all. No lecture about the whole coastline. No list of forty things to see. A circle, a small arrow and a path to the next street.

Jonas takes the map and stands outside in the wind, and something in his chest goes strangely quiet.

Because that is exactly what he doesn't have. Not a plan. Not a perfect system. Just a circle that says you are here.

The mess nobody sees

From the outside, Jonas runs a decent business. He edits videos for small companies. Clients say nice things. Work keeps arriving.

On the inside, it looks like this:

Invoices are in one folder, except the two from spring, which are somewhere in his email. Hours are in his head, plus a notebook, plus a few lines in a spreadsheet he stopped updating in June. He quoted a new client last month using a number that felt right in the moment. He has not looked at it since.

Somewhere in there is a client who may or may not have paid. He thinks so. He hopes so. He cannot say it with certainty, and that small gap is what he carries around all day.

He doesn't talk about it. How would that sound? I run a business and I don't know who owes me money. It feels like admitting he is not a real professional. So he smiles on calls and tells people things are "busy but good."

And every time he thinks about fixing it, his mind jumps to the whole mountain at once. New software. A proper bookkeeping setup. A rate review. A system for everything. The mountain is so tall that he walks around it, again and again, and goes back to editing.

What the circle taught him

He sits on a bench by the water and unfolds the map again.

The woman at the desk never tried to show him the entire town. She started where he stood.

He thinks: what if the mess isn't one giant thing? What if it is a handful of small questions that have simply been piled on top of each other?

Who owes me money right now?

What should I charge, and why?

Where did my time actually go last month?

Three questions. Not forty. He realises he has never asked them one at a time, in a quiet moment, with a cup of coffee. He has only ever asked them all together, at the worst hour, in a panic that made every answer feel worse.

The sea does not care about his invoices. A gull is trying to steal a chip from a toddler. Jonas laughs for the first time in days, a short, surprised laugh, and then the thing he didn't expect arrives: he feels lighter. Nothing has been fixed. He just knows where the first circle goes.

A different Monday

He lets himself picture it.

A Monday, a few weeks from now. Coffee on the desk. He opens one page and sees, in plain view, who has paid and who hasn't. The overdue one is no longer a vague dread. It is a name, a date, a calm sentence he can send before nine and then forget.

A new client asks for a quote on the phone. He doesn't flinch or stall or say "let me get back to you" because he is afraid of his own number. He knows what an hour of his work costs, and what a week off costs, and he says the figure in an even voice.

At six, he closes the laptop. Not because everything is perfect, but because he knows what is done and what is waiting. The knot he has carried for months is not tight anymore.

That is all he wants. Not a machine. Not a new identity. Just to see where he stands.

One small next step

Jonas puts the map in his pocket, deletes the note that said sort out money stuff, and replaces it with three shorter lines. Then he goes to find lunch.

If you recognise yourself in him, the person smiling on calls while the mess waits underneath, you don't have to climb the whole mountain. A calm starting point can be enough.
