Applied to the Same Company Twice? The Pretzel Stand Call

2026-10-29

The phone buzzes at 11:20 on a Wednesday, just as the woman at the pretzel stand hands you a paper bag. The screen shows a number you don't know.

You are standing in the middle of the station concourse. Pigeons, announcements, a suitcase wheel clattering past your ankle. You answer anyway, because unknown numbers have started to feel like the whole point of your week.

"Hi, this is Katrin from Hollenbeck Logistics. I'm calling about your application for the operations coordinator role."

Your stomach does a small happy lift. Then she adds, in a friendly voice: "I actually have two applications from you. One from the third and one from last week. Just wanted to check, is there a difference between them?"

The silence you try to cover

There is a difference. You just can't say what it is.

One version has the cover letter that opens with the warehouse project. The other one, you think, has the shorter CV. Or the other way round. You were tired both times. You remember the company's name being familiar the second time and thinking, surely not.

"Ah," you say. "Yes. I, um. I updated some things."

Katrin laughs kindly. "No problem at all, it happens. Which one should I look at?"

You hear yourself say "the newer one," and you don't know whether that is the right answer. The pretzel is going cold in your hand. A man behind you sighs at your spot in the queue, and you step aside without realising you have left it.

The call lasts four minutes. She is warm, and she moves you forward to a first conversation on Friday. You say thank you three times. Then you stand by the pillar with the departure board flickering above you, and the good news and the embarrassment sit side by side in your chest.

What it quietly costs

The embarrassment is not really about Katrin. She has seen it a hundred times.

It is about what the call showed you. Somewhere in the last few weeks, the search stopped being something you steer and became something you endure. Applications went out from the sofa, from the bus, from a phone at 7:15 in the morning. Some saved a confirmation email. Some didn't. A few live in a notes file called "jobs," and one lives in a spreadsheet you started in a burst of optimism and abandoned on a Tuesday.

So every call is a small gamble. Is this the company with the 4-day week, or the one with the odd salary range? Did you already send them a follow-up? What did you say you were good at?

You don't say any of this out loud. People who are looking for work get very good at sounding organised. But you notice that you have started avoiding your own inbox. And you notice that the double application is only the visible one. There may be others you will never find out about.

The bench by platform 9

You sit down on a bench with the cold pretzel and open your email. You search for "Hollenbeck." Two confirmations, nineteen days apart. Different subject lines, same job. You look at them for a while.

The thought that arrives is not dramatic. It is plain and a little tired: this doesn't have to be so hard to hold in my head.

Nobody is asking you to be a machine. You only want one place where a company appears once. A place that tells you what you sent, when, and what happens next. Somewhere to write down, before Friday, what you want to say about the warehouse project, so that you are not improvising it with a pigeon at your feet.

It is a small thought. But it is the first one in weeks that feels like a hand on the wheel.

A different Wednesday

Picture the same station, the same bag, the same unknown number.

This time you glance at the name before you answer, because you already know it. You have seen it in a list that morning. You know you applied on the third, that nothing has come back yet, and that your follow-up was marked for this week. You know which CV went out. You even remember the one sentence you were proud of in the letter.

Katrin says hello. You say hello back, and your voice is level.

There is no scramble in it. The call is just a call, and it ends with a time on Friday and a line in your notes about what to bring up. You buy a second pretzel because it's warm, and you eat it standing in the sun.

The search is still a search. It still has waiting in it, and some silences you can't fix. But it has stopped being something that ambushes you between a bag of pastry and a train.
