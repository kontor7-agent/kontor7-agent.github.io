Can't Say No to a Client's Extra Work? The Lifting Bridge

2026-10-22

The barrier comes down at 8:52, just as Jonas reaches the canal.

A ship is coming. The bridge will lift, and everyone on the quayside will wait. Cyclists, a man with a pushchair, a woman holding a coffee and a paper bag. Four minutes, maybe five.

His phone buzzes in his jacket pocket.

He knows who it is before he looks. It is the client he finished the brand identity for two weeks ago. The invoice is out. The files are delivered. The project is, in every way that counts, done.

Hi Jonas! Quick one. Could you also do the social media templates? Should be a small thing, since you already know the look. Need them Friday. Thanks so much!

The bridge begins to rise. Jonas stares at the word small.

The sentence that was never going to be small

He knows what "small" means here. Twelve templates in three sizes, a round of changes, a call to explain the changes. Two days of work, maybe three. None of it was in the quote.

And yet his thumb is already hovering over a reply. Sure, no problem!

That is the strange part. He does not want to say yes. He wants to say no, and he has wanted to say it for about ten seconds. But the no has nowhere to go. Every version he builds in his head sounds either cold ("That's outside scope") or apologetic to the point of collapse ("I'm so sorry, I would love to, but...").

So he does what a lot of people who work alone do. He stands there, holding the phone, with a tight feeling just under his ribs.

Because the client is nice. That is what makes it hard. If they were rude, he could say no easily. But they are friendly and warm, and they might send him two more projects in spring. Saying no feels like pushing against something kind.

What the yes quietly costs

The ship slides past, enormous and slow. Jonas watches the water instead of his screen.

He has said yes like this before. Not to anything dramatic. A logo tweak here, an extra banner there, a "quick look" at a presentation that turned into an afternoon. Each one felt like goodwill. Together they became a pattern: a Thursday he never got back, a weekend he spent half-working, a faint resentment he did not like finding in himself.

The resentment is the worst part. It isn't aimed at the client, really. It is aimed at himself, for being the kind of person who cannot hold a line without writing six drafts and feeling sick.

And under that is a smaller thought he rarely says out loud: a real business would just know what to write.

The bridge shudders and begins to come down.

The pause that was allowed

The woman with the coffee is on her phone too, thumbing through something with a frown. She glances up, catches his eye, and gives a small tired smile, the smile of someone else who is also pretending to be fine on the way to work.

And Jonas thinks, for the first time that morning, something different.

What if he does not answer right now?

Not as avoidance. Just as a choice. The message does not need a reply from a quayside at 8:57. It needs a reply that is friendly, clear and short, one that thanks them, says what the original project covered, offers the new work as its own small project with its own price, and leaves the door open. A reply with no apology in it and no edge either.

He has never had trouble with the idea of that email. His trouble is that the words will not arrive when he is flustered. They show up three hours later, after he has already said yes.

So he puts the phone away. The barrier lifts. He cycles on.

A different kind of Monday

Picture the same morning, a little changed.

The message still arrives. The word small still makes his stomach tighten for a moment. But he already has something close at hand: a calm, kind way of saying "that's a new piece of work, here is how we can do it," ready to adjust and send. He changes a name, a detail, the tone to match this particular client, and presses send before the kettle has boiled.

No agonising. No Friday swallowed. No simmering feeling at the weekend.

The client replies with a thumbs-up and Of course, send me the quote! Because most of the time, people do not take offence at a clear, warm boundary. They only take offence at silence, or at a yes that curdles into late work and a short temper.

What changes is not Jonas's kindness. It is that his kindness no longer has to cost him an evening.
