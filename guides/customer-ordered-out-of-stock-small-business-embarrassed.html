Ordered Item Out of Stock? The Christening Buffet

2026-10-19

The tray of egg sandwiches is balanced on one arm when your phone buzzes against your hip.

You're in the church hall kitchen. It's your cousin's baby's christening, there are paper napkins with little crosses on them, and somebody's uncle is asking where the teaspoons live. You glance down at the screen.

New order: Walnut Stacking Set, 2 x.

Your stomach drops before your brain catches up. You know that set. You sold the last one on Tuesday. Or was it Wednesday? You're almost sure you took it off the shop page. Almost.

You put the sandwiches down. You say "just a second" to nobody in particular, and you walk out to the car park, where the wind is flapping the bunting.

The knot that arrives before the facts

You open the shop dashboard with your thumb. It still shows three in stock.

Three. You've got none. The shelf in your spare room has an empty rectangle in the dust where the box used to sit.

The feeling isn't really about wooden toys. It's the heat in your face, the sense that you're a bit of a fraud. A real shop would have known. A real shop wouldn't be standing between a bin and a hired minibus, rehearsing an apology.

The customer is called Annika. Her message says it's a gift for her sister's twins, and she's so happy she found you. There's a little heart at the end.

That heart is the worst part.

The apology you draft three times

You type: So sorry, there's been a mix-up.

You delete it. It sounds like an excuse.

You type: Unfortunately the item is no longer available. Too cold. She'll think you don't care.

You type: I'm so embarrassed, I think I've sold it already, I'm really sorry, I can make another but it might take... and then you stop, because you don't know how long it might take. You don't know when the walnut arrives. You don't know whether you have any left to turn.

Inside, someone starts a toast. You hear glasses and a ripple of laughter, and you're standing outside with your thumb hovering over a send button, doing sums in your head that never come out the same twice.

This is the part nobody sees. It isn't a disaster. Nobody is hurt, and a refund is two clicks. But it's the third time this autumn, and each one chips a little at the picture you carry of yourself: someone who runs a proper small business.

What it quietly costs

It's not just the refund. It's the Sunday you spent half-watching the inbox. It's the way you've started checking the shop page before you've even had breakfast, just in case. It's the order you almost didn't want to open because you weren't sure you could keep the promise.

It's also the reorders you made too late, and the ones you made too early, when a box of oak offcuts sat for months, money tied up in a corner.

You know your stock. Or you think you do. You know it the way you know a route you've driven for years: mostly by feel, right up until the day there's a diversion.

Telling the truth, kindly

You breathe. You read Annika's message once more, slowly, and notice something. She isn't asking for magic. She's asking for a gift for two small children, and she'd like it to arrive before the end of the month.

So you write plainly. You tell her the set sold out and your shop page hadn't caught up, which is your mistake and not hers. You say when you can realistically make more, and you offer the refund without being asked.

She replies within ten minutes. Oh, no worries! I'll wait if it's by the 28th.

That's all. No drama. The knot, it turns out, was mostly yours.

You go back in. The teaspoons have been found. Someone presses a cup of tea into your hand and asks if you're all right, and you say yes, and for once it's nearly true.

A different kind of Monday

Here's the thing you think about on the drive home. The problem wasn't Annika, or the twins, or even the walnut. It was that you had to find out in a church hall kitchen.

Imagine the same week, differently. You sit down on Monday with your coffee and one page that simply tells you: this is running low, this one needs ordering by Friday, this one is fine for two months. Nothing dramatic. You glance at it the way you glance at a weather app, and then you get on with your day.

No scanning the shelf in your head. No flinch when the phone buzzes. When an order lands, you already know the answer, because the answer is written down somewhere you trust.

The next time you're holding a tray of sandwiches, it's just sandwiches.
