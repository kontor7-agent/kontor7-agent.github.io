Sell Handmade Crafts Online but No Profit? Car Park Lunch

2026-10-25

It's 12:40 on a Wednesday. You're in the staff car park, in your own car, with a sandwich you haven't unwrapped.

This is your lunch break. It's also your shop hour.

The phone shows three new orders. Two plant hangers and a set of coasters. A year ago, three orders would have made you grin at the steering wheel. Today you feel a small, odd flatness instead.

Because the thought is already there, quiet and persistent: so why is there never any money?

The question you don't say out loud

Imagine Mira. She has a day job and a spare room that smells of resin and cotton cord. She sells handmade things online and at the odd weekend market. People write nice reviews. Her sister has told three colleagues about the shop.

By any visible measure it's going well.

But when she opens her banking app, the number doesn't match the feeling of being busy. Money comes in and money goes out. A payout lands on Friday, then the supplier order, the packaging, the postage label and the new moulds she "needed". Somewhere in there, the profit is supposed to be.

She can't point to it.

So she does what many makers do. She tells herself it's the early years. She tells herself everyone starts like this. She tells herself that if she just sells more, it will sort itself out.

Selling more feels like the only lever she's allowed to pull.

What it quietly costs

Nobody warns you that not knowing is tiring in its own way.

It's the Sunday evening when you pack parcels until ten and wonder if the hours were worth anything. It's the moment a friend says "you should charge more" and you nod, because you don't actually know what "more" would be, or whether your current price is already fine.

It's feeling a little silly calling it a business at all. Hobby money. Pocket money. The thing you do "on the side", even when it eats your weekends.

And it's the small flinch when someone asks for a discount. You can't say yes with a clear conscience, and you can't say no with a clear head. You don't know where the floor is.

That's the real cost. Not just the money. It's the missing ground under your own prices.

The sandwich, finally unwrapped

Back in the car park, Mira finally eats.

She scrolls to the coaster order and tries to do the sum in her head. The sale price is clear. The material cost, roughly, is clear. Then it gets foggy. The platform's cut. The payment charge. The box. The tissue paper she always adds because it looks nice. The sticker. Her time.

She gets as far as "probably something" and stops.

Then she notices something. She isn't actually afraid of the answer. She's tired of guessing at it.

That's the turning point, and it's a small one. No dramatic realisation, no spreadsheet-fuelled epiphany. Just the thought that she could simply see it. Product by product. Cost, fees, what's left. Written down once, in one calm place, instead of hovering in her head every time an order pings.

It doesn't have to be a feeling. It could be a number.

A different Monday

Picture the same week, a little later.

Monday morning. Coffee. The phone buzzes with an order and, for once, you don't tense up. You already know what that item earns. Not roughly. Actually.

You know which product carries the shop and which one only looks busy. You know the one you've been selling at a price that feels generous to everyone but you. You decide, calmly, what to do about it. Maybe a small raise. Maybe a smaller version. Maybe you quietly stop making it.

At the weekend market, someone asks if you can do the set for less. You smile and answer without that familiar lurch. You know your floor. You can be kind and still say no.

The market-day cash box and the online orders no longer live in two different notebooks. They sit together, and the picture of the month fits on one screen.

And your lunch hour goes back to being lunch.

Nothing about your craft changed. The cord is the same cord, the resin the same resin. What changed is that you stopped working inside a fog.
