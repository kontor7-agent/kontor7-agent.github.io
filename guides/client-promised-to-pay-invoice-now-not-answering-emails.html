Client Promised to Pay, Now Won't Answer? Kilometre 30

2026-11-02

You are standing at kilometre 30 of a city marathon, holding a cardboard sign that says GO, MIRA, GO. It's 10:40 on a Sunday. The sun is out, a brass band is murdering a pop song, and your phone is in your left hand because you opened your email "just for a second" four minutes ago.

There is nothing new from Jan.

Jan is the client who wrote, three weeks ago: "Invoice received, payment goes out this Friday, thanks again for the quick turnaround!" There was an exclamation mark. You remember the small warm feeling it gave you.

That Friday came and went. So did the next one.

You sent a polite follow-up. Then a politer one. Then one that started with "Just floating this back to the top of your inbox", which you hated while typing it. No reply. Not even the little "Sorry, busy week!" that would at least be something.

The runners keep coming. Someone's shoe is untied. A child hands out orange slices. And you are watching a blank screen.

The quiet is the worst part

It isn't really about the money, although the money matters. It's the rent on the first. It's the laptop repair you put off. It's the number in your head that you quietly adjusted last week: if it comes Friday, then fine.

But underneath that is something harder to say out loud. You start to wonder whether you did something wrong. Whether the work wasn't good enough. Whether you were too pushy, or not pushy enough. Whether asking again makes you look desperate.

A promise followed by silence is a strange thing. A "no" you could deal with. A promise gives you hope, and then it takes the hope away slowly, one unanswered email at a time.

So you do what most people who work alone do. You wait. You tell yourself you'll write again "tomorrow, when the wording feels right." You open the sent folder and reread your last message, looking for what you did wrong.

Kilometre 30

A woman next to you is also holding a sign. She glances at your phone, then at you.

"Bad news?"

"No news," you say. "A client who said they'd pay. And now they don't answer."

She nods like she's heard it before. "My brother's a carpenter. He says the worst part is not the waiting, it's the guessing."

Then she turns, because her runner has appeared, and she's shouting a name, waving her sign above her head.

You stay with that word, guessing.

That's exactly what you've been doing for three weeks. Guessing whether Jan is on holiday or avoiding you. Guessing whether today is too soon to write again. Guessing whether the tone should be friendly or firm. Every email you've sent was a fresh improvisation, written alone, in a slightly anxious mood, with no sense of what comes next.

Nobody ever taught you what a next step looks like. You've only been inventing one, badly, each time.

Mira comes past

Then she's there. Mira, your friend, red-faced, grinning, sweat on her forehead, a bit wobbly. You scream her name. She points at you. You hold the sign up so high your arm shakes.

She runs on. Twelve more kilometres to go.

Watching her disappear into the crowd, you notice something. Mira didn't run this by feeling brave on the day. She had a plan on paper: pace for the first stretch, water here, a gel at 25, push at 35. When it gets hard, she doesn't have to decide anything. She just follows the next line.

You put your phone in your pocket. For the first time in three weeks, the thought isn't what do I say? It's quieter: maybe this doesn't have to be improvised.

A different Monday

Picture a Monday where an overdue invoice is just a line with a status on it. Not a knot in your stomach.

You open one file with your morning coffee. It tells you: this one is nine days late, the first reminder went out, the next calm message is due Thursday. You don't reread your sent folder looking for blame. The words are already written, friendly at first, clearer later, and none of them sound like pleading. You change the client's name, you send it, you close the laptop.

Silence from a client stops being a verdict on you. It becomes a step on a timeline, and the timeline knows what step comes after it.

You still care about the money. You'd still like Jan to answer. But your Sunday is no longer spent holding your phone like a hot cup of tea while a brass band plays.

Chasing payment starts to feel like what it always should have been: ordinary business. Not begging. Not a favour you're asking for. Just the next calm thing.
