Applied to 100 Jobs, No Reply? The Pub Quiz Tiebreaker

2026-10-28

It is Thursday, 20:15, and the quiz host is holding a pen over the tiebreaker card.

"Closest guess wins the bar tab," he says. "How many steps are there in the tower of the old cathedral?"

Your team leans in. Priya writes 412. Tomasz writes 380. They look at you.

You are thinking about something else entirely. You are thinking about a number you don't have.

The question that wasn't on the card

Earlier, while the first round was being marked, Priya had asked the friendly kind of question. "So, how's the job hunt? Any luck?"

"Loads of applications," you said. "Maybe a hundred. Nothing much back."

She nodded the way people nod when they don't know what else to do. The conversation moved on to whose turn it was to buy crisps.

But the word maybe stayed with you.

A hundred. Maybe. Or eighty. Or a hundred and twenty. You honestly don't know. Somewhere in your laptop there are three half-finished lists, a folder called "CVs final FINAL", and a search in your email for "thank you for applying" that returns more results than you want to scroll through.

You write 400 on the tiebreaker card. Nobody notices you didn't say a word.

What a hundred unanswered applications costs

It isn't only the silence from employers. That part is hard enough.

It's the small tax you pay every day because nothing is written down in one place.

It's opening a job ad and getting a flicker of have I already sent something here? It's the dread of the unknown number on your phone, because you can't tell which company it might be. It's the recruiter who says "great to speak again" while you nod and hope the role will come back to you.

It's the follow-up you meant to send last Tuesday, and then the Tuesday after, until it felt too late to send at all.

And the quiet verdict you start to hand yourself: I'm doing this badly. I'm not organised enough for this.

But the problem was never that you aren't serious. A hundred applications is a lot of work. You have simply been carrying all of it in your head, and your head is tired.

The walk home

The quiz ends. Your team comes fourth. Tomasz wins the tab with 391 steps, and everyone laughs about it.

You walk home the long way, past the shuttered bakery and the bus stop with the cracked shelter. The thought that comes isn't dramatic. It's small and practical.

What if I just knew?

Not knew whether anyone will hire you. Nobody can know that. But knew the plain facts. How many applications are out there, which companies have replied, which ones haven't, and which one you were supposed to chase on Friday.

You realise that most of what's been wearing you down isn't the rejection. It's the fog around it. Rejection, at least, is a clear answer. Fog is just weather you can't plan for.

A different Monday

Picture a Monday a few weeks from now.

You sit down with a cup of tea at 9:00. You open one file. In a few minutes you can see everything: what's out, what's waiting, what's gone quiet, and which two follow-ups are due this week. No digging through your inbox. No guessing.

A call comes in from a number you don't recognise. You pick up without that jolt in your chest, because a quick glance tells you which company it probably is and what you sent them.

If an interview invite arrives, you aren't scrambling at midnight to remember what you said. Your stories are ready. You've already thought about the moment you led a project through a mess, and the time you fixed a mistake before anyone noticed. They're short, honest, and yours.

By 9:30 you're done with the admin of job hunting, and the rest of the day belongs to you. A walk. A proper lunch. Maybe a course you've been curious about.

The search is still uncertain. But it no longer feels like you're juggling in the dark.

The number you can finally give

Next time someone asks, "How's the job hunt going?", you won't say "maybe a hundred."

You'll say, "I've sent 104. Eleven replied, three turned into conversations, and I have one interview on Thursday."

That is a very different thing to be able to say. It isn't bragging, and it isn't complaining. It's just you, standing on solid ground, treating your job search like the real project it is.
