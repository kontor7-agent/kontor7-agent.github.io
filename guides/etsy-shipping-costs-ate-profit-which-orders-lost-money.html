Etsy Shipping Ate Your Profit? The Post Office Scale

2026-11-04

The queue at the post office moves like cold honey. It is Saturday, 10:20, and you are holding four parcels against your chest like a stack of warm laundry.

Each one is a small framed linocut print, wrapped in card and tissue, with a handwritten thank-you tucked in. You made the prints on Wednesday. You wrapped them last night in front of a film you barely watched.

Now it is your turn. The clerk puts the first parcel on the scale, taps the screen and says the price.

You do the sum in your head before you can stop yourself. The customer paid for shipping at checkout. It was less than this. Quite a bit less.

"And the others?" the clerk asks, already reaching for the second one.

You say yes, because there are people behind you and the parcels are already on the counter. Your face stays friendly. Inside, something has gone quiet and tight.

The sum you never finished

Walking home, you try to be reasonable about it. Shipping is just a cost of doing business. Some orders go to the next town, some go across the country. It probably evens out.

Probably.

That word has been doing a lot of work for you lately. The shop is busy. Orders come in, the little notification sound goes off, and each one feels like a small yes. Your payout lands every week and the number looks healthy.

But when you look for the part that stays with you, you cannot find it. Your bank balance doesn't climb the way the sales suggest it should. You had assumed you were bad with money. You are starting to suspect the answer is simpler and more annoying than that.

Somewhere in those orders, some of them cost you money. You just don't know which ones.

The heavy framed prints? The ones that went abroad? The free-shipping weekend you ran in October, when you felt generous and the orders came in fast? You can't say. The postage receipts are in a jacket pocket, a drawer, and one crumpled in the bottom of your tote bag. The order details are on a screen. The material costs are in your head, and your head is tired.

What it quietly costs

The money is only part of it. The bigger cost is how it feels to not know.

You hesitate before you reply to a customer who asks for a discount on two prints. You don't know whether you can afford to say yes. You feel faintly embarrassed when a friend says, "Your shop is doing so well!" because you smile and nod and have no idea whether it is true.

You keep working harder. More listings, more packing, more weekend mornings at the post office queue. It feels responsible. It might also just mean losing money faster, with better packaging.

That is the part nobody says out loud. Being busy and being profitable look identical from the inside, until they don't.

The small quiet moment

That afternoon you sit at the kitchen table with a cup of tea going cold. You don't open anything dramatic. You are not planning a reinvention of your business.

You just ask yourself one plain question: what if I could see each order next to what it really cost me?

Not an accountant's report. Just the print, the frame, the card, the postage, the time it took to wrap, and the sale price, all in one honest row. Enough to see which orders left something behind and which left a small hole.

The thought is almost disappointingly simple. And that is the relief of it. The problem was never that you are bad at this. It was that the pieces lived in five different places, and nobody can add up a jacket pocket.

It doesn't have to be like this. A calm, plain place that holds your stock, your real costs and your orders together can answer that Saturday question in a glance, without an evening of detective work.

A different Saturday

Picture the same queue a few weeks on. Same cold-honey pace, same smell of cardboard and floor polish.

You still have four parcels. The clerk still says the price. But this time you already know roughly what it will be, because the shipping cost is part of what you looked at on Thursday.

You nod. No tightness. You even make small talk about the weather.

The heavy framed prints have a slightly higher shipping line now, and you didn't agonise over it. You saw the numbers and made a calm choice. The orders heading overseas are priced as the overseas orders they are. When someone asks for a discount, you can answer in a friendly voice, because you know what you can afford to give.

Monday morning is quieter too. You open one screen. It shows what is low on stock, what needs to ship by when, and which products are really earning their place on your shelf. It takes about as long as the kettle does.

You are still a maker with a small shop and a lot of tissue paper. But you are a maker who knows.
