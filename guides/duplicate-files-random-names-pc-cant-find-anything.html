Duplicate Files Everywhere on Your PC? The Car Park, 8:50

2026-10-23

It is 8:50 on a Tuesday, and Marta is sitting in her parked car outside a client's office. The meeting is at nine. She has ten minutes, a laptop on the passenger seat and a lukewarm coffee in the cup holder.

She is a freelance designer. The client has just texted: Could you bring the signed-off brochure layout? Printer wants to see it.

Simple. She knows she has it. She opens the laptop and types "brochure" into the search box.

Seventeen results.

Seventeen ways to be wrong

brochure_final.pdf. brochure_final (2).pdf. brochure_final_NEW.pdf. brochure-v3-signed.pdf. Then, further down, Scan_0023.pdf, download (14).pdf and 1b7e9c04-attachment.pdf.

She opens the first one. It looks right. Then she opens the second one. It also looks right, except the logo is a few millimetres lower. Or is it? She zooms in and her stomach tightens.

She does not know which one is the signed-off version. Nobody ever told her that one day the name of a file would matter this much. For two years the name just didn't seem to matter.

The clock in the corner says 8:56.

The quiet cost nobody mentions

This is not really about one brochure. Marta knows that, sitting there with the engine off.

It is the Downloads folder that stopped scrolling properly around spring. It is the desktop where screenshots pile up like leaves. It is the three folders named "Clients", "Clients old" and "Clients 2 DO NOT USE". It is the moment last month when she spent forty minutes looking for a photo she had already paid for, and then paid for it again, because buying it twice was faster.

Nobody sees this cost. It does not appear on an invoice. It shows up as a low hum of doubt: Am I sending the right thing? Is this the latest one? Will they notice I'm not sure?

And underneath, the small, unkind thought: A real business would have this under control.

She picks the one with the most recent date and hopes. At 8:58 she walks into reception with a file she is about 80 percent sure about. The meeting goes fine. It always goes fine. But she keeps one hand on her laptop the whole time, like someone carrying a tray of full glasses.

The turning point is small

The turning point does not arrive with a bang. It arrives that same evening, while she is cooking pasta and half-listening to a podcast.

A developer on the show says something in passing. He says he stopped trusting his own memory about where files were, and so he stopped asking his memory. He let a plain, boring routine sort things while he slept, and he made it show him what it would do before it did anything.

Marta stops stirring.

She has never wanted a clever system. She has wanted a safe one. The thought of software moving her files around on its own has always made her nervous, because what if it moves the wrong thing? What if she cannot undo it?

But show me first is different. Keep a record of every move is different. That is not magic. That is a tidy assistant who writes everything down and waits for a nod.

She leaves the pasta to go slightly soft and sits down with a notepad. Not to fix anything yet. Just to write one sentence:

I want to open my computer and trust what I see.

What a normal Tuesday could feel like

Imagine the same car park, a few weeks on.

It is 8:50. The client texts. Marta opens a folder called something plain, with a date at the front, and the signed-off brochure is sitting there with a name that says what it is. One copy. Not seventeen.

She attaches it and presses send. The whole thing takes less time than the coffee takes to cool.

There is a quiet satisfaction in it, the kind you only notice because it used to be missing. The Downloads folder holds only this week. Last month's files have been tucked into an archive with a tidy label, still there if she ever needs them. The duplicates are gone, and the list of what was removed sits in a small log, in case she ever wants to look back. Screenshots are no longer a drift on the desktop.

She does not think about any of this on Tuesday. That is the point. The relief is not a feeling of excitement. It is the absence of a hum.

She walks into the meeting with both hands free.

You do not have to do it all in one weekend

If you recognise Marta, you probably also recognise the guilt that comes with it. The plan to "spend a Saturday sorting everything out". The Saturday that somehow turned into something else. The fresh folder structure that lasted nine days.

Here is the gentler idea: you do not need a heroic clean-up. You need a small routine that does the dull part for you, shows you what it intends to do first, and writes down what it did so nothing is ever truly lost.

That is it. Then you get to spend your mornings on your work, not on hunting for it.
