New Freelance Client, No Process? The Porridge Monday

2026-10-17

It is 8:05 on Monday morning and the porridge is starting to catch on the bottom of the pan.

You stir it with one hand. With the other you scroll your phone. The new client, the one you were so happy about on Thursday, has written: "Hi! So excited to start today. Where do we begin? Also, can you send over what you need from us?"

You read it twice. Then the thought arrives, quiet and a little cold: I don't actually know.

The yes that came with no map

Let's call you Priya. You are a freelance UX designer, four years in. The client is a family firm that makes kitchen fittings, and they said yes after one good call and a short email. You were thrilled. You even told a friend, "I think this could be a proper long-term one."

Now the porridge is done and you are standing at the counter, opening the laptop before you have eaten.

Where is the scope? Somewhere in an email thread. Or was it in the call notes you typed into your phone in the taxi? Who signs off on the designs: the owner, his daughter, or both? Which tool do they use? Do you already have the login to their shop admin, or did you only ask for it and then forget?

You find three different versions of the deadline in three different messages. None of them agree.

Nobody has done anything wrong. There is just no shape to the beginning.

What messy starts quietly cost

You answer the email with something cheerful and vague: "Great to be starting! I'll send over a few things shortly."

Shortly turns into the whole morning. You write a list of questions, delete half of it because it feels like too many, and send a smaller list that you know is incomplete. You tell yourself you will ask the rest as you go.

That is how it begins. A question here on Tuesday, a missing password on Wednesday. On Thursday the daughter says, "Oh, we thought the product photos were included." You thought they weren't. Neither of you wrote it down anywhere you can both point to.

So you pause, and you feel the small hot flush of an awkward conversation coming. You don't want to be the freelancer who sounds rigid on day four. You also don't want to quietly do twice the work and resent it by Friday.

This is the part nobody warns you about. It isn't dramatic. It's a low hum of am I doing this right? sitting under the whole first week of a job you were happy to win. It's the feeling of not being a "real business", even though you are doing real, good work.

The call that went differently

Picture the same Monday, a few months from now, with a different client.

At 8:05 the porridge is fine, because you aren't scrambling. The client's message arrives: "Excited to start! Where do we begin?"

And you already know. There is a first step, and it's the same first step every time. You send a short, friendly welcome. It tells them what happens this week, what you need from them and by when, and who decides what. You are not inventing it at the kitchen counter. You are simply doing the thing you always do.

The kickoff call is on Wednesday. You open it with a clear agenda and a one-page summary of what is in scope and what isn't. When the daughter asks about the product photos, you smile, because it's right there in black and white, and you say, "Good question, that's listed as not included. Shall we add it as an extra?"

No flush, no stammer. It's a normal, friendly sentence between two adults who both know the plan.

What changes when the start has a shape

Here is the odd thing. It isn't really about paperwork.

It's that a clear start lets the client relax too. People who know what happens next ask fewer anxious questions. They send the logins when you need them because you asked clearly, once. They start to treat you the way you hoped they would from the beginning: like someone who runs a proper studio, even if the studio is a kitchen table and a slightly burnt pan.

And you get your attention back. You are not carrying six open questions in the back of your head through the supermarket queue. The work gets your best hours, not your leftover ones.

You don't need a grand system for this. A simple, repeatable start is enough: the same questions, the same agenda, the same friendly confirmation of what you are doing and what you are not. Written once, reused every time.

Back at the counter

Back in the first Monday, you eat the porridge cold. It's fine. You write the client a better email than the vague one, and the week gets a little less messy, because you are a capable person and you will work it out.

But somewhere in the middle of that day you also think: I don't want to improvise this part ever again.

That thought is worth listening to. It isn't a sign that you're bad at running a business. It means you have outgrown the "we'll see how it goes" way of starting, and you're ready for something steadier.
