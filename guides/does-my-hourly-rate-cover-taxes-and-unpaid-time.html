Does My Hourly Rate Cover Taxes and Unpaid Time? The Bus Home

2026-10-15

The Number That Looked Fine at the Kitchen Table

The 17:48 bus is full, and you are standing, one hand on the rail, the other holding your phone.

A translator friend of yours, Lena, has just told you what she charges. Casually. Over coffee. And now you are doing sums in your head between two stops.

Your own rate is on the screen. It has been the same for two years. You picked it because it sounded reasonable, a bit like a number you had once heard someone else say.

A thought arrives and will not leave: Does this actually cover everything?

Not the invoice. The invoice is fine. The invoice is always fine. You mean everything around it. The tax you will owe next year. The hours you spent on a proposal nobody signed. The Friday you spent "just tidying up" your inbox.

The bus brakes. Your stomach does the same.

The Hours Nobody Pays For

Here is the thing you rarely say out loud: you work a full week and bill about half of it.

The rest is real work. It just has no line on an invoice. Answering the client who "has a quick question". Writing the quote. Chasing the quote. Finding last month's file. Sending the invoice, then looking for the invoice.

You don't resent the work, exactly. You resent that it is invisible, even to you.

So when you think of your rate as "a good hourly number", you are picturing a world where every hour is a paid hour. That world does not exist in your calendar. It never has.

The Money That Is Not Yours Yet

Then there is the other quiet thing.

Some of every payment is not really yours. It belongs to next spring, to the tax office, to the part of the year when you open an envelope with your heart already beating faster.

You know this. Of course you know this. But on a good month the account looks full, and a full account feels like permission.

So you spend a little more. You say yes to the trip. You buy the better chair. And somewhere under it all, a small voice keeps asking whether the number you charge was ever built to carry that weight.

Nobody around you can answer it. Your friends are employees. Their pay already arrives with the taxes taken off and the holidays built in. Yours arrives raw.

What You Actually Do With the Worry

You do what most people do. You nudge the thought away.

You tell yourself that busy means fine. That if clients aren't complaining about the price, the price must be right. That you will "look at it properly" when things calm down.

Things do not calm down. That is how a rate stays the same for two years.

And the cost is not dramatic. That is what makes it hard to notice. It is a little less in the account each month than you hoped. A weekend that is not quite a weekend. A faint tiredness when a client says "great, that works for us", because part of you knows it worked for them a bit too easily.

The Moment It Shifted

Let's say you are home. Bag on the hook, shoes still on.

You open a blank sheet, not to fix anything, only to look. You write down the plain things. What you would like to take home in a month. How many weeks you really want off. How many hours in a week are truly billable, not hopeful. A rough cushion for tax, which you will check with a professional later, because that part is not a guess you should make alone.

And the number that comes out is not frightening. It is just specific.

It is a bit higher than your rate. Or it is exactly right, and you breathe out for the first time since the bus. Either way, the fog is gone. You are no longer wondering whether you are a real business. You are looking at one.

That is the whole turn. Not a new strategy. Just seeing the shape of your own year in one place, in your own words.

A Normal Monday After That

Monday, 9:05. Coffee still too hot.

A new client asks for a price. You don't stall, and you don't hear your own voice go up at the end of the sentence. You say the number as if you were reading it off a card, because in a sense you are.

They ask for a small discount. You know exactly what is underneath your figure, so you can say no kindly, or yes with a clear reason. Either answer feels like yours.

At six you close the laptop. The unpaid hours are still there, because they always will be, but now they have a place in the sum instead of eating the edges of your evening.

The bus ride home is only a bus ride.
