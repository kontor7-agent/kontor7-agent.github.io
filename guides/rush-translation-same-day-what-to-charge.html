Client Wants a Rush Translation Today? What to Charge

2026-11-01

Your phone buzzes against the plastic table at 14:10. Your sister is outside on the jetty, shouting encouragement at a small boat that is mostly going sideways. You have a paper cup of lukewarm tea and a plate of someone else's flapjack.

The email is short. It is from a direct client, a small engineering firm you have worked with twice. Hi, sorry for the short notice. We need the attached supplier contract in English by 18:00 today. Can you do it? What would that cost?

You open the attachment. It is about 3,000 words, dense, full of clauses and numbers. You glance at the clock on the clubhouse wall.

Then you notice what your body is doing. Your shoulders have gone up. Your thumb hovers over the screen. You have not said yes yet, but part of you already has.

The sentence you can't find

The question underneath is not whether you can do it. You can. You will probably say yes, because you are good and they are nice and it is a Friday.

The question is the other half of their email. What would that cost?

You know your normal per-word rate, so you start typing it. Then you stop, because your normal rate belongs to a normal day. A day where the work arrives with a week of runway, where you choose your hours, where nobody asks you to give up a Friday afternoon, a family outing and probably dinner.

So you type something higher. It looks greedy. You delete it. You type something a little lower, and it looks like a favour. You delete that too.

Outside, a whistle blows. Somebody cheers. You haven't looked up.

What the quiet guess costs

Here is the thing nobody says out loud. Many of us price rush work by feeling. We pick a number that won't make the client flinch. We call it "a bit extra" and hope it is enough.

Later, usually around 21:00 with the contract finally sent and the family long since back from the jetty, the doubt arrives. Was the bit extra really enough? That whole afternoon, the plans you cancelled, the dinner you ate at the laptop. Did the money say thank you for any of it, or did it just say sure, no problem?

You don't know. That is the uncomfortable part. You don't have a number to point at. You have a vague sense of having been generous again, and a faint, familiar resentment toward a client who did nothing wrong.

It isn't their fault. They asked a fair question. They are waiting for a fair answer. The trouble is that you've never decided, in daylight and in advance, what a same-day request is worth to you.

The pause that changes the afternoon

So you do something small. You put the phone face down and watch the boat for a minute. It finally catches the wind and straightens out, looking almost like it meant to do that.

You think about a quieter version of this afternoon. In it, you already know what your hour is really worth after the unpaid parts: the back-and-forth, the formatting, the second read-through. You already know what a rush does to the rest of your day, and what you'd want in return for moving a whole afternoon.

In that version, you don't feel your way to a price. You glance at a page you made when you were calm, and the number is simply there. It isn't a negotiation with yourself. It's just your rate, with the rush written into it.

You can't build all of that in the next ten minutes at a plastic table. But you can do the honest thing. You reply:

Thanks for thinking of me. I can do this by 18:00. Because it moves my whole afternoon, same-day work is charged at a rush rate. I'll send the exact figure in a moment, and you can confirm before I start.

It is polite and clear, and it is not an apology. You press send and your shoulders drop an inch.

The figure you send twenty minutes later is higher than your instinct wanted. It's also the first time in a long while that you picked a rush price on purpose. Their reply comes before your tea is cold: Fine, thanks. Go ahead.

A different Monday

Now picture the Monday after the one in your head.

You open your laptop with coffee, not dread. Every job from last week sits in one calm view: the words, the time it really took, the rush fees, the discounts a CAT tool quietly took off a repeat-heavy file. Each job has an hourly figure next to it. Not a guess, a figure.

There is also a short list of who has paid and who hasn't. The agency that always takes sixty days is easy to spot. So is the direct client who pays on the day.

You don't feel like a business winging it. You feel like someone who runs one, quietly and well, and who can answer what would that cost? in a single steady sentence.

Nothing dramatic has happened. You still translate. You still have Fridays like this one. But the Friday-afternoon knot isn't there anymore, because the number stopped being a mood.
