Client Promised to Pay Twice, Still Nothing? What to Say

2026-10-16

The plum queue

It is Saturday, 10:40, and you are third in line at the farm shop checkout with a basket of plums and a jar of honey.

Your phone buzzes in your jacket pocket. You pull it out too fast.

It's not the bank. It's a newsletter about autumn sales.

Let's call you Jonas. You edit video for small companies. Three weeks ago you delivered the final cut for a client who said it was "exactly what we wanted." Two weeks ago, the invoice came due. Nothing arrived.

So you wrote a polite note. The reply was friendly: "Sorry, it's with accounting, you'll have it by Friday."

Friday came. Nothing.

You wrote again, a bit shorter. "Hi Anna, just checking in on the invoice." The answer arrived within the hour: "So sorry! Payment is scheduled for early next week, promise."

It is now the week after "early next week."

Two promises and a silence

The cashier scans your honey. You smile at her, and in your head you are drafting message number three.

Hi Anna, I hope you're well. I just wanted to gently...

Gently what? Remind her? Beg? Threaten?

The problem isn't the money alone, though the money matters. Your rent goes out on the first. The problem is that two promises have now been broken, and every possible sentence feels wrong.

Too soft, and she'll say "of course" again and nothing changes. Too firm, and you might lose a client who sends you work four times a year.

So you stand there in a farm shop, holding a paper bag of plums, running a negotiation in your head with someone who is probably at her own Saturday breakfast, not thinking about you at all.

That's the part nobody tells you. Being owed money is quiet. It doesn't shout. It sits in the back of your mind while you pretend to enjoy the weekend.

What it quietly costs

On the drive home you notice it.

You've checked your banking app four times since breakfast. You were short with your partner over something small. You've been rehearsing sentences instead of listening to the radio.

And underneath it all, a thought you'd never say out loud: Maybe I'm not a real business. A real business wouldn't have to ask twice.

But that isn't true. Late payment happens to people with accountants and receptionists too. The difference is that they have a rhythm for it. A first note on this day, a second on that day, a firmer one after that. Nobody is wondering whether they're being rude. The rhythm decides, not their nerves.

You don't have a rhythm. You have a knot in your stomach and a draft folder.

The turning point

At home you put the plums in a bowl and sit down at the kitchen table with your laptop. You open your sent folder and read your own two messages one after the other.

They look different on the screen than they felt in your head. They're polite. They're clear. They are also, you notice, completely vague. Neither one has a date. Neither one says what happens next.

You realise that you've been asking Anna a question ("could you maybe pay?") when what you need is to tell her something calm and factual: this is the invoice, this is the amount, this is the date I'd like to see it settled.

That's not begging. That's just clarity, and it was never rude.

The second realisation is bigger. You are writing this message from scratch, with a racing heart, on a Saturday. Next month you'll do exactly the same thing with another client. Nothing about this has to be reinvented each time.

You could have the words ready. You could know in advance what the first nudge sounds like, the second, the one after that. You could see at a glance which invoices are overdue and what the next step is, without opening three tabs and guessing.

A different Monday

Picture it a few weeks from now.

It's Monday, 9:05. Coffee beside the keyboard. You open one sheet and see two invoices marked overdue and a suggested next step beside each.

For the first, the gentle reminder is due today. You paste a message you already trust, change the name, and send it. It takes ninety seconds.

For the second, a firmer note is due on Thursday. You don't think about it until Thursday.

Then you close the tab and get on with the edit you actually enjoy.

No draft folder full of half-sentences. No refreshing the banking app in the supermarket queue. No lying awake composing the perfect sentence.

The money still arrives when it arrives. Some clients are slow, and that doesn't change. But you are no longer slow, and you no longer feel like you are pleading. You are simply following through, calmly, the way a professional does.

Back to Anna

That Saturday, Jonas writes the third message in about four minutes. It names the invoice, the amount and the original due date. It thanks her for the earlier replies, and it asks for a specific payment date by midweek.

No apology. No drama. Just a clear sentence and a friendly sign-off.

He hits send, eats a plum, and goes for a walk.

That message is the easy part. The lasting relief comes from not having to build it from nothing every single time.
