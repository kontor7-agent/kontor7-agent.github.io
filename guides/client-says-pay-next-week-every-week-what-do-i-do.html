Client Says 'Next Week' Every Week? The Balcony Tomatoes

2026-10-18

It is Monday, 8:05 am. Mara is standing on her balcony in socks, watering tomatoes that are not doing well, with her phone in the other hand.

The message on the screen is from Jonas, her biggest client. It says: Hi Mara, sorry, payment goes out next week, promise!

She scrolls up. Last Monday: Should go out next week. The Monday before: Finance is on it, next week. Before that, a thumbs-up emoji and the word "soon".

Six weeks. One invoice. Mara counts them on her fingers, then stops, because counting feels a bit like picking at a scab.

The nice reply

She types: No worries, thanks for letting me know!

She looks at it. The exclamation mark is doing a lot of work. She sends it anyway.

This is the part nobody warns you about. The money matters, of course. But the real weight is the little performance: staying friendly, staying easy, staying the kind of freelancer who doesn't make things awkward. Meanwhile the rent is due on the first, and her own planning for the month now has a hole in it the exact shape of Jonas's invoice.

She puts the phone face down on the railing. A tomato leaf curls a little further.

What "next week" costs

It is never just the invoice.

It is the second-guessing at the supermarket checkout, when she holds her card and does a quick sum in her head before it beeps. It is the project she says yes to at a lower price than she would like, because a slow month would be frightening. It is the way she rereads Jonas's emails now, looking for tone, as if she were a detective instead of a designer.

And there is the quiet question she hasn't said out loud to anyone: Is it me? Am I doing this wrong? Would a "real" business have this problem?

She has half-written three reminders in the last month. One was too soft, so it apologised for existing. One was too sharp, and she deleted it with her heart racing. The third is still in her drafts, titled "Re: Re: Re: Invoice", untouched since a Thursday she no longer remembers.

The call at ten

At ten she has a call with a different client, Priya, about a logo refresh. It goes well. Priya is warm and organised, and at the end she says, "I'll pay the deposit today, just send me the details."

Mara hangs up and feels something odd. Not joy, exactly. More like surprise at how ordinary that was. Someone said they'd pay, and the sentence simply meant what it said.

She sits there with her tea going cold and thinks about the difference. It isn't that Priya is a better person than Jonas. Jonas is probably buried in his own inbox, and "next week" is just the easiest thing to type. The difference is that with Priya, there was never a gap for "next week" to fit into.

With Jonas, there was. And every week she left the gap open, it grew a little more comfortable for both of them.

The turning point is small

Nothing dramatic happens. She doesn't storm anywhere or send a furious email.

She just notices, standing in her kitchen, that she has been treating each "next week" as a fresh, separate event, something to react to on the spot with a nervous smile. As if she were improvising every time. As if nobody had ever been in this situation before.

But people have. Freelancers have been waiting on "next week" for as long as there have been invoices. Which means there must be a calmer way to handle it: a rhythm, a set of words that are kind and clear at the same time, a point where the tone changes gently and on schedule instead of in a panic. Something decided in advance, on a quiet day, so that the Monday-morning version of her doesn't have to invent it with a pounding pulse.

The thought is almost a relief by itself. She isn't bad at this. She just never had a plan, only a feeling.

A different Monday

Picture the same balcony a few weeks later.

The tomatoes have recovered, mostly. Mara's phone buzzes with a message from a client: Payment next week!

She doesn't flinch. She glances at one overview, sees that this invoice is eleven days past due, sees which step comes next, and sends a short, friendly, clear note she has already written. No apology in it. No edge either. Just a date, an amount, and a polite request to confirm when it will arrive.

Then she goes back to her coffee.

The invoice might still take a few days. That's fine. What has changed is that she is no longer waiting. She is following something. The knot that used to sit under her ribs on Mondays has loosened to a small, manageable thing, the way a stiff shoulder does after a good stretch.

She closes the laptop at six. Not because the money has arrived, but because she knows exactly where things stand, and that is enough to let the evening be hers.
