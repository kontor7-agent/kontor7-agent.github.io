Bank Statement CSV Messy Columns? The Twenty-Minute Wait

2026-11-08

It is 14:40. You are parked outside the school gate with the engine off and a laptop balanced on your knees.

The bell rings at 15:00. Twenty minutes. Enough time, you told yourself this morning, to pull the bank export into your spreadsheet and finally see what came in this quarter.

You imagine Mira, an illustrator, in exactly this seat. She downloaded the file an hour ago. Now she double-clicks it.

Everything lands in column A. One long, grey smear of text, commas and quotation marks. She tries the import wizard. Now the dates are split across two columns, and one of them says 03/04 when it could mean March or April. The amounts have a minus sign in front of some and a little "S" or "H" behind others. The payee names have been squeezed together with the reference text, as if someone had packed a suitcase in a hurry.

And there, in row 14, sits a header line again. The bank apparently decided to introduce itself twice.

The thought behind the screen

Mira does not think "my data needs cleaning." She thinks something quieter and sharper.

A real business would have this sorted.

She thinks about the client who paid last month. Did that money arrive? She believes so. She is almost sure. But she cannot point at it. It is somewhere in that grey smear, between a supermarket and a streaming subscription.

A car door slams two spaces away. A parent walks past with a scooter under one arm. Mira's cursor hovers over the delete key, then over the "undo" arrow, then back again.

She tries the manual way. Select a column. Split it. Fix the dates. Replace the commas. Now the amounts have turned into text, and the total at the bottom says zero. She laughs once, without any joy in it.

What it quietly costs

Nobody counts this part. It is not the twenty minutes. It is what the twenty minutes were supposed to buy.

It was supposed to buy a clear picture before the invoice call on Thursday. It was supposed to buy the sentence "yes, I can see exactly what's open." Instead she will open that call with a vague "I'll check and get back to you," and hear how small it sounds.

It costs the Sunday evening she will spend untangling it anyway. It costs the small flinch each time her bank sends a new statement. She starts to dread the file itself, as if it were the file's fault.

Mira closes the laptop lid halfway. Through the windscreen she watches the gate. Still nine minutes.

The turn

Here is the thing she does not know yet, and maybe you don't either.

The mess is not a sign that you are bad with money. The mess is just how banks export. Odd columns, mixed date styles, headers that repeat, signs in strange places. Every export carries a bit of this, and it will look the same next month and the month after.

Which means it can be handled once, in the same way, every time.

Picture a different version of that car. Mira drops the file onto something small that she set up one quiet afternoon. It tells her what it would change before it changes anything: so many dates tidied, so many duplicate rows spotted, so many columns split. She glances through the list, nods, and lets it run. Her original file stays untouched, and a short report says exactly what happened.

The spreadsheet fills in. Amounts add up. The client payment sits in its row, plain as a loaf of bread on a shelf.

That whole thing takes less time than the kettle at home.

An ordinary Monday, afterwards

In that version, Monday morning looks unremarkable, and that is the point.

The new export arrives. She drops it in, glances at the change report with her coffee, and opens her overview. She sees who has paid and who hasn't. She sees one amount that looks odd and checks it with a calm mind, because she trusts the rest.

There is no heavy feeling when the bank email comes. The thursday call opens with "yes, I have it in front of me." Her voice sounds like a person who runs a business, because she does.

And the twenty minutes outside the school gate are twenty minutes of nothing in particular. A podcast. A cold apple. Watching the doors for a small figure with a rucksack.
