Rainy Market Day, Barely Covered Pitch Fee? Any Profit?

2026-11-01

4:40 pm, under a dripping gazebo

The rain started at nine and never really stopped.

Now it runs off the corner of your gazebo in a thin, steady rope, and you have stopped moving the crates to dodge it. Your cash tin is on the folding table. You have counted it twice. The second count was just to hear the coins again.

The pitch fee was paid at eight, before the sky turned. You remember handing it over, cheerful, a bit proud. Another market day. Look at me, doing this.

Now you stand there with a pen behind your ear and a thought you do not want to finish: did I make anything today? Or did I just pay to stand in the rain?

The sum you do with your thumb

You are Ines, in this story. You print linocut cards and tote bags at the kitchen table. Ines is made up, but you may know her well.

The tin holds more than the pitch fee. Not much more. A woman bought two cards and said "lovely" in a voice that meant she was leaving. A man in a green anorak bought a tote and then asked if you had a smaller bag, which you did not.

So you do the sum the way everyone does it in a wet stall. Takings, minus fee. A small number is left. A small positive number. That feels almost like a win, so you hold onto it.

But something nags underneath, the way a stone sits in a shoe.

The fee is not the only thing you spent. There was the petrol. The two cards that curled in the damp and cannot be sold. The ink and paper that went into every tote on the table, bought weeks ago and long forgotten. And the Thursday and Friday evenings you spent carving and printing, which cost you something too, even though no one handed you an invoice for them.

None of that is in your thumb sum.

The part nobody says out loud

You pack the crates slowly. The tarpaulin drips down your sleeve.

What you rarely admit is that you have never really known. Some market days you feel rich because the tin is heavy. Some days you feel foolish because it is light. But the feeling follows the weather, not the facts.

The best-selling tote might be the one that quietly earns the least. The cheap cards might be carrying the whole stall. Or the other way round. You honestly could not say.

And there is a smaller, sharper worry beneath that one. If a friend asked, "Is the stall worth it?", you would smile and say, "Oh, it's going well." It would be a polite answer. It would not be a true one, because you do not know.

That is the part that stings in the rain. It is not the loss. It is not knowing whether there was one.

The drive home

The wipers swish. The crates rattle in the boot. Somewhere near the roundabout you notice that you are not tired from the work. You are tired from the wondering.

You think about the notebook at home with half a column of market sales in it. The other notebook for online orders. The envelope of receipts marked "stall stuff", which is mostly just a feeling in paper form.

You think: it should not be this hard to find out if a day was good.

And then, quieter: it probably does not have to be.

What a calmer Sunday looks like

Picture the same rain, a different ending.

You get home. You put the kettle on first, because you are still cold. You open one page and enter what sold, what is left, and what was in the tin at the start and at the end. It takes a few minutes, not an evening.

And then the page simply tells you. Not with a lecture, just a plain answer: this much came in, this much went out, this much stayed with you. You see which things earned their place on the table and which ones only filled it.

Maybe the answer is "not much today." That is allowed. A rainy day can be a thin day. But a thin day you understand is very different from a thin day you dread.

You can look at it, nod, and think: fine. Rain. Next time I'll bring more of what actually sells, and fewer of what only looks pretty.

Then you close the laptop. The evening is yours. The tote bags can wait until Tuesday.

Not a better season, just a clearer one

The weather will do what it likes. Stalls will have wet days. That is part of working with your hands and standing in a field with a gazebo and a hopeful heart.

What can change is the quiet after. The knot in the stomach at 4:40 pm. The "it's going well" you say without being sure. The feeling of being a hobbyist who happens to pay a pitch fee, instead of a maker who runs a stall.

You are the second one. You just have not been able to see your own numbers yet.
