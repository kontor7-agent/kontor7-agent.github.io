Customer Ordered, Out of Stock Again? The Yarn Pickup

2026-10-19

It is Thursday, 12:40, and you are standing in the car park behind the garden centre with a crate of yarn on the bonnet.

This is your pickup point. Every week, a handful of customers drive up, say hello, and take home the little paper-wrapped parcels you packed the night before.

A woman in a green raincoat walks over, smiling. She gives her name. You find her parcel and start to hand it over.

Then she says, "Oh, and is the blue one in there too? The dusky one? I ordered two skeins."

You look down. One skein. One.

You already know why. Somewhere in your head, you knew last Sunday that the dusky blue was nearly gone. You just didn't know how nearly. Someone else bought it on Tuesday. The shop page still said "in stock" when she clicked.

The sentence you've said too often

"I'm so sorry, I seem to have miscounted."

You hear yourself say it, and it sounds smaller than you want it to. She is kind about it. That is almost the worst part. She says, "Oh, don't worry, these things happen," and her eyes drift to the crate as if she is deciding whether to be disappointed.

You offer a refund. You offer to dye more. You promise a date you are not sure you can keep.

She nods, takes her one skein, and says she'll see. Her car pulls out slowly. You stand there with the crate and the drizzle and a hot face.

It isn't the money, exactly. It is the small, specific shame of looking like someone who doesn't quite run a real shop.

What it quietly costs

You don't tell anyone about moments like this, because they sound so minor. One skein. One customer. Hardly a crisis.

But they add up in ways that are hard to see.

There is the refund email you write on your phone in the car. There is the apology that takes three drafts. There is the customer who was going to tell her knitting group about you, and now maybe won't.

There is also the Sunday evening habit you've built: walking around the shelves in the spare room, counting by eye, trusting your memory a little too much. Some weeks it works. Some weeks a colour you swore you had is simply gone, and you find out from a stranger.

And there is the other side of the same problem. The mustard yellow you over-ordered in spring still sits in a box under the bed, quietly holding your money hostage, while the colours people actually want run out.

Both are the same worry. You don't see your stock. You feel it, vaguely, and hope.

The drive home

On the way back, you're not angry at the customer. You're not even really angry at yourself. You're just tired of being surprised.

At a red light, a thought arrives, quietly, without drama: What if I didn't have to remember this?

Not a bigger memory. Not more careful counting. Just a place where the numbers live, so that the dusky blue could say, days earlier, "you're getting low", instead of letting you find out at a car park in the rain.

It seems almost too ordinary to be the answer. But ordinary is exactly what you want. You don't need a warehouse system. You need your shelf, written down in a way that tells you the truth.

A different Thursday

Picture the same pickup a few weeks on.

The crate is on the bonnet again. The drizzle is back, because of course it is. A customer walks over and asks about a colour.

And you already know. You knew on Monday morning, over tea, when you glanced at one page and saw which three colours were running down and which one was sitting too long. You reordered the dye base before it mattered. The blue is there, and so is a second skein if she wants it.

Nothing dramatic happens. She takes her parcel, says thank you, and talks about the cardigan she's making.

That's the whole change. No apology sentence. No hot face. No refund drafted at a red light.

On Sunday night you don't walk the shelves with your stomach tight. You open one sheet, you look, you close it. Ten minutes, maybe less, and the evening is yours again.

It also feels a little different in your own head. Not smug, just steadier. Like someone who runs a proper shop, because you do.
