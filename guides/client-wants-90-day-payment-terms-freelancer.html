Client Wants 90 Day Payment Terms? The Bike Rack Call

2026-10-28

It's 11:05 on a Wednesday, and you're standing by the bike rack outside the co-working space with your phone pressed to one ear. You stepped out because the call felt too big for the open-plan room.

The client is friendly. That's the problem. "We'd love to work with you again," she says. "Just so you know, our standard payment terms are 90 days."

You hear yourself say, "Okay. Sure. Let me have a look."

You don't mean it. Your mouth just wanted the silence to end.

The sentence you didn't say

After the call you stay by the bike rack for a while. A cyclist locks up next to you and nods. You nod back like someone with nothing on their mind.

In your head the sentence you didn't say is already running on a loop. Ninety days is three months. Three months of rent, groceries and software subscriptions, paid out of my own pocket while someone else's accounts department takes its time.

Then a second thought, quieter and worse: Who am I to push back? They're a big name. If I make a fuss, they'll just find someone who doesn't.

You go back to your desk and open the project. It's good work, the kind you like. You can't concentrate on it.

What it quietly costs

The worry isn't really about the number 90. It's about everything that comes with it.

It's the fear of a slow month stacking on top of a late payment. It's the feeling that you'd have to keep track of one more invoice in one more tab, in a head that's already full. It's the dread of the day an invoice turns overdue, when you'd have to write the email that starts with "Just a quick follow-up..." and takes you forty minutes.

And under that is the old feeling you rarely say out loud: that chasing money feels like begging.

So you do what many of us do. You tell yourself it'll be fine, and you carry it around like a stone in your coat pocket.

At lunch you stand in the supermarket queue, checking your banking app for the third time this week. The total in front of you is small. The knot in your stomach isn't.

The question underneath

That evening you cook pasta and finally let yourself think instead of just worry.

The real question isn't "Do I accept 90 days?" That's a decision about your business, and it's yours. You might negotiate. You might ask for a deposit. You might say yes for a client that matters, or say no. Anyone who knows your contracts and your situation, a tax professional or a lawyer, can help you with the specifics.

The real question is smaller and kinder: If I did say yes, would I know exactly where I stand?

Would you see, on a Monday morning, what's due this week, what's three weeks out and what's already late? Would the next step be obvious without you having to find the right words from scratch? Or would every open invoice be another thing you have to remember?

That's when it clicks. Your fear isn't really about the number of days. It's about being alone with the waiting, with no clear picture and no calm way to say something when the date passes.

That part can change.

A different kind of Monday

Picture the same bike rack, a few weeks later.

It's Monday, 9:10. You have coffee. You open one sheet and look at it for two minutes, no more. Three invoices are open. One is due Friday. One is a month out. One passed its date yesterday, and next to it a plain note says what the next step is.

No guessing. No scrolling through old threads to remember what you wrote last time.

You copy a short, warm message that sounds like you on a good day, change the client's name, and send it. It's polite. It's clear. It doesn't apologise for existing.

Then you close the tab and get on with the work you actually enjoy.

The long payment terms, if you choose to accept them, are still long. But they're no longer a fog. They're dates on a page, and each one has a calm next move waiting.

On the next call, when someone says "our standard is 90 days," you have room to breathe. You can say, "Let me come back to you on that," and mean it. You're not freezing at a bike rack. You're thinking clearly because the rest of your money admin is not on fire.

What changes when chasing stops feeling like begging

People who work for themselves rarely get taught how to follow up on money. You learn design or code or writing, and then you're expected to know how to be firm and friendly at the same time, alone, at your kitchen table.

But it's a skill made mostly of small things: knowing when to write, having words ready, and seeing the whole picture in one place. Once those are in order, the dread shrinks. Waiting stays just waiting.

You might still decide to negotiate those terms. That's a good conversation to have with a clear head. But whatever you decide, you don't have to carry the open invoices in your head.
