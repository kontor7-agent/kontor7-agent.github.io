Forgot Which Lessons You Taught? The Barbecue Question

2026-10-20

The sausages are spitting on the grill when your brother-in-law turns to you with a paper plate in his hand.

"So how's the teaching going? Busy month?"

It's the last Saturday of the month, mid-afternoon, in your sister's garden. Someone has put out a bowl of crisps and a jug of lemonade with too much ice. You open your mouth to say "Yes, busy," and realise you don't actually know.

You think of Elena, who does Italian on Tuesdays. Did she come three times or four? There was a Thursday when Tom's Spanish slot moved to Friday. Was that this month or last? And who paid? There was a transfer that said only "lesson", from a name you didn't recognise.

"Yeah, good," you say. "Pretty full."

You eat your sausage. You do not taste it.

The sum you do in your head

Here's what nobody tells you about teaching one-to-one. The lessons are the easy part. You like the lessons. The moment a student finally gets the subjunctive, or the Bach piece stops falling apart in bar twelve, is why you do this.

The hard part is the thin layer around the lessons. A note in your phone. A half-filled page in a diary. A message thread where someone said, "Can we swap to Thursday? I'll pay both next time."

So on the way home from the barbecue, you start the sum. Which lessons happened. Which were cancelled. Which were cancelled so late you couldn't fill the slot. Who paid in cash, who by bank transfer, who said they'd do it "this week" two weeks ago.

You are on the tram, holding the rail, adding it up in your head like a shopping list with no pen.

And there's a second, quieter thought underneath. Am I the kind of person who doesn't know this? A real teacher, a real business, surely has it written down somewhere. You have it written down in six places, all of them slightly different.

What it costs when you don't know

The money is the obvious cost. A lesson taught and never paid for is an hour of your life given away by accident. But you've noticed the other costs too.

You don't send the reminder, because you aren't sure you're right. Maybe they paid and you missed it. Maybe it was the other student. Writing "Just checking on last month's lessons" to someone who may have already paid is a particular kind of embarrassing. So you wait, and the waiting turns into a small grudge, and the grudge shows up in the lesson as a slightly shorter smile.

Then there are the late cancellations. One here, one there. Each feels too small to mention. Added together they might be a whole day of your month, but you'll never know, because nobody is adding them up. You only feel it as a vague sense that you're working a lot and it doesn't quite show.

And when you think about raising your rate, you can't. You don't know what you really earn per lesson, once the gaps and the no-shows are counted. So the price stays where it was two years ago, and you quietly resent it.

The Sunday that went differently

The turning point isn't dramatic. It's a Sunday morning, with coffee, and one quiet thought: It shouldn't take a barbecue question to make me realise I can't answer this.

What you want is small. You want one place where a lesson goes in the day it happens. Taught, cancelled, moved. You want one place where a payment goes in the day it arrives. And you want to look at it and see, without any detective work, who is up to date and who is a little behind.

Not a system with fifteen tabs. Not accounting software that wants to know about your business structure. Just the plain, honest record you'd have kept if anyone had ever shown you how.

An ordinary Monday, afterwards

Imagine the last Saturday of next month. Same garden, same lemonade, same brother-in-law.

"Busy month?"

"Thirty-one lessons," you say. "Two cancelled late. One student's a bit behind, I'll nudge her on Monday."

That's it. No tram-ride arithmetic. No small cold feeling in your stomach.

On Monday morning you open one file. You see the name, you see the date she's behind from, and the message you send is friendly and clear because you know you're right. Nobody feels begged. Nobody feels accused. It's just a teacher who knows her own month.

A few weeks later you notice something else. Looking at what the late cancellations actually added up to, you finally have a real number to think about when you consider your rate. Not a guess. Just something you can see.

And the lessons feel lighter, because the layer around them has stopped nagging.
