Accountant Wants Your Receipts? The Passport Office Ticket

2026-10-18

It is 10:25 on a Wednesday, and you are holding ticket B214.

The passport office smells of wet coats and printer toner. The screen above the counters says B189. You have a good half hour of sitting ahead of you, so you take out your phone and open your email.

There it is, between a newsletter and a calendar reminder. Subject line: Documents for your annual accounts.

Your accountant is friendly, as always. "Could you send me all receipts and expense records for the year by Friday? Thanks so much!"

Your stomach does a small, familiar drop.

The inventory you start without wanting to

Tobias edits video for a living. You could be Tobias, or a designer, or a translator, or a coach. The details change, the drop in the stomach does not.

You begin to count in your head, because that is all you can do on a plastic chair.

The new lens: there was an email receipt. Somewhere. The software subscriptions: those go through the card, so they must be in the bank app. The co-working day passes in spring: paper, possibly in a jacket. The train ticket to the shoot in March: you think you photographed it. Or you meant to.

Then there is the shoebox on the wardrobe. And the folder on your desktop called "Misc". And the camera roll, where a receipt for a tripod sits between a sunset and a picture of your neighbour's dog.

B191.

None of this is a disaster. You know that. Nobody is accusing you of anything. But you are quietly ashamed, and that is the part nobody talks about. You run a business. You send invoices, you meet deadlines, clients praise your work. And still you cannot answer a simple question from the person who helps you with your books: where are your receipts?

What it quietly costs

The cost is rarely the receipts themselves. It is everything around them.

It is the Sunday you spend with the shoebox open on the sofa instead of going for a walk. It is the low hum of "I should really sort that" that follows you through months of otherwise good work. It is the guess you make when a receipt is missing, followed by the small doubt that you guessed wrong. It is the faint feeling of not being a "real business", even though you very much are one.

And it is the reply you now have to write. You can feel it forming already: Sure, I'll get everything over to you! Said with confidence you do not have.

B194.

The thought on the plastic chair

You look at the people around you. A man with a folder in a clear plastic sleeve. A woman holding a passport photo like a winning lottery ticket. Everyone has brought exactly what the counter asks for, in the order it asks for it.

And you think: they have a list.

That is all it is. The folder man is not smarter than you. He was just told what is needed, and he put it in one place.

It hits you gently. Your problem is not that you are disorganised by nature. Your problem is that nobody ever gave you one simple place for receipts to land, and one short list of what the year-end needs. You have been improvising with a shoebox, a camera roll and good intentions.

A place for each receipt as it arrives. A list that shows what is done and what is still open. A date or two written down where you can see them. That is not a transformation. It is just a bit of order.

It doesn't have to be like this.

The same email, a different Wednesday

Imagine the same email arriving, but you are somewhere else in your head.

You read it. You breathe out. You think, yes, I know where that is.

The receipts from the year sit in one list, line by line: date, what it was, how much, and whether the proof is saved. Nothing is perfect, but it is there. A couple of lines have a little note: still need the invoice for this. You know which ones. A progress bar shows you are most of the way through, and the remaining bit looks like twenty minutes, not a weekend.

You reply from the waiting room: "Yes, I'll send it over on Thursday." And you mean it.

Then you put the phone away and watch the screen tick over to B197, with nothing heavier on your mind than your passport photo.

The part where nothing dramatic happens

That is what calm looks like, by the way. It is not a big moment. It is a normal Monday where the year-end is a small, visible task rather than a fog.

You drop a receipt into the list when you get it, or once a week with your coffee. You glance at your checklist now and then. You know which date matters and when. The shoebox, if it exists at all, is just a box.

And in January, when everyone else is turning their flat upside down, you close the laptop at six.
