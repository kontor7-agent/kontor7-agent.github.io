Double Booked Clients, Tips and Cash Off? The Two Coats

2026-10-28

It is 10:58 on a Saturday, and two coats are hanging on the same hook.

Mara, a stylist who rents a chair in a three-chair salon, is standing between two women who both smile at her. One booked a colour refresh. The other booked a cut and a blow-dry. Both were promised eleven o'clock. Both are sure of it.

She checks the diary. Her handwriting says one thing. A text on her phone says another. The booking from last Tuesday was never copied across.

You know this feeling. The heat climbs up your neck while your face stays friendly. "Give me two minutes," you say, and you hear your own voice sounding a little too bright.

The apology you can't charge for

One client agrees to wait forty minutes and takes a coffee. She is kind about it. That makes it worse.

By the time the second one sits down, Mara is behind for the whole afternoon. The 2 pm arrives to find her mid-foil. The lunch she planned is a banana eaten standing up between clients.

Nobody is angry. Everyone is nice. But a small voice is doing arithmetic behind her eyes: that's a gap I can't get back, and a client who now thinks I'm disorganised.

And you are not disorganised. You are one person doing the work of a receptionist, a bookkeeper and a stylist, with a diary, a phone and a memory.

The jar, the card reader and the Sunday kitchen table

Sunday afternoon, Mara sits at the kitchen table with a cup of tea going cold and a small zip bag of cash.

She has the card terminal's weekly summary. She has a notebook with ticks and half-numbers. She has a jar where tips went. Some tips came as cash, some on the card, and some she can't place, because a client said "keep the change" and she nodded, thinking I'll remember that.

She counts the cash twice. She gets two different numbers. She counts a third time, slower, with the radio off.

The number is not wrong, exactly. It just doesn't match anything. It is too low compared with what she thinks she earned. Or her memory is too high. She can't tell which, and that is the part that sits in her chest.

It isn't about being cheated. Nobody cheated her. It's the not knowing. Did that haircut get paid for? Did the Wednesday no-show ever send her deposit? Is the week good, or does it only feel busy?

She closes the notebook and tells herself she will sort it properly next weekend. She has told herself that before.

What the muddle quietly costs

The double booking cost her a bit of face and an afternoon. The tally costs her something quieter.

It costs her the Sunday evening. She was meant to be done by four, but the unfinished sum hangs around until bedtime. It costs her the ease in her voice when a friend asks, "So how's business?" and she says, "Busy!" without being able to say whether it's true.

And it costs her the sense that she runs a real business. She has chair rent, products, a loyal book of regulars and a skill people travel for. What she doesn't have is a single place where the week sits still long enough to be looked at.

The moment it clicks

None of this needs a dramatic fix. That's the turning point, and it is gentle.

It comes on an ordinary Wednesday, when a regular reschedules and Mara thinks: what if every booking, every payment and every missed appointment lived in one calm place, and I just filled in a line as I went?

Not an app that nags her. Not accounting software built for companies with staff. Just a simple sheet, made for someone like her, that answers three questions without a fight: who is coming when, what came in, and what did the empty slots cost?

That's all. One page that already knows what a cut, a colour and a beard trim are worth.

A normal Saturday, afterwards

Picture the same Saturday, a few weeks on.

At 10:50 two coats arrive, but they are not on the same hook, because the diary showed two different times. Mara has already had a quiet look at the day over her morning coffee and knows exactly where the gaps are.

When a client no-shows, she doesn't feel the sting alone. She notes it, and the cost is simply there, plain and unemotional. She can decide what to do about it, a deposit perhaps, or a kinder reminder, without the guilt.

On Sunday she opens the sheet, matches the cash and the card against what she wrote down, and the numbers agree. It takes her about as long as the kettle. The tips are where she logged them. The week has a shape.

She closes the laptop at four. The afternoon is hers. She is walking by the river and, for once, not counting anything.
