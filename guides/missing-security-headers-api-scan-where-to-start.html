Missing Security Headers on Your API? The Tool Library

2026-11-08

Monday, 9:12 a.m. You are third in line at the tool library, holding a borrowed drill bit case you meant to return on Friday. Your phone buzzes against the cardboard tag in your hand.

It is a forwarded email from a client. The subject line says only: "Scan results, can you look?"

You open the attachment while the man in front of you debates the merits of two sanders. The PDF is mostly red. A tidy table of findings. Near the top, in bold: Missing security headers. Then a list of names you half recognise, each with a severity badge next to it.

Your stomach does a small, familiar drop.

The sentence you can't un-read

You built this API. You shipped it. It works. Nobody has complained. And yet there it is, in a document with the client's logo on the footer: something is missing.

The thought arrives fully formed: What else is missing that the scanner didn't mention?

You scroll. The report is eleven pages. Page two links to a glossary. Page three links to a standard. Page four says "remediation advice" and then gives you a paragraph that reads like it was written for someone with a team and a Tuesday free.

The woman behind the counter says, "Next?" You step forward, hand over the drill bits, and say thank you in a voice that sounds completely normal.

Inside, you are already composing the reply. Thanks, will look into it. Too vague. On it, will fix by Friday. Too bold. You don't yet know what "it" is.

What it quietly costs

Here is the part nobody puts in the report.

The cost is not the headers. Headers are, in the end, small. The cost is the way the whole morning tilts. You carry the PDF around like a stone in your pocket. You take the bus home and read page six twice. You open three browser tabs, each promising a complete guide, each a different length, each disagreeing on what matters most.

By noon you have learned a great many things and decided nothing.

By the afternoon you notice the other voice, the quiet one. A real professional would just know where to start.

That voice is wrong, but it is persuasive. Because the truth is that a scan result is a list of symptoms. It does not tell you the order. It does not tell you which items are a quick tidy-up and which deserve a proper look. It does not tell you what to say to the client who is waiting, politely, in your inbox.

And so you do what many solo developers do. You fix the first thing on the list, then the second, and somewhere around the fourth you realise you are not sure whether you are making the API safer or just making the report greener.

The turning point is small

It doesn't arrive as a lightning bolt. It arrives while you are rinsing a mug.

You think: What if the report is not the question?

The client did not ask for a perfect scan. They asked: Can you look? What they want, underneath, is to hear a calm voice say: "I've reviewed it, here is what I found, here is what I changed, here is what's next."

That is not a heroic task. It is an ordinary one. It needs a shape, a plain list of the things worth checking, in a sensible order, with a place to tick them off and a way to write it up so the client can read it in two minutes.

The headers become one line in that list. Not the whole mountain. One line among others, next to the login questions, the access questions, the things a scanner cannot see from outside.

You breathe out. First time since the bus.

A different Monday

Picture the same morning, with a small difference.

The email arrives. You glance at the red table. You still feel the flicker, you are human, but you know where the notebook is. You know the list. You know that the scan is one input, and that you have a way of looking at the rest.

So you stay in the queue. You hand over the drill bits. You walk out into the cold, and you reply to the client, short and level: "Thanks. I'll go through the findings and the wider checks, and send you a short summary by Thursday."

Then you go to work. By lunchtime something has changed that has nothing to do with code. The stone is out of your pocket. You have a list with some boxes ticked, a few notes, and a plan you can say out loud.

Thursday comes. The summary is one page. The client writes back: "Great, thanks."

That is the whole drama. Nobody applauds. You simply close the laptop at six, and the evening is yours.
