Keep Selling Items You Don't Have? The Cinema Foyer

2026-10-15

It is Sunday, 14:20, and you are standing in a cinema foyer holding a paper cup of popcorn you did not want.

Your sister booked the tickets weeks ago. It is her birthday. You promised yourself: phone on silent, shop closed in your head, two hours off.

Then it buzzes against your hip. Just once. A new order.

You look. It is the hand-dyed yarn, the deep teal one, the last skein of the batch. Lovely. Someone wants it.

Then the cold little drop in your stomach. You sold that skein on Friday. At a market. For cash. You remember handing it over in a paper bag.

The shop still said "1 in stock".

The trailers start and you don't see them

You sit down in row F. The screen goes dark. Your sister leans over and whispers something about the lead actor.

You nod. You are not there.

You are drafting the email in your head. Hi Maren, I'm so sorry, there has been a mix-up. No. Too stiff. Hi Maren, unfortunately. Worse. You have written this email before. Twice this autumn, if you are honest. Once for a candle, once for a notebook set.

Each time the customer was kind about it. That is almost the hardest part. A kind reply makes you feel smaller, not better.

Under the apology sits the other thought, the one you rarely say aloud: A real shop would know what it has.

What it quietly costs

It is never just the one cancelled order.

It is the Saturday you spent refreshing the shop admin instead of enjoying the market. It is the habit of counting shelves with your lips moving, then not trusting the count. It is the notebook in your bag, the spreadsheet on your laptop and the number in your head, all three saying something slightly different.

It is the small flinch every time a notification arrives. Orders are supposed to be good news. You have started to brace for them.

And it is the sale you did not make because you hid a product "just in case", when in fact you had six.

The film is ninety minutes of someone else's problems. You spend them rehearsing your own.

The foyer, afterwards

When the lights come up, your sister is laughing and wiping her eyes. You smile, because you did enjoy her laugh, if not the film.

Outside, by the bins and the bike rack, you finally type the apology. You offer a refund and a restock date you are not sure about. Then, because she is already waiting for you with two coffees, you stop.

She asks, "Bad news?"

"Sold something twice."

"Isn't that a good problem?"

You almost laugh. It is the kind of thing people say who have never had to write that email. Then she says the useful thing, without meaning to: "Don't you have one list that just says what's left?"

You do not. You have three lists, which is the same as none.

It doesn't have to be a guessing game

That is the turning point, and it is a small one. Nothing dramatic. Just a quiet sentence on a pavement: one list that says what is left.

You realise you have never actually tried to be organised about this. You have only tried to be careful. Careful is tiring. Careful means holding every skein, every candle and every jar in your head, all the time.

A simple system does not ask you to be more careful. It asks you to write things down in one place, once, and lets that place do the remembering. Including the unglamorous part: telling you when a thing is running low, while there is still time to do something about it.

A different Monday

Picture it, a few weeks on.

It is Monday morning, 8:40. The kettle is on. You open one file, not three. You look at the shelf column and it matches the shelf in your studio, because you updated it after Saturday's market, in about two minutes, standing up.

Three items are flagged as getting low. Not out. Low. You message your dye supplier before your coffee has cooled, and the email takes four lines instead of forty minutes of dread.

When an order notification arrives that afternoon, you glance at it and keep walking. You know the answer already. Yes, you have it.

The teal yarn is back on the shelf, with a small honest number next to it.

That is all the change is. Not a bigger business. Just a shop that stops surprising you.

And the next time you are in a dark cinema on a Sunday afternoon, the phone buzzes in your pocket and you let it. It is just an order. Good news again.
