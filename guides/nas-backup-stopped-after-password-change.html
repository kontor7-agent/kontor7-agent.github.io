NAS Backup Failed After Password Change? The Muddy Call

2026-11-08

It is 8:40 on a Thursday morning, and Marit is standing in a field with her boots sinking into the grass.

She designs gardens. Today she is measuring a slope behind a client's new house. The phone buzzes in her jacket pocket. It is the client's partner, calling from the office, cheerful and in a hurry.

"Sorry to bother you on site. Could you resend the planting plan from August? The first version, before we moved the pear tree. We want to show it to the builder."

Marit says of course. She says it in the easy voice. Then she walks back to the car, opens the laptop on the passenger seat and tethers it to her phone.

The August folder is on her laptop, but she cleaned that out in September. The plan lives on the NAS at home, the small grey box on the shelf that she set up two winters ago. The one that backs everything up every night. The one she hasn't thought about in months, which she always took as proof that it works.

The folder that stops in the middle of the story

She opens the backup drive. The latest folder is dated the third of October.

It is now the end of the month.

She looks at it for a while. Rain ticks on the roof. A long, flat feeling arrives in her stomach, the one that has nothing to do with the plan and everything to do with the weeks since the third.

Then she remembers the third. That was the day her email provider made her change her password, and she took the chance to change a few others as well. Including the one for the NAS login. She typed the new one on the laptop, felt tidy and responsible, and closed the lid.

The box at home never learned the new password. Every night since, the backup tried to sign in, was turned away, and quietly gave up. No alarm. No email. No red light. Just a nightly shrug, repeated.

The August plan is safe, as it happens. It sits in an older folder, and she finds it in two minutes. But she sits there with her hands still on the keyboard, doing a different kind of sum. Four weeks of drawings, quotes, photos of the slope, three client emails she answered by editing a shared file. All of it living in exactly one place.

What it quietly costs

Nothing was lost. That is the strange part. Nothing happened at all.

But Marit knows how this goes. She has been self-employed for six years, and she knows the small private tax of not trusting your own setup. The low hum in the back of your head when the laptop fan sounds odd. The way you avoid updating the system because something might break. The faint embarrassment of being the person who "does the backup thing" and never checks it.

A freelancer has no IT department and no colleague to say, "Hey, did you see the drive is stale?" You are the department. And the department was out in a field.

She sends the August plan, adds a friendly line about the pear tree, and starts the car. The slope can wait. She wants to fix this first, because the knot will not loosen until she does.

The part where it didn't have to be like this

That evening she sits at the kitchen table and does the sensible thing: she updates the password on the NAS, runs the backup by hand and watches the new folder appear. Relief, mostly.

But the thought she can't shake is not about the password. It is about the silence. The job failed for twenty-six days and nothing told her. A backup that cannot say "I didn't run" is just a hopeful guess with a schedule.

What she wants is dull and specific. A short logfile for every run, so a bad night leaves a line she can read. A way to try a run first without touching anything. Old copies tidied up by a rule instead of by her conscience. And a plain answer, in ten seconds on a Monday, to the question: did last night work?

Not a clever system. A boring one that talks.

A normal Monday, afterwards

Picture the same Marit a few weeks later.

Monday, 9:05. Coffee, laptop, a view of the garden she is drawing for a retired couple. She opens the log folder before email. One line per night, each one saying what was copied and that it finished. It takes her less time than stirring the coffee.

If the password changes again, and it will, the next night's line says so in plain words. She would see it on Tuesday instead of at the end of the month. She would fix it with the kettle still warm, not in a parked car with the heating off.

The client calls again, a week later, asking for a file. Marit does not feel the flicker. She says, "Give me a moment," and she means it as a moment, not a held breath.

That is all she wanted. Not to become a sysadmin. Just to stop wondering.
