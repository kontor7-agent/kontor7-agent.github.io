Faded Thermal Receipts, Self-Employed? The Sun Visor

2026-11-06

It is 12:40 on a Wednesday, and you are sitting in your parked car outside the bakery, holding a piece of paper up to the windscreen.

It used to be a receipt. Now it is a pale grey rectangle with a ghost of a logo in one corner.

You tilt it. You squint. You turn it over, as if the numbers might be hiding on the back.

Nothing.

The paper came off the sun visor, where you have kept it since the spring. That was the "I'll deal with it later" spot. Later turned out to be six months of sunshine through glass.

The stack behind the visor

You pull the whole bundle down. A fuel stop. A printer cartridge. Parking at the client's office. A lunch with a prospect, maybe. You can't say for sure, because that one has gone completely white.

Some are fine. Some are faint but readable if you hold them at just the right angle. Some are blank, like clean paper.

Your stomach does a small, familiar drop.

The thing nobody tells you when you start working for yourself is how much of it is paper. Small, flimsy, slightly greasy paper that fades if you look at it wrong. You are the finance department, the filing clerk and the person who forgot, all at once.

You think of the bank statement. It shows that money left your account on a certain day. It does not show what it was for, and you cannot remember every one.

So you do what you always do. You tell yourself it was probably only a few small ones, and you start the engine.

The thought you don't say out loud

It isn't really about the receipts. It is about what they mean.

A real business keeps its records. A real business knows what it spent in March. A real business does not rescue grey paper from behind a sun visor and hold it up to the light like a bank note.

That is the quiet voice, the one that says you are not quite a proper business yet. It never shouts. It just sits there in the passenger seat while you drive back to your desk.

And it costs you things. A free Saturday morning, spent in a pile of paper. A low hum of dread every time someone mentions the tax adviser's appointment. A tiny flinch when you buy something for work and think: where will I put this one?

None of it is dramatic. It is just heavy, in a way that never quite lifts.

What the afternoon looked like

Back at home you spread everything on the kitchen table. You sort by readable, half-readable and gone.

The gone pile is small. It is smaller than the dread suggested. But it is still there, and you notice you feel oddly embarrassed, as though someone were watching.

Nobody is. It is only you, a cold coffee and a stack of paper that was never designed to last.

Here is the thing you realise somewhere around the third receipt. The paper was never the system. It was only ever the starting point. The receipt is just a reminder that something was spent. The moment it matters is the day you spend it, not the day you need it.

If that moment has somewhere calm to land, the paper can fade all it likes.

What do the people who seem relaxed about this do differently? You suspect it is boring. A list. One place. A habit so small it hardly counts as a habit.

For whether a faded receipt is accepted for anything, you would ask your tax professional. That part is not yours to guess, and it is not ours to answer. What you can decide is how you want to treat the next receipt.

A different Wednesday

Picture the same bakery car park, a few months on.

You buy your lunch. You tuck the slip into your wallet, and that evening, or the next morning with your coffee, it takes thirty seconds to write it down. Date, amount, what it was for. Done. The paper can go in a drawer and fade in peace.

At the end of the month there is no pile. There is a page that already knows where the money went, and a total you did not have to dig for.

When the year ends and the appointment comes, you are not hunting. You are bringing a list. Your adviser can read it. You can read it too, which is quietly the best part.

The quiet voice in the passenger seat gets very little to say.

No one hands you a medal for this. It just feels like running a real business, which is exactly what you are doing.
