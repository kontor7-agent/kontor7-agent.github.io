Invoices and Timesheets Apart? The Stalled 14:10 Train

2026-10-19

The regional train has stopped between two stations, and nobody has said why.

It is 14:10 on a Friday. Mara, a freelance illustrator, has her laptop open on the fold-down tray. A coffee she regrets is wobbling beside it. The tax return is due soon, and she has told herself for three weeks that she will "pull it together on the train."

Now she has forty minutes of forced stillness and no excuses.

Mara is an illustrative character, but you may know her.

Two folders and a notebook

She opens the invoices folder first. Files called Rechnung_final, Rechnung_final2, and one just called neu. Some are PDFs. Some are Word documents she never exported. One is a photo of a paper note from a client she did a quick job for in spring.

Then she opens the timesheet. It is a spreadsheet she started in January with great energy. The columns are tidy until March. After that, the rows get shorter, the notes get vaguer, and in June there is a single line that says lots of revisions, approx.

She has a notebook too. Half the hours are only in there, in pencil.

She tries to line them up. This invoice, these hours, this client. But the invoice for the children's book client doesn't match any total she can find. Did she bill that week twice? Did she forget a week altogether? Was the payment from the publisher the 1,800 on the bank statement, or was that something else?

The train gives a small, apologetic jolt and stays where it is.

The knot that has nothing to do with the delay

Here is what nobody tells you about this moment. It isn't really about the numbers.

It's the feeling underneath: I run a business, and I can't answer a simple question about it. Which invoices are paid? Which are still open? How many hours did I actually work for whom?

Mara knows people who would hand this to a professional and shrug. She will too, eventually. But she also knows that whoever she hands it to will ask exactly those questions. And "it's all somewhere" is not an answer.

So she feels a little like an imposter. Not because she draws badly. Because the paperwork says hobby, and she knows it isn't.

The cost is quiet, and it doesn't show up as a line anywhere. It's the Sunday evening she spends hunting through folders. It's the client she doesn't chase because she can't be sure what they owe. It's the low hum of dread every time a deadline gets near.

The woman across the aisle

A woman sits across the aisle with a laptop of her own. She doesn't look stressed. When the train stops, she doesn't groan. She glances at her screen, types a line, and closes it.

Mara can't help asking, in the low voice you use on trains, whether she's a freelancer too.

"Translator," the woman says. "You look like you're doing taxes on a tray table."

Mara laughs, mostly out of embarrassment.

"I used to," the woman says. "Hours in one place, invoices in another, and me in the middle. Then I just put it all in one sheet. Hours go in, invoices come out of the same place, and it tells me what's open and what's paid. It's not clever. It's just all in one spot."

She shrugs, as if she had said something about her bus route.

This is a made-up conversation, in a made-up train, with no claims behind it. But the thought it plants is real, and it lands in Mara's head with surprising weight. It doesn't have to be spread across three places.

What a normal Monday could feel like

The train finally moves. Mara doesn't finish her return on the tray table. She closes the laptop and watches the fields slide by, and for once she isn't doing sums in her head.

Instead she imagines a Monday morning that is different.

She sits down with a coffee she actually wanted. She opens one file. At a glance she sees which invoices are open, which are overdue, and which are paid. She logs Friday's two hours in the same file, and the next invoice number is simply there, already counted up. The publisher's payment moves from open to paid with one change, and the overview quietly updates.

No hunting. No pencil notebook. No approx.

When her accountant or tax adviser asks, she can answer in a sentence. Not because she became a bookkeeper, but because the answer is sitting where she can see it.

Ten minutes, and she's done. The rest of Monday belongs to the actual work.

That's the whole change. It isn't dramatic. It's the difference between a drawer you dread opening and a shelf you can read at a glance.
