Three Interviews, Mixing Up Companies? The Visitor Badge

2026-10-31

The lobby smells of fresh coffee and new carpet. You are early, which is good. You are sitting on a low grey sofa with a visitor badge stuck to your jacket, which is also good.

The receptionist has just asked a simple question: "And who are you here to see?"

Your mouth opens. A name comes up, and it is the wrong one. It belongs to Thursday's company, or maybe Tuesday's. For one long second you can't tell which.

You say, "Sorry, give me a moment," and pull out your phone. You scroll through emails with subject lines like Next steps and Quick chat? and Following up on your application. They all look the same.

Three interviews, one blur

This is what a good week looks like when you've been searching for a while. Three invitations in five days. Last month you would have celebrated.

Now you are standing in a lobby with a pounding heart, trying to remember if this is the company with the open-plan office and the dog, or the one with the founder who writes long emails.

Imagine Tomasz, a product designer. He is illustrative, not a real person, but you may know him. He applied to about thirty places over six weeks. He kept track in three spots: a notes app, a half-finished spreadsheet, and the inbox itself, where he searched for company names and hoped for the best.

On Sunday evening he had a plan. He would sit down and prepare properly for all three. But Sunday evening had laundry in it, and a phone call with his mother, and a vague tiredness that had nothing to do with the day. He told himself Monday morning would do.

Monday morning arrived with a quiet panic. He opened his laptop and read the first invitation. Then the second. The details slid into each other like cards in a bad shuffle. Which one had asked for a portfolio walkthrough? Which one had mentioned the team of six? Who had he spoken to on the phone, and what had he promised to send afterwards?

The cost nobody puts on the calendar

It isn't the forgetting itself that stings. It's what it does to you.

You start to feel like someone who can't keep their own life straight. You rehearse a generic answer to "Why us?" because you can't recall what made each place different. You open a company's website ten minutes before the call, skim it, and hope.

And under all of that sits a small, unkind thought: if I can't even remember who they are, why would they remember me?

That thought is unfair. You're not careless. You're a person doing a hard job, searching for work, with no assistant and no system. Your memory was never meant to be a filing cabinet for thirty companies.

Back on the grey sofa, Tomasz found the right email. The name on the badge matched the name on the door. He said it out loud to the receptionist, a little too brightly, and she smiled and said, "Take a seat, they'll be right down."

The interview went fine. Not perfect, but fine. He still left with a feeling in his chest like he'd gotten away with something.

The turning point

On the walk back to the station, he did something small. He stopped at a bench, took out his phone, and made a list. Three rows. Company, role, date, who he'd spoken to, what they cared about, what he wanted to ask.

It took six minutes. The knot in his stomach loosened a little, the way it does when you finally write down the thing you've been carrying around in your head.

That was the moment he understood it didn't have to be like this. The problem was never his memory. It was that every detail lived in a different place, or nowhere at all.

He could have one place. One row per application, with a date for the next step and a note for what happened last time. A spot for the stories he wanted to tell, so he wasn't inventing them on the spot, and a short list of questions, so he wasn't scrambling for them in the lift.

Nothing fancy. Just a place where the week lives, so he doesn't have to.

A different Wednesday

Picture the same week, but calmer.

Wednesday morning. Coffee. You open one file and see today's interview at the top. The company name, the interviewer, the two things they said mattered most. Beside it, a couple of stories from your own work, already worded the way you like. Below it, a line you wrote after the first call: they seemed worried about deadlines.

You read it once. You close the laptop and go for a walk.

In the lobby, the receptionist asks who you're here to see. You say the name without thinking. You sit on the grey sofa, badge on your jacket, and your heart is beating a little faster than usual, which is simply the sound of caring about something.

The blur is gone. You walk into the room as yourself.

And afterwards, the follow-up doesn't slip. It's sitting there with a date next to it, waiting politely for you.
