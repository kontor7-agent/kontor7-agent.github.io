Minimum Freelance Hourly Rate & Day Rate: The Quote at 11 pm

2026-10-12

It's 11:07 pm. The kitchen is dark except for your laptop and the little light on the kettle you forgot to switch off.

The email is open. A new client, a friendly one, asks the question you have been dreading all evening: "What's your day rate?"

You type a number. You delete it. You type a slightly higher number, feel brave for about four seconds, and delete that one too. Then you open a second tab and search for what other people charge, as if the answer were hiding in someone else's portfolio.

The cursor blinks. The thought in your head is the same one it always is: If I say too much, they'll leave. If I say too little, I'll regret it for months.

The number that was never really a number

Here is the quiet truth about that moment. Your rate is not a price. It's a feeling dressed up as a price.

Maybe it started as what you earned at your last job, divided by something that felt right. Maybe a friend mentioned what they charge. Maybe it's simply the number that didn't make you flinch the first time you said it out loud, and you never changed it.

So it sits there, year after year, doing a job it was never designed for.

And nobody talks about what that costs. It's rarely one dramatic loss. It's small and spread out:

  • The project that took twice as long as you quoted, finished on a Saturday.
  • The week when you were fully booked and still felt oddly short at month's end.
  • The slightly too-quick "sure, that works" when a client pushed back, because you had no solid ground to stand on.
  • The low hum of feeling unprofessional, because you couldn't explain your own number if someone asked.

You can be good at what you do and still quietly subsidise your clients, one friendly discount at a time.

What the guess leaves out

The trouble with a guessed rate is everything it forgets.

It forgets the days you don't bill. The emails, the proposals that go nowhere, the admin, the invoice you chase, the afternoon you spend fixing a spreadsheet instead of doing the work. It forgets the holiday you want to take and the sick day you hope you won't need. It forgets the software, the laptop that will eventually die, and the tax bill that arrives like weather.

A job hides all of that from you. A salary simply shows up. Freelancing hands you every piece of it, and nobody gives you the manual.

So you guess low, because low feels safe. And low is exactly what keeps you at the kitchen table at 11 pm.

The small moment that changes the tone

Imagine a different evening. Not a dramatic one.

Say you sit down on a quiet afternoon with a coffee and, for once, no client waiting. You gather a handful of honest numbers: what you need to live on, what the business itself costs, how many days you can realistically bill, and how many you can't. Nothing fancy. Just the truth, written down in one place.

And something unexpected happens. The number that comes out is not scary. It's just a number, with reasons behind it. It has edges. You can point to it.

For the first time, you can see the floor. Not the price you'd love to charge, and not the price you're afraid to ask, but the minimum below which the work stops making sense for you.

That's the shift. You stop negotiating against a feeling and start standing on something solid.

A normal Monday, after

It's 9:40 on a Monday morning. A message arrives from a prospective client: "Could you send a quote for the project?"

You don't flinch. You don't open the second tab of other people's prices. You glance at your own number, the one you worked out on a calm day, and you write your reply while the coffee is still hot.

If they push back, you can say no to the discount, or offer a smaller scope instead. Both feel like choices rather than panic. Sometimes a client walks away, and that's fine. The work that stays is work that pays you properly.

The day rate isn't a secret anymore, and neither is the hourly one. When a small job comes in, you know what it should cost. When a big one arrives, you know where your lowest line sits.

By 5:30 pm the laptop is closed. The evening belongs to you, and the kettle gets switched off on time.

Nothing about your skill changed. You just stopped guessing.

And then, the invoice that matches

There's a second, smaller relief tucked inside the first. Once your number feels earned, sending the invoice stops feeling awkward. The amount looks like what it is. The total is clear, the tax line is where it belongs, the due date sits right there in plain sight.

No apologetic cover note, no "whenever you get a chance." Just a tidy page that says: this is the work, this is the amount, this is when it's due.
