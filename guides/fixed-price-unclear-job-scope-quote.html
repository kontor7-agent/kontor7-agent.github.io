Fixed Price, Unclear Scope? The Kitchen Doorframe Pause

2026-11-04

It's 8:40 on a Tuesday morning, and you're standing in someone else's kitchen with a tape measure in one hand and a mug of tea you didn't ask for in the other.

The homeowner leans on the doorframe. Friendly. Hopeful. "So, can you just give me one price? Fixed. I don't want any surprises."

The job sounds simple. Move a socket, add two lights, tidy up the old wiring behind the cupboards. But the wall is plaster over something you can't see yet. The old fuse board is a question mark. You don't know if the cable runs through the ceiling or around the back.

You know what you'd like to say: I can't tell you yet.

What comes out is: "Let me work something out and send it over today."

The number you carry out to the van

In the van, you sit with the engine off. The tea is still warm in your memory, and your head is already running sums.

Three days? Four? Is the cable accessible? What if the fuse board needs more than a tidy-up? What if the materials jump again before you order?

You think of the last fixed price you gave. You said it kindly and quickly, because the customer was standing right there, and because saying "it depends" felt like admitting you weren't sure. Two weeks later you were on your knees behind a cupboard doing unpaid hours, smiling, saying "no problem at all."

Nobody saw that part. Not the customer, not your family. You just quietly worked for less than you meant to, and told yourself it was good for business.

This is the worry nobody says out loud on a job like this. It isn't that you can't do the work. It's that the price has to be said before you know the work. And a price said too low doesn't feel like a mistake. It feels like your own fault.

The two bad options

So you do what most people do. You round up and hope.

Or you round down, because you want the job, and because a number that's too high might send them to someone cheaper. You can almost hear the polite "we'll think about it," and then the silence.

Either way you're guessing, and guessing alone in a van is lonely. The customer wants certainty. You want to be fair, to them and to yourself. Somewhere between those two wishes, you lose a bit of sleep and a bit of confidence.

The real cost isn't only the occasional lost margin. It's the way every new enquiry starts to feel like a small test you might fail.

The pause that changes the call

Now imagine the same kitchen, a different morning.

The homeowner says it again: "Can you just give me one price?"

You don't flinch. You've already measured, noted what you can see, and written down what you can't. You smile and say something like:

"Here's what I can price firmly today, and here's the part behind the wall that I'll confirm once I've opened it up. You'll know where we stand either way."

There's no apology in your voice. It's the voice of someone who has done this before and knows how to say it.

The homeowner nods. They don't want a surprise. They want to feel safe. A clear structure gives them that more honestly than a number pulled from the air.

What made the difference isn't cleverness. It's that your labour, your materials and your own margin were already sitting in front of you, in one calm place, before you opened your mouth. You weren't guessing in the hallway. You were reading.

What a normal Tuesday feels like then

You drive home, or to the next job, with the radio on, and nothing is chewing at the back of your head.

The quote goes out before lunch, clean and plain. The materials you buy are noted as you go, not stuffed in the glove box until some future panic. When the job ends, you can see what it actually earned, not what you hoped it earned.

And when a customer pays, you know. When they haven't, you know that too, without opening four folders and a notebook.

The difference isn't dramatic. It's quiet. You stop dreading the first sentence of the next enquiry. A fixed price becomes something you choose to offer, rather than something that's dragged out of you in a doorway.

Your evenings stay yours. Your Fridays stop feeling like a trial. You're allowed to be a professional who says "here's what's firm and here's what isn't", and nobody thinks less of you for it.
