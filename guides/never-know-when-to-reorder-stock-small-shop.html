I Never Know When to Reorder Stock: The Bus Stop at 7:52

2026-10-14

The bus is four minutes late, and you are standing in the drizzle with a coffee going lukewarm in your hand.

Your phone buzzes. A customer, someone you have never met, asks: Hi! Will the dusk grey be back in stock soon? I'd love enough for a cardigan.

You read it twice. Then you do the thing you always do. You open the shop on your phone and scroll to the grey. It says Sold out. It has said that for a while, and you didn't know.

That is the part that makes your stomach dip. You didn't decide to let it run out. It just happened, quietly, somewhere between a busy weekend and a week of packing parcels.

The answer you don't have

You type: Soon! Then you delete it, because soon is not true. Your dyer needs about three weeks. You haven't even ordered yet.

You try: I'm so sorry, I'm not sure. That sounds like a shop that doesn't know its own shelves. You delete that too.

The bus arrives. You find a seat by the window and watch the wet street slide past, and you think the thought you have had many times and told nobody: I should know this. It's my shop.

You do know a lot. You know which colours people fall for. You know the little notes that make a parcel feel like a gift. What you don't know is the number, the point at which "still plenty" turns into "order now, or there will be a gap."

So you reorder by feeling. Sometimes you reorder too early, and a shelf of mustard yellow sits there for months, with money tied up in wool nobody asked for. Sometimes you reorder too late, and a customer who wanted the grey buys from somebody else.

Neither mistake is big. But they add up to a low hum of worry that comes along to dinner and sits next to you on the sofa.

What the hum costs

It costs small things. You open the stock cupboard "just to check" on a Sunday afternoon that was meant to be free. You run a quick count in your head while a friend is telling you about her holiday. You skip a sensible-looking offer from a supplier because you can't tell whether you'd sell it all.

It also costs a little of the feeling that this is a real business. Real businesses, you tell yourself, know what is on their shelves. They have someone in a back office with a clipboard. You have a notebook, a half-remembered number, and a feeling about the grey.

By the time you reach your workroom, the reply is still unsent. You put your bag down, switch on the kettle, and stare at the shelves.

The line in the notebook

That afternoon you do something slightly different. You don't tidy the shelves or reorder in a panic. You sit with your notebook and ask a calmer question than how much is left?

You ask: How long does it take to get more, and how fast does this actually sell?

It is a small shift, and it is surprisingly kind. The question is no longer a verdict on whether you are a good shop owner. It is just a piece of arithmetic that two ordinary facts can answer. How many go out in a week. How many weeks until the next delivery. Put those side by side and the "when" stops being a mood and starts being a line on a page.

You realise, with a mix of relief and mild irritation, that you've been carrying around something that never needed to live in your head. It could live somewhere you can see it. A place that taps you on the shoulder before the shelf is empty, instead of a customer doing it after.

A Monday that feels different

Picture the same shop a few weeks on.

It is Monday morning, a bit grey, the kind of morning where the radiator ticks. You open one sheet with your coffee. Most of it is calm. A few items are marked as order soon, and you can see why. Nothing is shouting at you. There is no pile of numbers to decode.

You send one email to your dyer: the grey, the mustard in a smaller batch this time, and the green that has been quietly selling. It takes six minutes. You close the laptop.

A customer writes asking about the dusk grey. This time you answer in a sentence that sounds like the person you actually are: It's on its way, and I'll have it back in about two weeks. I'm happy to let you know the moment it lands.

Nobody is let down. No apology. And you are on time for the bus.

The hum doesn't vanish forever, because a shop is a living thing. But it gets quieter. The shelf stops surprising you, and your Sundays start to belong to you again.
