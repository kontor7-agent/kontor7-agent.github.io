Asked to Sign Off API Security? The Empty Release Ticket

2026-10-27

It is 11:20 on a Wednesday, and the office kitchen smells of someone else's microwave porridge.

Jonas stands by the kettle with his phone in his hand. The release ticket is open. Under Security sign-off there is one new word, typed by his lead twenty minutes ago: Jonas.

No comment. No checklist. Just a name and a smiley face.

He has been a developer for eight months. He knows how to build an endpoint. He does not know what it means to put his name next to the sentence this is safe.

The kettle clicks off. He doesn't pour anything.

The word that sounds so small

Sign off. Two words. They carry a lot.

In his head, the conversation is already running. What if I tick it and something is open? What if I ask too many questions and look like I can't do the job? What if the thing I miss is the obvious thing, and everyone sees it afterwards?

His lead is on a train to a family wedding tomorrow. The client demo is Friday. The ticket is Jonas's.

He walks back to his desk and does what many of us do. He opens six browser tabs. One is a long security guide. One is a forum thread from 2019 that ends in an argument. One is a list titled "Top 10" that he suspects should be "Top 40". He reads, nods, scrolls, and feels less sure with every paragraph.

At 13:00 he eats a sandwich without tasting it.

What the worry quietly costs

Nobody puts this in the sprint review. The cost is small and constant.

It is the half-attention in the stand-up. It is the way you reread the same route file three times because nothing in it looks wrong, and that is exactly what bothers you. It is the thought that arrives in the shower on Thursday: did I even look at the password reset?

It is also a quiet shame. You wonder whether real developers just know, whether there is a feeling you are supposed to have that tells you it's fine.

There isn't. Experienced people don't carry that feeling around. They carry a list.

The sentence that changed the afternoon

At 15:40, Jonas does something small. He messages a friend who works on the platform side of another company, someone he's known since his course.

He expects a lecture. What he gets is three lines:

"Nobody does this from memory. Don't sign off a feeling. Sign off a list you worked through, and write down what you didn't check."

He reads it twice.

Sign off a list. Write down what you didn't check.

Something in his shoulders drops half an inch. The job he was afraid of was be certain about everything. The job in front of him is different. It is be careful, in order, and honest about the edges. That one he can do.

A different Thursday

Imagine the version where the ticket doesn't scare him.

It is Thursday, 10:15. Jonas has a plain list open next to his code. It is not a wall of text. It has items he can work through one at a time, each with a clear checked, not checked or needs a fix. Beside it sits a short note he can hand over without feeling like he is confessing.

He finds one real problem, a route that should have been protected and wasn't. It is the kind of thing that, by Friday evening, would have been a very different conversation. But it is a Thursday-morning fix now, not a Monday-morning apology.

When his lead texts from the train, how's the sign-off going?, he doesn't answer with "I think it's fine?"

He answers with what he checked, what he fixed, and the two things he flagged for a second pair of eyes.

Friday's demo is a demo. He is in the room, listening to the client, and not rehearsing in his head what might be open on the other side of the screen.

That is the whole difference. The API did not become magically perfect. He stopped carrying it alone.
