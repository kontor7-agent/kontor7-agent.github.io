Said It's in Stock When It Isn't? The Rowing Machine

2026-10-24

It's 6:50 on a Thursday morning, and Jonas is on the rowing machine at the gym. He isn't thinking about rowing.

His phone is propped on the little shelf in front of him. A message from a customer has just lit up the screen: Hi! Just checking my chalk bag order is still on track for the weekend trip?

He keeps pulling. His stomach has already turned over.

Two days ago he told this customer, warmly and with confidence, that yes, the green one was in stock. He'd looked at the shelf in his spare room, seen a few bags, and said yes. What he hadn't registered was that three of those were already promised to other orders. Or that the last one had a torn seam he'd set aside.

Now the machine counts down the metres, and Jonas counts something else. How many bags are really on that shelf? He doesn't know. He thinks he knows. That is the problem.

The sentence you said too quickly

You know this one. Someone asks, "Do you have it?" and you answer from memory, because answering from memory is fast and it feels like being on top of things.

Most days it works. Then one day it doesn't.

What stings isn't the stock. It's the sentence. Yes, it's available. You said it with a smile, and now you have to take it back.

You rehearse the apology in the shower. You open the email draft and close it again. You wonder whether the customer will quietly decide you're the kind of shop that can't be trusted. You aren't, but the knot doesn't care.

What it quietly costs

The refund is the small part. The bigger cost is the low hum underneath your week.

You hesitate before saying yes to anyone. You check the shelf twice, then a third time. A message arrives and your first feeling is dread instead of pleasure that someone wants your thing.

You also start to feel a little unprofessional. Other shops seem to know what they have. You have a spreadsheet from last spring, a notebook by the packing table, and a rough picture in your head that goes slightly wrong a little more every week.

Nobody warns you that this is what "small business" feels like from the inside. It isn't the big decisions that wear you down. It's the hundred tiny I think sos.

The reply he didn't want to write

Back in the changing room, Jonas sits on the bench with a towel round his neck and types the honest reply.

Hi, I'm so sorry. I told you it was in stock and I should have checked properly. The bag you ordered has a flaw, so I'd rather not send it. I can offer the blue one this weekend, or a full refund. Your choice, and again, I'm sorry.

He reads it once. It's kind, and it's true. It's also the third message like it this season.

The customer replies within ten minutes. No worries, thanks for telling me. Blue is fine!

The relief lasts about a minute. Then Jonas notices that the problem isn't the customer. The customer was lovely. The problem is that he'll stand in this same spot again, unless something changes about how he knows what's on that shelf.

The moment it clicked

On the walk home he doesn't think about systems or software. He thinks about something smaller.

What if the answer to Do you have it? were something he could just see, instead of something he had to remember?

Not a clever dashboard. Just one calm place that says: this many left, this many promised, and this one is running low, so deal with it before someone asks.

That's all he wants. He wants the shelf and the number to agree, and he wants to be told early instead of finding out late.

A different Thursday

Picture the same morning, a few weeks on.

The phone lights up on the rowing machine shelf. Is the green chalk bag available?

Jonas finishes his set and glances at the screen. He doesn't dig through memory or run a mental stocktake. He opens one sheet and sees it: two left, one already reserved, and a small note that says it's time to reorder.

Yes, there's one left, and I'll hold it for you. I'm restocking this week too.

That's the whole exchange. It takes ten seconds. His shoulders stay where they are.

On Monday he spends a few minutes with the numbers over a cup of tea, updates what sold, and sees what's running low. Then he closes the laptop. The apology emails are gone from his week, and so is the creeping feeling of being a little bit of a fraud.

The shop is the same size and the shelf is the same shelf. The only difference is that he knows, and knowing makes his yes sound like it means something.

If you recognised yourself

Maybe you've also said "yes, it's in stock" a moment too fast. Maybe you've had a Thursday like this one, with a phone buzzing and a stomach dropping.

It doesn't mean you're bad at this. It means your shop outgrew your memory, which is what happens when things go well.
