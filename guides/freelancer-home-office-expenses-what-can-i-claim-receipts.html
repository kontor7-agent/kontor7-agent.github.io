Not Sure What Home Office Costs You Can Claim? The Lamp

2026-11-05

It's 14:10 on a Tuesday. Jonas has twenty minutes between two calls and a cold coffee beside the laptop.

He is a freelance copywriter. His office is the corner of the bedroom, behind a bookshelf he uses as a room divider. He looks up from the screen and, for no reason, starts counting what's around him.

The desk lamp. The chair his back finally stopped complaining about. The second monitor. The router blinking on the shelf. The headset he bought in March because a client said his audio sounded like a tunnel.

Then a thought arrives, quiet and unwelcome: Which of this is business? Which of it can I even put down?

He doesn't know. He realises he has never known.

The question you keep postponing

You might not be a copywriter. You might be a designer, a coach, a developer, a photographer who edits at the kitchen table. The scene is the same.

Your home is also your workplace. The line between "this is for the business" and "this is just my life" runs straight through your living room, and nobody ever drew it for you.

So you do what most people do. You keep some receipts. You lose others. You tell yourself you'll look it up properly later, when things are calmer.

Things are never calmer. The question just sits there, a small stone in your shoe.

What it quietly costs

The cost is not dramatic. It is small and constant.

It's the paper bag in the hallway drawer, the one with a printer cartridge receipt, a parking slip and something from a hardware shop you can't place. It's the phone photo of a receipt that you're sure is somewhere in your camera roll, between a sunset and a screenshot of a recipe.

It's the faint embarrassment when someone asks, "Do you keep track of all that?" and you say, "Yes, more or less," in the voice of a person who means no.

And it's the feeling that other freelancers have a system. A real business would know what its chair cost. A real business would know where the router bill went.

Jonas's call at 14:30 is with a client he likes. He still spends the first five minutes only half listening. Part of his head is in the drawer in the hallway.

The January that always arrives

He knows how it goes. He's done it before.

It's the first weekend of January. The heating is on, the festive leftovers are going stale in the fridge, and he is on the floor with a shoebox, a pile of bank statements and the particular silence of someone adding things up and not liking the sum.

He finds a receipt with the ink faded to nothing. He finds two for the same thing. He finds a purchase he can't explain, and one he's certain he made but cannot prove.

By Sunday evening he's tired in a way that has nothing to do with work. He has spent two days in a year he can no longer see clearly.

None of this makes him a bad freelancer. It makes him someone who has been doing the admin in one frantic push, once a year, with a tired brain.

The moment it shifts

The shift for Jonas isn't a big one. It happens on a Thursday, while he's waiting for a file to upload.

He thinks: What if the question were smaller?

Not "what can I claim this year?" That's a huge, foggy question, and the honest answer is that it depends on where you live and your situation. A tax professional is the right person for it, and no spreadsheet should pretend otherwise.

But there's a different, smaller question that only he can answer, and he can answer it in about thirty seconds: What did I buy, when, and what was it for?

That's it. A date, a shop, an amount, a note. A place to put the slip the moment it appears, so it never goes into the drawer.

If the record exists, the big question stops being scary. He can walk into his accountant's office, or open a tax form, with something solid in his hands. The decisions stay his and his adviser's. The facts are simply sitting there, in order.

A normal Tuesday, afterwards

Picture the same Tuesday, 14:10, the same corner behind the bookshelf.

The lamp is still there. So is the chair, the router, the headset. But when the courier drops off a new cable, Jonas takes the slip, types four short entries and closes the file. It takes less time than the coffee takes to cool.

He has a short list of year-end jobs, and a few are already ticked. He can see which deadlines are close and which are far away. A small bar shows how much of the tidying is done, and the number goes up a little each month.

When the 14:30 call starts, his head is in the call. Nothing is waiting for him in the hallway drawer.

In January, there is no floor, no shoebox, no lost weekend. There's an ordinary Saturday, and perhaps a walk.
