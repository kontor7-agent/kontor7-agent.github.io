Rate Too Low for Taxes and Holidays? The Museum Café

2026-10-17

It is raining on a Wednesday, and you are in the museum café because your friend Lena had a free afternoon and you did too. That is the thing about working for yourself. You always have a free afternoon. You just never feel allowed to enjoy it.

She stirs her tea and says it casually, the way you mention the weather: "I've still got eleven days of leave to take before December. I think I'll just tack them onto Christmas."

You smile. You nod. Inside, something small and cold turns over.

Eleven days of leave. Paid. Nobody sends her an invoice for them. They simply exist, like the heating in her office.

The sum you never quite finished

You are a freelance illustrator, three years in. Your hourly rate is a number you chose one evening because it felt reasonable and because it was a little higher than the last number. You have never been able to say why it is that number.

On the walk past the dinosaur skeleton, while Lena talks about her new flat, the questions come up one by one.

Does that rate pay for the days you are ill? Does it pay for the week in August when nobody answers email? Does it cover the tax bill that arrives later, the one you try not to picture too clearly? And what about the hours you never invoice at all: the emails, the quotes, the Thursday you spent chasing a file?

You are not sure. That is the uncomfortable part. You are not sure, and you have been working as if you were.

What it quietly costs

Nothing has gone wrong yet. That is why it is so easy to ignore.

But you notice how it shows up in small ways. You take a Friday off and feel guilty by lunchtime. You say yes to a project that pays a bit too little because a slow month is a frightening thought. When money lands in your account, you feel rich for about a day, then you remember that it has to cover things you have not named yet.

You feel like someone who is earning, but not quite someone who is running a business. A real business knows what it needs to bring in. You know what you hope to bring in.

Lena asks, "Are you okay? You went quiet."

"Just tired," you say. Which is true, but it is not the whole of it.

The turning point, on a tram

On the ride home you sit by the window with your bag on your knee, and the thought arrives, quietly: maybe this is not a feeling problem. Maybe it is just a question nobody ever walked me through.

You are not bad with money. You were simply never shown how your rate is built. Nobody taught you that a freelance hour has to carry more than the hour itself. It has to carry the sick days, the slow weeks, the time off, the admin, the tax set aside, and the unbilled time in between. That load is spread across fewer billable hours than you would guess.

You do not need to become an accountant. (And a calculator is not a tax professional, so you would still check the details with one.) You just need to see the shape of it, once, in plain numbers, with your own life in them.

That evening, you open a blank sheet and write down three things: the weeks you would like off, the monthly amount you need to live on, and the share you want to put aside for tax. It takes less than ten minutes. It is the first time the question has a place to sit other than your stomach.

A different Monday

Picture a Monday a few weeks later.

You make coffee. You open the inbox and there is a new enquiry: a small brand, a set of illustrations, a polite request for "your rates."

The old you would have typed a number, deleted it, typed a lower one, and hit send with your jaw tight.

This time you already know your minimum. Not a hope, not a guess, but a floor. It has your holidays in it. It has your tax set-aside in it. It even has the unbilled hours, the ones that used to vanish. So you write your reply in a calm voice, because the number is not a plea anymore. It is just a fact about your business.

You send it. Then you close the laptop, and you do not feel the need to check for a response in the next ten minutes.

Maybe the client says yes. Maybe they ask for a discount, and you know exactly how far you can bend and where you cannot. Either way, the knot is gone. When you take your week off in August, you take the whole week. It was already paid for, in the rate.
