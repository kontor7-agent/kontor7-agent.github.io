Client Ignoring Your Follow-Up Email? What to Say Next

2026-10-19

The kayak hut, Saturday, 11:10

You are standing in a queue of people in life jackets, holding two paddles that aren't yours. Your sister's birthday trip. Sun on the water, someone laughing behind you.

Your phone is in your other hand, because of course it is.

The email thread is open. Your first message: friendly, clear. Your second, a week later: "Just a quick nudge!" Your third, four days ago: "Hope you're well, circling back on this." Under each one, the same thing. Nothing.

You are an illustrator, and let's call you Mira. The client has the final artwork. The approval is still pending. And so is the money that depends on it.

You know the next message should be a fourth. You just don't know what it should say.

The draft you keep deleting

The queue shuffles forward. You start typing.

"Hi Jonas, I wanted to follow up again..." Too needy. Delete.

"As per my previous emails..." Too sharp. You'd never say that out loud. Delete.

"No worries if you're busy!" But you do worry. That's the whole problem. Delete.

You notice you've read the same line five times without hearing the instructor explain where to put your feet.

There's a particular kind of silence that comes from a client who stops answering. It isn't loud. It just leaks into everything. Into the walk to the water. Into the photos where you're smiling but running a different conversation in your head: Did I say something wrong? Did they hate it? Are they just busy? Are they avoiding me? Will I have to chase this for a month?

And underneath, the quiet one: Maybe a real professional wouldn't have to ask four times.

What the silence costs

Nobody puts this on an invoice, but it costs something.

It costs the afternoon you can't fully start the next project, because this one might come back with changes. It costs the Sunday evening dread. It costs the little flinch every time your inbox pings and it isn't them.

It also costs you tone. The longer the silence runs, the more every new message sounds either apologetic or annoyed, and neither is who you actually are. You are someone who does good work, on time, and would simply like a clear answer.

Your sister waves from the dock. "Mira! You're next!"

You lock the phone and slide it into the dry bag. The fourth email can wait twenty minutes. It's been waiting for two weeks already.

Out on the water

Here is the odd thing about paddling out to the middle of a lake: it gives you distance. Not from the problem, but from the feeling around it.

Floating there, with your paddle across your knees, you think about what you'd say to a friend in your position. You wouldn't tell her to apologise. You wouldn't tell her to fire off something icy either. You'd say: be warm, be short, make it easy for them to answer. A person who has gone quiet usually isn't plotting anything. They're buried, or embarrassed, or the email slid under forty others.

What would help them is not a bigger apology. It's a message that is clear about what you need, kind about the silence, and simple enough to answer in ten seconds.

The trouble was never that you didn't know this. It's that on a Saturday, with sunscreen in your eyes and a knot in your stomach, you can't find the words fast enough. So you either send nothing or send something you regret.

The turning point on the pontoon

Back on the pontoon, dripping, you have a small realisation.

You have written this email before. Not this exact one, but its cousins: the nudge, the second nudge, the "friendly" final reminder, the polite way to say a deadline has now become a real deadline. Each time you started from a blank box and your own nerves.

What if the awkward part wasn't a fresh problem every time? What if you had the wording ready, in a few different tones, with the blanks marked where the client's name and the project go? Then the question wouldn't be "how do I say this without sounding desperate?" It would just be "which version feels like me today?"

That's a different afternoon altogether.

A different Monday

Picture the next one.

It's 9:05. Coffee. You open the thread, and instead of the old cold dread, you pick a message that already sounds like you: calm, specific, kind. You change the name, add the project title, read it once, and send it. Done before the kettle clicks off.

You don't spend the morning wondering whether you were too soft. You don't rehearse it in the shower. The reply may come that afternoon or next week, but either way you've said the clear thing in a human voice, and you can get on with drawing.

That's all. Not a trick, not a magic sentence that forces anyone to answer. Just the relief of not facing the blank box alone, again.
