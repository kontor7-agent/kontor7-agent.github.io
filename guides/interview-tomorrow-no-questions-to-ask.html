Interview Tomorrow, No Questions to Ask? The Risotto Pan

2026-10-30

Jonas stands at the stove at 18:15, stirring risotto with one hand. His phone leans against the spice rack, open to a note titled Questions for tomorrow.

The note is empty.

He has the shirt ready. He knows the company's name, the team's name, even the name of the person who will interview him. He has rehearsed "tell me about yourself" in the shower, twice. But at the end of every interview comes that sentence: "So, do you have any questions for us?" And his mind produces nothing. Just a bright, humming blank.

He types: What is the culture like? Deletes it. Everyone asks that. He types: What are the next steps? That one feels like asking for the bill before dessert.

The rice sticks a little. He adds stock and keeps stirring.

The question behind the question

It's strange, how much this small moment weighs. You can survive a hard question about your weaknesses. You've prepared for that. But the moment they hand the conversation back to you, it feels as if the whole interview is being judged on one thing: do you sound like someone who has thought about this?

And the quiet fear underneath: what if I ask something that shows I haven't?

So you do what most of us do. You search for "good questions to ask in an interview" and get a list of thirty. They all sound polished and all slightly the same. You read them until they blur. None of them sounds like you, and none of them connects to this job, this team, this particular posting you read three weeks ago and can barely remember now.

That's the part nobody mentions. The problem isn't that there are no questions. It's that the job ad, the notes from the first call, and the thing the recruiter said about the team are all scattered. One is in an email. One is in a browser tab you closed. One is a half-sentence in your head from Tuesday.

Jonas puts the spoon down and scrolls through his inbox. He applied to eleven places this month. Which one was this again? Ah, the one with the flat team and the product that ships every two weeks. He remembers the recruiter saying something about a "new lead starting in spring." What was that exactly? He can't find it.

The plate gets cold

He eats standing up, still scrolling. By 19:40 the note has three lines. Two of them are crossed out in his mind before he's finished typing them.

Here is what this costs, in small coins. An hour of a Sunday evening. The appetite. A low buzz of dread that follows you to bed and sits on your chest at 3 a.m. And tomorrow, in the room, a slightly rushed, apologetic "No, I think you've covered everything", which is the one answer that tells them nothing about you.

It isn't a character flaw. You were never taught to build this. Nobody hands you a place where the job, the people you spoke to, and your own stories live together.

The other version of the evening

Imagine the same Sunday, slightly different.

There is one place where the application sits. The date you applied. The name of the recruiter. The two things she mentioned on the phone. Next to it, a short list of questions that grew over the past week, one at a time, each time you read something about the company and thought hm, I wonder. Not thirty. Five or six, and they're yours.

There's also a short list of stories ready for the harder questions. A moment you handled a difficult situation. A time something went wrong and you fixed it. You wrote them down when you had a quiet minute, so tonight you don't have to invent them from nothing.

You read it all once, over a cup of tea. It takes twenty minutes, not two hours. Then you close the laptop.

Nothing in this version is clever. It's simply that the information was gathered before the night before. The questions don't have to be brilliant. They have to be real, and real ones come from having your notes in front of you.

Tomorrow, 11:30

In that version, tomorrow goes like this. The interviewer leans back and says, "What would you like to ask us?" You look at your small page and say, "You mentioned a new team lead is starting in spring. How do you imagine that changing how the team works?"

She pauses. Smiles. Says, "That's a good question." And the conversation turns into something that sounds like two colleagues talking, not a test.

You walk out into the daylight with your shoulders lower than they were this morning.

Whether you get the job is up to a hundred things you don't control. But you were there, fully, instead of fighting a blank.

Back at the stove

Jonas rinses the pan. The note on his phone still has three lines. He's tired, and there's still tonight.

But he stops, and does something small: he opens a fresh page and writes down only what he knows about this job. The recruiter's name. The flat team. The two-week releases. The new lead in spring. Four facts.

The questions arrive on their own, one after another, like they'd been waiting for the facts to show up first.
