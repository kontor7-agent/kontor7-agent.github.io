Forgot Travel Time in Your Quote? The Ring Road at 7:55

2026-10-17

The ring road is at a standstill, and you are in the third lane with a van full of tools and a coffee that went cold at the last roundabout.

The satnav says 42 minutes. You thought it was twenty.

This is the fourth trip to the Hartmann house this month. A garden gate, a bit of fencing, a patch of paving. You quoted it on the doorstep three weeks ago, standing on the lawn with Mrs Hartmann asking if Thursday would suit. You said a number. You were pleased with it. It sounded fair and it sounded like a yes.

Now you sit with the engine ticking and the thought arrives, quietly, the way the worst ones do: Did I put the driving in?

The number you said out loud

You know you didn't.

You counted the timber, the posts, the concrete. You counted your own hours with the spade and the saw. You rounded it nicely. But nobody stands on a lawn and adds up forty minutes each way, four times, plus the detour to the builders' merchant because they were out of the right brackets.

That is more than five hours of your week. None of it is on the quote. None of it can be.

The awkward part is that the job itself is going fine. The gate swings well. Mrs Hartmann is lovely. She brought you a biscuit on Tuesday. And you feel a small, mean resentment toward a woman who has done nothing wrong, because you can feel the money leaking out of a job she thinks is going well.

What it quietly costs

It isn't only the money, although the money is real.

It is the way you start doing the sums in the van, with the radio off. It is the evening you spend half-listening to your partner because your head is still on the ring road. It is the Sunday feeling that you worked all week and the account balance doesn't quite agree.

And it is the small shame of it. You have done this for years. You know your trade. Yet here you are, a person who forgot the one thing that happens before every single job: getting there.

You don't tell anyone. Who would you tell? Telling a customer feels like complaining. Telling a friend feels like admitting you're not a proper business. So you chew on it alone, somewhere between the exit for the retail park and the one for the hospital.

The call that doesn't have to happen

At 8:20 you are finally outside the Hartmann house. You switch the engine off and sit for a second.

You consider ringing her. Mrs Hartmann, the quote didn't cover my travel. You hear how it would sound. You close the idea like a drawer.

So you work. You hang the gate, check the latch, tidy the offcuts into the van. It is a good job, properly done, which is the only part you control today.

On the drive home, in lighter traffic, something shifts. Not a big idea. Just a small, plain thought.

The mistake wasn't the doorstep. It was that there was nowhere for the driving to go.

You did not forget because you are careless. You forgot because the quote lived in your head, and your head, on a lawn, with a friendly customer waiting, is a poor place to keep a list. Travel, materials, the second trip, your own time. All of it needs somewhere to sit where you can see it before you open your mouth.

A different Monday

Picture the next one.

It is Monday, a little after eight. You are on a doorstep in a different part of town, and a woman asks what a new bathroom shelf and a bit of re-tiling would cost.

You don't guess. You say, "Give me a moment," and you take out your phone or the laptop from the van. The job goes in. The materials go in. The drive there, and the drive back, are already part of it, because they have a line of their own and you are no longer relying on remembering.

The number that comes out is a little higher than the one you would have said from memory. You say it calmly. She says, "That sounds fair."

No apology in your voice. No rounding down to be liked.

Later, at the builders' merchant, you keep the receipt. Not in the glove box, where receipts go to disappear, but in the place it belongs. On Friday, you look at the week and you can see what each job actually left you with. Not what you hoped. What you got.

And you close the laptop at six.

If you recognise yourself

Maybe you are not on a ring road. Maybe you are a cleaner who drives across the city, a gardener with three sites a day, an electrician whose "quick call-out" is never quick. The scene changes. The feeling is the same: a price that was said too fast, and a quiet bill that arrives afterwards.

It doesn't mean you're bad at this. It usually means you've been doing it all in your head.
