Client Can't Pay the Invoice at Once? The Sandcastle Call

2026-11-04

The bucket is half full of wet sand when your phone buzzes on the towel.

It's Thursday, 11:40. You told yourself this was a day off. Your nephew is building a fortress, your sister is unwrapping sandwiches, and the sea is doing its slow, ordinary thing.

The notification says: Re: Invoice 0147.

You tell yourself you'll read it later. You read it now.

Hi, thank you for the lovely work. I have to be honest, we can't pay the full amount at once right now. Would it be possible to pay in instalments? So sorry about this.

The sand is warm. Your stomach is not.

The two answers you can't stand

Let's call you Ines. You design websites for small businesses, and 0147 is the biggest invoice you sent this spring. It was due two weeks ago. You already wrote one reminder. You deleted the first version because it sounded sharp, and the second because it sounded like an apology.

Now this.

The client isn't rude. That's the trouble. A rude client you could be firm with. This one is polite and embarrassed, and clearly telling the truth about something. It makes you feel like a bank and a bad friend at the same time.

Your head offers you two answers.

Yes, of course, no problem! And then you're the person who agreed to something vague, with no dates, hoping it works out.

No, the invoice is due. And then you're the person who said no to someone who just asked honestly.

Neither feels like you. You sit there with the phone, a crust of sandwich in your hand, while a seagull inspects your bag.

What it was quietly costing

It isn't only this invoice. It's the pattern around it.

You know money is late somewhere, but you couldn't say exactly where without opening three files. You have a feeling about who owes you, not a list. So when someone says "can we split it?", you answer from the feeling. And the feeling says: don't make it awkward.

Awkward has been expensive. A rent date that crept closer while you waited politely. A weekend that wasn't restful because part of your mind was in the inbox. A quiet resentment that you'd never say out loud: you did the work, and now you're the one who has to be gentle about being paid.

You look up. Your nephew is waving a plastic flag. You wave back, smile, and feel nothing like a person on a day off.

The pause

Your sister notices. "Work?"

"A client. They can't pay all at once."

"Can they pay some of it?"

It's such a simple question that you almost laugh. Some of it. When. How much. Not "yes" or "no", but a shape with edges.

You realise the problem was never the request. Instalments can be perfectly reasonable. The problem is that you've been answering without anything underneath you: no clear wording ready, no dates you can point to, no view of what happens if a payment doesn't arrive. Just your nerves, standing in for a process.

You don't need to be tougher. You need to not be improvising.

The other version of this Thursday

Imagine the same message arriving, and you have a calm, kind reply already written, one that says yes to a plan and also puts the dates in writing. You change a name and an amount. You send it in two minutes.

Imagine opening one sheet later, and each late invoice already carries a status. Who is on a plan, which date is next, and what the sensible next step is if a date passes. You aren't carrying it in your head. It's sitting there, quietly, like a tide table.

Imagine the first instalment arriving on the day it should. You see it, tick it, and that's it. No bargaining with yourself. No dread when a notification lights up the lock screen.

And if one doesn't arrive, you don't spiral. You have the next gentle message ready, and you know it's the right one for that moment.

That's the difference. Not harder. Just held.

Back to the sand

You won't answer this one on the beach. You write one line to the client: Thanks for telling me, I appreciate it. I'll send you a proposal today. It's honest, warm and commits you to nothing you haven't thought through.

Then you put the phone face down on the towel.

Your nephew needs a moat, and apparently you're the engineer. You dig. The water fills the channel, and for the first time since 11:40 your shoulders come down from your ears.

The invoice is still there. The question is still real. But the knot that comes from not knowing what to say has loosened, because you know that the saying can be prepared. It doesn't have to be invented in the moment, with a seagull watching.
