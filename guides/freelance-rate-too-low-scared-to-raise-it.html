Freelance Rate Too Low, Scared to Raise It? The Pier Café

2026-10-18

The invoice you paid for with your own patience

It is 12:40 on a Tuesday, and you are sitting in a café at the end of a pier, which is not where you planned to be.

Your sister is in town for the day. She went to the counter to order chips. You have your phone face down on the table, because the last email you opened was from a client you have worked with for three years. Subject line: "Quick favour?"

The favour is a day of work. The rate you quoted that client in your first year has never changed.

You know it is too low. You have known for a while. It sits in the back of your head like a stone in a shoe: small, constant, and somehow always the thing you notice when you try to relax.

The sentence you keep not writing

Seagulls argue over a dropped bun outside the window. Your sister comes back with a tray and asks how work is going.

"Busy," you say. It is true. It is also the thing you say when you do not want to say the other thing.

The other thing is this: you are busy and still tight at the end of the month. You have taken on more work to cover the gap, which means less time, which means more tiredness, which means you are even less likely to have the energy for the one conversation that would actually help.

You have drafted that email in your head perhaps forty times. "From next quarter, my rate will be..." Then a number appears, and your chest tightens, and the draft dissolves.

Because what if they say no? What if they say, "Oh, we didn't realise you were that expensive"? What if three years of easy, friendly work turns into a polite goodbye?

So you do nothing. Doing nothing feels safe. It is only later, in the quiet, that you notice it has a price too.

What the quiet costs

Nobody tells you this part. Undercharging does not feel like a mistake while it is happening. It feels like being nice. Like being flexible. Like being the kind of freelancer people love working with.

But it adds up in small, specific ways.

The weekend you spent catching up. The holiday you shortened because the maths did not quite work. The flicker of resentment when a client says "you're a lifesaver" and you think: then why does it feel like I'm the one drowning?

And underneath it all, a quieter feeling: that you are not quite a real business. That a real business would simply know its number and say it.

Your sister dips a chip in ketchup. "You've gone somewhere," she says.

"Sorry. Just thinking about a client."

"The one you're always thinking about?"

You laugh, because she is right.

The turning point is smaller than you think

Here is what you realise, somewhere between the third chip and the fourth gull.

You have been treating the rate as a feeling. Something you either have the courage to say or do not. But it is not only a feeling. It is a number that comes from somewhere: your costs, your unpaid hours, the weeks off you would like to take, the tax you will owe, the work you can realistically do in a month.

You have never actually sat down and looked at it that way. You picked a figure in year one because it felt acceptable, and then you defended it out of habit.

It is a strange relief to see it plainly. The fear does not vanish. But it changes shape. It stops being "I'm scared to ask for more" and becomes "here is the minimum I need, and here is why."

That is a different sentence. You can say that one out loud.

A Monday with the number in your pocket

Picture a Monday a few weeks from now.

You open your laptop with a coffee. There is no stone in the shoe. You know your minimum hourly rate and your day rate. You know them the way you know your own address.

When a new enquiry arrives, you do not flinch. You do not do quick, anxious sums in your head while pretending to think about the brief. You reply calmly, with a figure that has a reason behind it.

For the long-standing client, you write the email you have drafted forty times. This time it is short. Kind. Clear. You press send and then, remarkably, you go for a walk instead of refreshing your inbox.

Maybe they say yes. Maybe they negotiate. Maybe, once in a while, someone says no, and you discover that your week does not collapse. There is room in it now, room you used to fill with underpaid work.

And at six, you close the laptop. Not because you have finished everything, but because the money and the hours finally add up.
