No Contract or Brief, Work Already Started? The Dog Walk

2026-10-18

The rain is coming sideways across the park, and your dog has decided that this particular bush needs a full investigation.

You stand there holding the lead in one hand and your phone in the other. It's 7:30 on a Tuesday. There's a message from your new client, sent at 6:52.

"Morning! Attached the old logo files and some photos. Can you have a first look by Thursday? Also, my business partner has a few ideas, he'll email you."

You read it twice. Then you do the thing you've been doing since Friday: you scroll up through the thread, looking for the part where someone said what exactly you're making.

It isn't there.

How it started

It began so nicely. A friend of a friend. A phone call that ran long and warm. "We'd love to work with you, we're a bit rushed, can you just get going and we'll sort the paperwork after?"

You said yes. Of course you did. You're a freelancer, someone offered you work, and saying "first, let me send you a form" felt like standing in a doorway with your arms crossed.

So you got going. You made a folder. You made a start.

Now it's Tuesday and you're three days in, and you realise you couldn't write down, in two sentences, what you've agreed to deliver. You don't know how many rounds of changes are included. You don't know who signs things off: the client, or the client plus the partner with the ideas. You don't know when the first payment is supposed to happen, or whether anyone has said the word payment at all.

The dog finishes with the bush. You walk on, and the knot in your stomach walks with you.

The quiet cost

Nobody talks about this part. It isn't a disaster. Nothing has gone wrong yet. It's more like carrying a glass of water across a room that you can't quite put down.

You open the files while the kettle boils and find yourself guessing. Is this a refresh or a full redesign? Do they want three options or one? You make small decisions on their behalf and each one feels like a bet.

You also notice what's missing in yourself. You don't feel like a professional with a client. You feel like someone doing a favour that might turn into an argument.

And you know how these stories can go. Not always, not even often. But enough that the thought sits there: the partner's ideas arrive on Thursday, and they are all a little bigger than what you pictured.

The worst part is the awkwardness. Raising it now feels like admitting you started without a plan. Staying silent means every extra hour is a hour you can't name.

The moment it shifts

Back home, towel on the dog, coffee on the counter, you open a blank email to the client and type: "Hi, just to make sure we're aligned..."

Then you stop. Because the problem isn't the email. The problem is that you're inventing the whole conversation from scratch, at 8 am, while a little afraid of it.

You imagine a different version of this Tuesday. The one where, before any file arrived, the client had already answered a handful of simple questions. What are we making. What does done look like. Who decides. What do you need from me, and by when. Where do the logins go.

In that version, the first call has an agenda, so nobody wanders off into the partner's ideas. The scope is written down in plain words, and both sides have said "yes, that's it." A welcome email goes out that sounds like a person who runs a real business. Because you do.

None of it is dramatic. It's just ordinary. Almost boring, in the best way.

And you realise it was never a question of being pushy or cold. A clear start is a kindness. It tells a client: you're in good hands, and here's how this will go.

What you do with this morning

You can't undo the weekend. But you can still make a calm, friendly move today.

You delete the nervous draft. You write a short, warm note instead: thank you for the files, a few quick things to confirm before Thursday so the first look lands where you want it, and a short list of those things. You sound like someone who has done this many times, because the shape of it is simple enough to borrow.

You press send. The knot doesn't vanish, but it loosens a notch.

If anything about money or obligations gets complicated, that's a conversation for a professional who can advise on your specific situation. What you can do yourself is get the basics onto a page where both of you can see them.

A normal Monday, later

Picture a Monday a few weeks from now. A new enquiry arrives at 9:10. You're not nervous. You send the intake form, then the welcome email. The client fills in the form over their own coffee, and by the time you speak, you already know what they want.

The kickoff call is thirty minutes, with an agenda. At the end you both say the scope out loud, and it goes into a short confirmation. Access details land in one place, not scattered across four threads. A small tracker shows you where each new client stands: asked, confirmed, started, handed over.

At 6 pm you close the laptop. Nothing is hanging in the air.

The dog, by the way, still stops at every bush. Some things you don't fix.
