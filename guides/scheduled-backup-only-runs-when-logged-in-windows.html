Backup Only Runs When Logged In? The Cottage Kitchen

2026-11-07

The rain on the cottage window

It is Thursday, a little after eleven in the morning. Rain is running down the window of a rented cottage, and Mara has her laptop open on a kitchen table that smells of old wood and toast.

She calls it a week off. She has a half-finished video edit for a client, a mug of tea, and a plan to "just check one thing."

The one thing is her backup.

She opens Task Scheduler. The task is there, named something sensible, set to run every night at 22:00. Under Last Run Result it says the operation completed successfully. She feels her shoulders drop.

Then she looks at the date next to it. Monday.

There was nothing on Tuesday. Nothing on Wednesday. Last night, nothing.

The quiet thought

She checks the backup drive at home through the remote link. The newest folder is from Monday evening, and three days of edits aren't in it.

She does not panic. It is a quieter feeling than that, a small cold weight under the ribs.

She thinks: I set this up properly. I remember doing it.

Then she works it out. On Monday she was at her desk until ten, logged in, laptop awake. The task fired. Since Tuesday she has been closing the lid at six, locking the screen, and sometimes signing out entirely. The task was never broken. It only runs when she is there to watch it.

A backup that needs you in the room is not really a backup. It is a favour your computer does you on the days you happen to behave.

What it quietly costs

Nobody talks about this part. It is not the lost files, because nothing is lost yet. It is the low hum of doubt that sits behind everything.

It is the half second of dread when an app freezes mid-save. It is the way you avoid deleting anything, just in case. It is the client call where someone says "can you send me last Tuesday's version?" and you say "of course" while your stomach says please exist.

Freelancers carry this alone. There is no IT department, no one to ask, "Did the nightly job run?" There is you, and a green tick you stopped questioning months ago.

Mara sits with her tea going cold. She thinks about the three days of cutting she would have to redo, and about the client who believes this project is simply, calmly, in good hands.

She copies the project folder to a cloud drive by hand. It takes forty minutes over cottage Wi-Fi. She watches the progress bar and does not enjoy her week off.

The turning point

The thing she realises, somewhere around minute thirty, is that she has been asking the wrong question.

She kept asking, "Why did the task fail?" It did not fail. It did exactly what it was told, in a setup she made on a busy afternoon and never looked at again.

What she wanted was simpler. She wanted a backup that did not depend on her mood, her lid, or her login. She wanted a log she could read in ten seconds: ran at 22:00, copied this much, kept the last few snapshots, tidied the old ones. And she wanted to rehearse a change before trusting it with her real files.

She did not want a project, just a quiet routine she could stop thinking about.

A normal Monday, after

Picture a Monday morning a few weeks later. Mara is back at her own desk with a coffee, the radiator ticking.

She opens a small text file. One line per night, each with a time, a count and the word done. The weekend lines look exactly like the weekday lines, though the laptop sat locked and dark for two days.

She reads it in the time it takes the kettle to click off. Then she closes it and starts work.

The client messages at 9:15. "Could you send me the version from before the colour changes?"

She says, "Give me a minute," and means it as a plain fact, not a prayer. She finds the folder, sends the file, and carries on with her morning.

Nothing dramatic happens. That is the point.

The next holiday is a holiday. The rain on the window is just rain.
