Am I Undercharging as a Freelancer? The Sunday Walk That Hurt

2026-10-12

The sun is out, the park is full, and you are the only person on the path who is not looking at the trees.

You are looking at your phone. Specifically, at a message from a client: Could you squeeze in one more round of changes before Tuesday? Should be quick!

It is Sunday, 15:10. You said yes before you finished reading it. You always say yes.

Mara, the illustrator we will follow through this story, is not a real person. She is a mix of many people who work alone, and you may know her anyway. She has been fully booked for five months. Her calendar looks like success. Her body feels like a tab that has been open for far too long.

Busy is not the same as fine

On paper, things are going well. Clients return. Referrals arrive. Nobody complains.

And yet.

She is tired in a way that sleep does not fix. She feels a small flicker of resentment when a project she once loved lands in her inbox. She has started to dread her own good news.

When a friend asks how work is going, she says, "Busy, which is good!" She says it the way you press a doorbell you are not sure anyone will answer.

What she does not say: I think I am working harder every month and the money is not moving.

The question you ask quietly

Most people who work for themselves have asked it at least once, usually alone, usually at an awkward hour.

Am I undercharging?

It rarely arrives as a clear thought. It shows up as small things:

  • You feel a little sick when you type your price into a quote.
  • You say "it's just a small thing" about work that took half a day.
  • You are relieved when a client says yes instantly, and you suspect that means you were too cheap.
  • You haven't raised your rate since the day you set it, because you set it in a hurry, in a different year, as a different version of yourself.

The cost is easy to miss because it never arrives as one bill. It leaks out slowly. A weekend here. A holiday you don't take. A flat tone in your voice when you finally log off.

Burnout, for many people who work alone, doesn't look like collapse. It looks like quietly running out of patience for the work you chose.

The call that made it plain

On Wednesday at 10:30, Mara has a call with a long-standing client. It's friendly. The client says they'd love to start a new series, "same as last time, if the budget is the same."

She opens her mouth to agree.

Then she remembers last time. The three rounds of changes. The extra files. The call that was supposed to take twenty minutes. She remembers sitting at her desk on a Saturday with a sandwich she forgot to eat.

She doesn't say yes. She doesn't say no either. She hears herself say, "Let me come back to you by Friday."

It is the first time in months she has bought herself a pause. Her hands are slightly shaky when she hangs up, the way they are after you've finally said something true.

Looking at the actual picture

That evening she doesn't open a design file. She sits with a notebook and a cup of tea that goes cold.

She isn't trying to be clever. She is just trying to see.

What does she actually need to earn to live the way she wants to live? How many hours of the week are really paid hours, and how many disappear into emails, revisions and the long tail of "quick things"? What would a fair number look like if it were allowed to include rest, taxes, a slow month and a day off?

She has never put those things on one page before. She always started from what other people charge, or from what felt safe to say aloud. She never started from her own life.

The number she lands on isn't outrageous. It isn't a fantasy. But it is clearly higher than the one she has been using.

She stares at it for a while. Then something odd happens. She doesn't feel greedy. She feels relieved, as if a number she'd been carrying around without a name finally had one.

A different Monday

Picture a Monday a few months later.

Mara wakes at 7:30 without an alarm. Coffee. The window is open, and someone downstairs is dragging a bike out of the hallway.

She has two projects this week, not five. Both are priced the way she now prices things, with a calm sentence she has practised and no apology attached. One client pushed back a little. She stayed friendly and didn't move. The other said "sure, that makes sense" in under a minute, and she was surprised how little the sky fell.

At 17:45 she closes the laptop. Not because she has finished everything, but because the day was built to fit inside a day.

On Sunday afternoon, the park is just a park. Her phone stays in her pocket. A message arrives, and it can wait until morning.

Nothing about her talent has changed. The difference is that her price now matches the person doing the work.

If you recognise yourself

You may not be undercharging. Some people aren't, and the tiredness has other causes. But if you have never put your real costs, your real hours and your real life on one page, it is hard to know either way, and not knowing is its own kind of weight.

Seeing a number is a smaller step than changing your whole business. It's just a way to stop guessing.
