Abandon Every Planner by February? The Blank Page Call

2026-10-21

It is 10:05 on a Tuesday in February. Mara is on a video call with a client, and the client has just asked a simple question.

"When could you have the second round to us? Roughly?"

Mara says, "Let me just check my calendar," in the bright voice of someone who has everything under control.

She does not check her calendar. She reaches for the planner on the desk, the linen-covered one with the gold ribbon. It cost more than she wanted to admit. She flips it open below the camera frame.

The first three weeks of January are full. Neat handwriting, little colour-coded boxes, a tiny star beside "invoice Dana." Then the pages thin out. A scribble on the fourth Monday. Then nothing. Blank page after blank page, ribbon dangling in the gutter.

"Thursday week," she says. "Probably."

She has no idea if that is true.

The drawer of good intentions

After the call, Mara does not open the planner again. She just looks at it for a moment.

There is a drawer in the sideboard behind her. She knows what is in it without looking. A dated diary from a stationery shop. A "productivity system" with quarterly goal pages. A minimalist one, with only a week on each spread, which she bought because the previous one had been too busy. Each began with a clean Sunday night and a good pen.

Every January it feels like a promise. By February it feels like evidence.

That is the part nobody says out loud. You don't just stop using the planner. You start to feel there is something wrong with you. Other people seem to manage this. They have routines. They look like a real business, and you look like someone who buys notebooks.

So you tell yourself the real problem is willpower, and you begin to dread next January's shopping trip.

What the blank pages were actually saying

That evening, Mara sits on the sofa with the planner on her knees and, for once, reads it like a witness instead of a judge.

The pages that stayed full were the ones where she wrote down what had happened: which client, which job, which invoice went out. The pages that went blank were the ones where she was asked to plan in a format that didn't match her life. A page for "top three priorities" on a day when a client moves a deadline at 9 am. A habit tracker with twelve rows, when she has two habits that matter.

There was never a day she had room for the planner. The planner always wanted something from her first.

And her year doesn't look like a standard year. A big project lands in March. There is a quiet week in August that she half dreads and half needs. Some clients pay in fourteen days and one pays whenever it remembers. A planner built for a nine-to-five person with identical weeks was never going to survive contact with that.

It wasn't a discipline problem. It was a fit problem.

The idea of a planner that asks for less

She doesn't need more features. She needs fewer decisions.

A month she can see at a glance, with room for the odd shape of freelance life. A week that lets her put down the three things that matter and not much else. A place to note who she billed and who still owes her, somewhere she can find it in ten seconds, even on a hectic Thursday. Paper she can print again if she spills coffee on it, or if she wants to start over in July without feeling like she failed.

She thinks, perhaps for the first time, that a planner could be a quiet thing. Something that sits on the desk and waits, instead of something that keeps score.

An ordinary Monday, later

Imagine it a few months on.

It is 9:40 on a Monday. The kettle has clicked off. A client message arrives asking about timing. Mara opens the planner to this week, glances at the page, and sees a clear Thursday afternoon.

"Thursday works. You'll have it by five."

That is the whole conversation. Nobody notices. Nobody can tell the difference between her and the freelancer with the perfect routine. But she can feel the difference: no bright voice, no reaching below the camera frame, no small flinch.

In March the pages are not blank. They are a bit untidy, maybe, with an arrow, a crossed-out date, a coffee ring. But they are used, and that is the only thing a planner has to be.

She finishes her tea and gets on with the day. At six, she closes the laptop.
