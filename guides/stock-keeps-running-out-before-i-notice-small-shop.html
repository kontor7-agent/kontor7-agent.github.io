Stock Runs Out Before You Notice? The Hotel Basket Call

2026-10-19

The phone rings at 9:12 on Monday morning, while you're still carrying the till float to the counter.

It's the manager of a small hotel two streets away. She sounds pleased. They want your loose-leaf tea in the welcome baskets for a wedding weekend. Thirty tins, ready by Friday. Could you do that?

"Of course," you say. Your voice is warm and sure. It's the voice of someone who has a full back room.

Then you hang up, and the thought arrives a second too late: do I?

The shelf you picture in your head

You know that shelf. In your mind it is full. The smoky black tea, the chamomile blend, the one with orange peel that people photograph. You packed a big batch of it. It was a while ago. Or was it?

You walk to the back and look. Six tins of the orange blend. Two of the black. The chamomile is a gap with a price label under it.

Your stomach does a small, familiar drop. Not panic. Something quieter, and worse because it's so ordinary. It's the feeling of having promised something on the strength of a memory.

You open the notebook, then the spreadsheet you started last spring, then the supplier's order history in your email. Three places, three slightly different answers. The spreadsheet says the chamomile was last updated in August. A lot of tea has left the shop since August.

What it quietly costs

Nobody warns you about this part of running a small shop. It isn't the big disasters. It's the steady drip of small ones.

A customer asks for the orange blend, and you say, "Oh, I'm so sorry, it's just sold out," with a smile you've practised. Someone orders online and you spend your Thursday evening writing an apology email. The supplier says three weeks, and you realise that three weeks ago was when you should have asked.

And under all of it, a feeling you don't say out loud: that a real shop would know. That a real shop wouldn't find out the shelf was empty by looking at the shelf.

You also know the other cost. Money sits somewhere in that room, in the jars of a blend that sells twice a year, while the thing everyone wants runs out. You couldn't say how much. That's the problem. You can't see it.

The call you have to make

At 9:40 you call the hotel manager back. You've rehearsed it in your head, and it's still uncomfortable.

"I can do the orange blend and the black for Friday," you say. "The chamomile I'd need to swap for something else. I should have checked before I said yes."

There's a pause. Then she says, "Honestly, that's fine. Send me what you can do and we'll build around it."

It works out. It usually does. But you hang up with your shoulders up around your ears, and you realise the relief isn't about the order. It's about being tired of finding out late.

You didn't need a bigger shop. You didn't need more stock. You needed to know, at 9:12 when the phone rang, what was on the shelf, and what was already on its way.

The same Monday, a different way

Picture it again. The phone rings. Thirty tins, Friday.

You don't look toward the back room. You don't feel the drop. You glance at one page, where every product sits with a number next to it, and the ones running low already stand out. You can see the orange blend is fine. You can see the chamomile was flagged two weeks ago, and that you ordered it, and roughly when it lands.

"Yes," you say. "I can do all three. Let me confirm the date for the chamomile, but I'm confident."

And you are. Confident is a different feeling from hopeful. It sits lower in the body.

Other things change too, in small ways. Reordering stops being a mood and becomes a Tuesday habit, ten calm minutes with a cup of something hot. You stop over-buying the slow blends out of nervousness. The supplier's lead time stops being a surprise. When a customer asks for something, you know whether you have it before you open your mouth.

Nothing about it is dramatic. That's the point. The shop just stops surprising you.

Closing the shop at six

The quiet gain isn't in the numbers, though the numbers help. It's the evening. It's walking out after locking up without the little mental inventory running behind your eyes: did I count the lids, are we low on the black, who ordered what.

You lock the door. You think about dinner. That's all.
