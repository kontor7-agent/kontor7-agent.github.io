Forget to Reorder Stock? The Birthday Party Phone Buzz

2026-10-21

The trampoline park smells of rubber and warm pizza boxes. Somewhere behind you, eleven seven-year-olds are screaming in a way that sounds like joy and might be injury.

You are holding a paper cup of lukewarm tea. Your phone buzzes against your hip.

It is a customer. Hi! Just checking when the lavender soap will be back? I wanted it for my mum's birthday.

Your stomach drops, because you know the answer. You haven't got any. You haven't had any since Wednesday. And the shea butter you need to make more is not in the workshop, because you meant to order it two weeks ago.

You meant to. That is the whole problem.

The sentence you keep saying

Let's call you Mira. You make soap and bath salts in a spare room, and you sell them online and at two markets a month. You are good at it. People write kind things about the scent.

But there is one sentence you say to yourself more often than you'd like: I'll reorder it when I get a minute.

The minute never comes. Or it comes at 23:00, when you are tired, and you can't remember how many bags are left, so you decide to look tomorrow. Tomorrow the orders come in, the parcels need packing, and the thought slips away again.

So you run on memory. You glance at the shelf, you think that looks fine, and you trust it. Most weeks it works.

Then one week it doesn't. And it never fails on a quiet day. It fails right after a good weekend, when you sold more than you expected, and the shelf you glanced at on Friday is not the shelf you have on Monday.

What it quietly costs

The cost is not only the missing sale. It is smaller and meaner than that.

It is the apology email you write standing up in the kitchen. It is the supplier who says three weeks, sorry, and the rush fee you pay anyway. It is the half-bag of something you bought twice because you forgot you already had it, sitting in a cupboard like a small, scented reproach.

It is also how it makes you feel. A little unprofessional. Like someone who runs a real business on the outside and a sticky-note one on the inside. You'd never say that to a customer. You'd barely say it to yourself.

And there is the knot. The low, constant what have I forgotten now? that sits under every good day. You can enjoy a market stall and still be half-counting jars in your head.

The reply you type with one thumb

Back at the party, a child in a foam helmet runs past you, shrieking.

You type: So sorry, it's sold out at the moment. I'll let you know when it's back. You delete "I'll let you know when it's back", because you don't actually know when that is. You send a vaguer version.

Then you stand there. Not angry. Just tired in a very specific way.

A parent next to you, who sells vintage lamps online, glances at your face and says, "Work?"

"Stock," you say. "I always find out too late."

She laughs, not unkindly. "I used to find out when a customer told me."

You both look at the trampolines for a moment. Neither of you says anything wise. But you walk back to the car afterwards with one clear thought, and it is not a dramatic one: finding out from a customer is a terrible way to run a shelf.

It doesn't have to work like this

That evening you don't rebuild your whole business. You just sit with a notebook and ask a smaller question.

What if the shelf told you first?

Not a complicated system. Not software with fourteen menus. Just one calm place where every item lives, you can see what's left, and something quietly says this one is getting low while there is still time to do something about it. While the supplier's three weeks is still just an inconvenience and not a crisis.

The idea is almost embarrassingly simple. The reorder moment moves from "a customer asked" to "I saw it coming". That is the entire difference.

A Monday that feels different

Picture the next Monday. Same spare room, same kettle, same parcels waiting by the door.

You open one file with your coffee. It takes a couple of minutes. A few lines are marked as running low. You nod, you order the shea butter, you order the little kraft boxes you were nearly out of, and you close the file.

That's it. No digging through cupboards. No frantic mental arithmetic. No guilt about the thing you forgot, because this time you didn't forget it.

When a customer asks about the lavender soap that week, the answer is yes, it's in stock. Or, if it isn't, you already know exactly when it will be.

The knot loosens. You notice it only because it's gone: that background hum of what have I missed? is quieter. Your Saturday belongs to the trampolines again.

You are not a different person. You are the same maker with the same good soap. You just stopped relying on your memory for something memory was never good at.
