Lost Track of Recruiters and Contacts? The Bike Stand

2026-11-07

Saturday, ten past nine. You're standing in a bike shop with a flat tyre and a coffee going cold on the counter. Your phone buzzes.

Marta Weiss. A former colleague, one of the good ones.

"Hey! Did you ever hear back from Daniel? I mentioned you to him two weeks ago. He said he'd reach out."

Your stomach drops a little. Daniel? Which Daniel?

You scroll. There's a Daniel from the agency. There's a Daniel who runs a studio, and one you met at a meetup in the spring. You have no idea which of them Marta means. You have no idea whether he wrote to you. You have a faint, sinking feeling that someone did, and that the message is sitting somewhere under forty others.

"Ha, I'll check!" you type. Then you stand there with a thumb over the screen, feeling like a person who can't be trusted with their own life.

The pile nobody sees

Here's the part you don't say out loud to anyone. The job search isn't only applications. It's people.

There's the recruiter who called on a Wednesday and said she'd send details. The old boss who offered to "keep an eye out." The woman from a conference who said, "Send me your portfolio." The friend of a friend who agreed to a fifteen-minute call "sometime after the 20th."

Each of them is a small act of kindness. And each one lives in a different place: an email thread, a LinkedIn message, a text, a sticky note on the monitor, your head.

So you carry them. All day, a low hum of who owes whom a reply? You can't tell whether you're waiting on them or they're waiting on you. You can't tell whether the silence means no or just means the message got buried.

And it costs you things. You skip following up because you're not sure you haven't already. You put off thanking someone because you can't recall what they did. Last month you nearly wrote to the same person twice with almost the same question, and you caught it only because the subject line looked familiar.

That's what's quietly expensive. Nobody is being rude. You're just starting to feel like someone who's careless with people who were generous to you.

The tyre, the stand, the pause

The mechanic wheels your bike onto the repair stand. It's a plain thing, a metal arm that holds the wheel still so he can see what's going on. He spins it. He finds the thin spot in about four seconds.

"There," he says. "Thorn. Tiny."

You watch him and think: he can only fix it because it stopped moving.

Your search has never stopped moving. Every contact, every thread, every maybe is spinning at once, and you've been trying to hold all of it in your hands while it turns.

You don't need more effort. You need a stand. One place where everyone sits still long enough to be seen: who they are, how you met, what they offered, what you promised back, and when it's worth a gentle nudge.

It doesn't have to be clever. It just has to be the one place you look.

Monday, another version

Picture it. Monday morning, the kettle clicking off, the laptop open.

You don't scroll through three inboxes with your shoulders up around your ears. You open one sheet. Ten minutes, maybe less.

Two names are marked as due for a follow-up this week. You see that Daniel from the studio was introduced by Marta, that you haven't heard from him, and that it's been twelve days. A friendly nudge is reasonable. You write it in a minute, because the context is right there and you aren't reconstructing it from memory.

You notice you haven't thanked the woman who read your portfolio. You do that now, properly, with the detail she'd appreciate.

The recruiter from Wednesday has a note beside her name: promised details, nothing yet. Her row doesn't nag you. It just waits.

Then you close it. You go and stand in the kitchen with your coffee, still warm for once.

Nothing about the market changed. There are the same number of openings and the same slow replies. But the knot has loosened, because you're no longer the only place where everything is stored. You can answer Marta honestly: "Daniel hasn't written yet. I'm sending him a note today. Thank you for thinking of me."

That sentence sounds like a professional. Because it is one.

What you get back

It isn't really about being organised. It's about being the kind of person people want to help again.

When someone gives you a favour, you remember it. When someone is waiting on you, you know. When you prepare for a conversation, you walk in knowing how you got there. You stop dreading the phone buzzing at a bike shop on a Saturday.

And you stop quietly wondering whether you've let something slip.
