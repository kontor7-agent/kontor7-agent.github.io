Client Says I Never Sent the Invoice? The Permit Queue

2026-10-22

You're standing in a queue at the town hall, number 41 in your hand, number 27 on the screen. It's 10:35 on a Tuesday. You came for a parking permit. You're holding a phone.

The email came in at 10:12. Four lines, polite, a little puzzled:

Hi, we're closing the books for the quarter and can't find an invoice from you for the website copy. Could you send it over?

Your thumb hovers. You think you sent it. You can almost see it: the PDF, the subject line, the little whoosh of the send button. But was that this invoice, or the one before? Was it three weeks ago, or four? Did it go to the right address, or to the person who left in June?

The screen says 28.

The question underneath the question

On the surface, this is about one document. Underneath, it's about something you rarely say out loud: am I the kind of person who runs a real business?

A real business knows what it sent. A real business doesn't stand in a queue scrolling through its sent folder with a hot face, typing "invoice" into the search bar and getting forty results.

So you scroll. There's a draft called "Invoice_final". There's another called "Invoice_final_v2". There's a PDF in Downloads with a date that could mean anything. Nothing tells you whether the thing ever left your laptop.

The woman ahead of you shifts her bag. The screen says 29.

The two bad answers

You have two replies in your head, and you dislike both.

The first: "I definitely sent it, please check your spam." Maybe true. But if you're wrong, you've just told a client they're careless when you were the careless one.

The second: "Oh, so sorry, I'll send it right now!" Easy. Fast. But it quietly says I forgot, and it hands over a free extra week of waiting. Their payment terms start from the day they receive it, so every day of doubt is a day your money sits in someone else's account.

This is the part that costs more than the invoice. It's the small loss of ground. You were the one who was owed, and somehow you're the one apologising.

You type a reply, delete it, type another. Number 30.

What you find at 10:51

It takes eleven minutes. You find it in the sent folder under a different client's thread, sent to an address with one letter wrong. It bounced. The bounce notice is sitting in a folder you never open.

So: you did try. And the client is right. They never got it.

Neither of you was lying. The honest answer was in a dozen scattered places, and none of them was a place that knew.

You send it properly, with a calm line: "Thanks for flagging this. The first one went to an old address. Attached is the invoice, and I've noted the new due date." No grovelling, no blame. It's the voice of a person who has this handled, even though you only just figured it out in a queue.

Then your number comes up. You get the permit. You walk out into a perfectly ordinary day, and the knot in your stomach is still there, because you know it will happen again.

The quiet idea

Later, over a sandwich on a bench, you realise what was missing. It wasn't discipline. It wasn't better memory. You don't need to remember what you sent.

You need one place that already knows: this client, this project, this invoice, sent on this date, due on that one, paid or not. A single line you can glance at, instead of a detective hunt through email, downloads and half-finished drafts.

Not a system with a hundred buttons. Just a short list where each invoice has a status you can read in two seconds.

A different Tuesday

Picture the same email arriving. Can't find an invoice.

You don't flinch. You open one file, find the client's name, and see it in a second: sent on the 3rd, due on the 17th, still open. Or: not sent yet, and now it is. Either way, you answer in a minute, warmly, with the date in your reply and no knot.

You're still in the queue. The screen says 27 and you're number 41. You put the phone away and look at the noticeboard. Somebody is selling a bicycle. You have fourteen minutes of nothing to do, which is exactly what a quiet mind feels like.

That's most of what "professional" means, really. Not never making a mistake. Just knowing where you stand.
