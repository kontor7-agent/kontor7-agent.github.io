No Plan for 2027 as a Freelancer? The Frosted Windscreen

2026-10-20

The first frost of the year came overnight, and you didn't check the forecast.

It is 7:30 on a Tuesday. You stand beside your car in your slippers-and-coat compromise, scraping the windscreen with a loyalty card because the real scraper is somewhere in the flat. Your breath hangs in the air. Your phone buzzes in your pocket.

It's a message from a friend: "Do you already know what you're doing next year? I feel like everyone has a plan except me."

You keep scraping. You don't answer.

The question you've been avoiding

Let's call you Lena. You're a freelance web designer, six years in. It's an illustration, but you might know her.

You don't have a plan for next year. You have a feeling. It sits somewhere under your ribs, and it has been there since the clocks changed.

You know there are two months left in this one. You know the shops already have their lights up. You know that in January, everyone will say "new year, new start" as if it were a switch.

You also know that you don't really know:

  • how much you want to earn,
  • how many weeks you want to take off,
  • which clients you'd like more of,
  • which one you'd quietly like to stop working for.

So you do what you always do. You tell yourself you'll think about it once the current projects are done. They are never done.

What it quietly costs

Nobody sees this kind of overwhelm. It doesn't look like a crisis. It looks like a person who is busy.

But it leaks. It leaks into the small decisions. You say yes to a project in October because you can't picture saying no to anything in March. You skip the holiday you wanted because you haven't looked at whether you could afford it. You open a blank document called Plans 2027, type the title, and close it again.

The real cost is that you feel like you're driving with a frosted windscreen. You can move. You're moving. You just can't see very far.

And there's a small, mean voice that says: a real business would have this figured out by now.

The ten clear centimetres

The card does its job. A patch clears in the glass, about the size of a letterbox, and through it you see the street. The bin lorry. A neighbour waving. The ordinary morning, still there.

Standing in the cold, you realise something. You don't need the whole windscreen clear to start driving. You need a small clear patch, and then another.

You've been treating "the plan for next year" as one enormous thing that must be solved in a single heroic evening. Perhaps it's twelve months. Perhaps it's fifty-two weeks. Perhaps it's a handful of small, quiet decisions, each one written down where you can see it.

That thought is smaller than the feeling. It fits in your coat pocket.

A different Monday

Picture it, not as a promise, just as a picture.

It's a Monday in February. You make tea. Next to your laptop lies a page you can actually hold. At the top is the week: three client days, one day for your own work, one lighter day. A line for who owes you money. A corner where you wrote down, back in November, what a good month looks like.

You don't open six tabs to find out where you stand. You glance at the page, and you know.

A client writes asking if you can start something next week. You look at your weeks. There's a gap in April, a week you marked as off, and you simply say, "I can begin on the 14th." Calm voice. No scrambling, no apology.

The knot under your ribs is not gone forever. But it has a smaller job now.

At six, you close the laptop. The evening is yours, because the plan already did the worrying for you.

Back at the car

You finish the windscreen. Your fingers ache. You get in, and the heater begins its slow, grumbling work.

Before you pull away, you type a reply to your friend: "Not yet. But I'm going to start with something small."

It feels better than you expected. Not because you've solved anything, but because you've stopped pretending you should have solved it already.

Plenty of people who work for themselves go into a new year this way: capable, busy, and without a map. It isn't a character flaw. It's what happens when nobody hands you a calendar for a business of one.
