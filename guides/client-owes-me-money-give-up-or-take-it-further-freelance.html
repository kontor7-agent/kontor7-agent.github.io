Client Owes Me Money: Give Up or Take It Further?

2026-10-30

The skatepark opens at nine on Saturdays, and by 9:20 Priya is already on the cold concrete bench with a paper cup of tea going lukewarm. Her nephew is somewhere in the bowl, falling and getting up again. She is meant to be watching.

She is looking at her phone.

The screen shows an invoice she knows by heart: €1,850 for a product launch text, brand voice guide and three rounds of edits. Sent in July. Due in August. It is October now.

The thought running in a loop behind her eyes is the one she hasn't said out loud to anyone: Should I just give up on this? Or do I take it further?

The question underneath the question

She has sent reminders. A polite one, a firmer one, and one she rewrote so many times it ended up sounding like an apology for existing. The replies were a thumbs-up emoji, then silence, then "Will look into it this week!" from a person who clearly did not look into it that week.

The frustrating part is that the amount isn't ruinous. She could survive without it. That is exactly what makes the decision so slippery.

If she lets it go, she tells herself she is being practical. Mature. Above the drama. But a small, sour voice says she is also teaching this client, and maybe herself, that her work is optional to pay for.

If she pushes on, she imagines the worst: an ugly exchange, a lawyer's letter, a reputation for being "difficult", hours of her life spent on someone who already took enough of them.

So she does what many people do. She does neither. She carries it.

It sits in her chest during the grocery run. It's there when a new enquiry arrives and she wonders whether this one will pay either. It is there at the dinner table, when someone asks how work is going and she says "busy, good" in a voice that is half a note too bright.

What the waiting actually costs

Nobody puts this on an invoice, but waiting has a price.

It is the Sunday afternoon you lose to rehearsing a call you never make. It is the quiet resentment when you work on the next project. It is the way "give up or take it further" turns into a debate you hold with yourself at 3 am, because nothing has been decided and nothing is scheduled.

Priya realises something on that bench, watching her nephew wobble on the lip of the ramp for the fourth time before dropping in.

She isn't stuck because the decision is hard. She is stuck because she has no next step. There's no "after this date, I do that." Every day the invoice stays open, she has to decide again from scratch, from the stomach, with all the dread attached.

That is exhausting in a way the €1,850 doesn't capture.

The shift

Her nephew lands the drop. He turns and gives her a double thumbs-up, wildly proud. She gives one back. And for a moment the answer feels simple, not because she knows the "right" move, but because she sees what's been missing.

A decision made in the heat of a bad feeling is a different thing from a decision made along a calm timeline. Reminder one on the due date. Reminder two a week later. A clear, friendly line saying what happens next, written before she is tired and hurt and tempted to either explode or vanish.

Then, if the money still doesn't come, she is not agonising over whether she is allowed to take the next step. The step was already on the list. And if she decides to let something go, that is a choice she made with her eyes open, not a silence she drifted into.

Whether it ever goes beyond emails (a collections agency, a lawyer, a small claims route) depends on her country, her contract and the amount. That is a question for someone qualified. But she doesn't need to answer it today. She only needs to know what this week looks like.

A different Monday

Picture it. Monday, 8:40. Coffee. She opens one sheet and sees three overdue invoices, each with a date and a single plain next action. One says: send reminder two. One says: wait, due Friday. One says: time to decide.

She sends the email in two minutes, in a tone that sounds like her on a good day: warm, steady, clear. No rewriting nine times. No apology. Then she closes the laptop and walks to the bakery.

The invoice hasn't magically paid itself. But the knot is smaller. Because chasing money without begging isn't about being tougher. It's about having the words ready and the order of events decided, so your feelings don't have to carry the whole thing.
