Customer Won't Pay a Materials Deposit? The Utility Room

2026-10-31

Tuesday, 10:20 in the morning. You are standing in a utility room that smells of damp plaster and somebody else's washing powder.

Your phone is in one hand. In the other is a tape measure you forgot to put down. The customer is in the doorway, friendly, arms folded, waiting.

On the phone, the account desk at your supplier is polite in the way people are when they are about to say no. The new boiler, the pipework and the controls are all ready to be picked up. They just need the payment first. Your own account is already close to its limit from the last two jobs.

You covered the phone and said it quietly, the way you'd say something embarrassing in church.

"Could we do the materials up front? Just the materials. Not my time."

The customer smiled, a little surprised. "Oh. I'd rather pay when it's finished, if that's okay. That's how I always do it."

And you heard yourself say, "Of course. No problem."

The sentence you didn't say

Here is the part nobody talks about. You didn't say what you were thinking.

You were thinking that you haven't been paid for the last job yet. That your card has a number on it you don't like looking at. That "when it's finished" means three weeks of your own money sitting in a pipe in somebody's wall.

But the customer was nice. The house was nice. The job was good. And asking for money upfront felt like saying I don't trust you, which you don't mean at all. You just can't afford to be the bank.

So you said yes. Then you stood in the car park outside and did the sum in your head, the one you always do afterwards and never before.

What it costs you quietly

It isn't the job that costs you. It's everything around it.

It's checking your banking app at the supermarket queue, hoping the last payment landed. It's choosing the cheaper fittings because the better ones would stretch the card. It's the small flinch every time the customer says, "Any idea when you'll be finished?" because finishing means invoicing, and invoicing means waiting again.

It's quoting from memory on the doorstep, rounding the materials down so the number sounds friendly, and finding out on Thursday that the pipework alone ate a fifth of what you charged.

And it's the feeling that other people running proper businesses have a way of doing this. A sheet, a policy, a calm voice. You just have a van, a phone, and a habit of saying "no problem."

The call that went differently

Picture the same utility room, a different Tuesday.

Not because you became someone else. You still have the same tape measure and the same kind customer. The difference is that before you drove over, you sat for ten minutes with the quote and could see it plainly, line by line: the labour, the materials, the margin you actually keep. Not a guess. Not a feeling.

So when the customer says, "I'd rather pay when it's finished," you don't flinch.

You say, calmly, "The boiler and parts come to this much. I'd like that covered before I order, and the rest is on completion. That's how I keep the price fair for you."

Notice what that sentence is. It isn't an apology or an accusation. It's a number you can stand behind, because you know where it came from.

Maybe the customer says yes. Maybe they ask for a day to think. Maybe they say it doesn't suit them, and you gently decide whether this is a job you can carry. Any of those is fine. The difference is that you are choosing, instead of just absorbing.

A normal Monday, afterwards

It's not dramatic. That's the point.

Monday morning, you drink your tea standing up. You open one sheet and see the week: what's quoted, what's ordered, what's been paid for, and what's still owed to you. A receipt from the trade counter is already in the right place instead of the glove box. The job in the utility room shows its real margin, not the hopeful one.

You ring the supplier from the van with the order and the payment in hand. No hold music dread. No silent maths.

When a customer asks for "just a rough price," you can take ten minutes to give them a proper one. When someone owes you, you know who, since when, and how much, so the chasing doesn't feel like begging. It feels like admin.

And at six o'clock the laptop closes, and the evening belongs to you. Not to a card statement.

It was never rudeness

Asking for materials upfront isn't rude. For many trades it's an ordinary part of running a job. But it only feels ordinary when you can see your own numbers clearly enough to say them without apologising.

Most of the awkwardness was never about the customer. It was about not being sure of the figure in your own hands.
