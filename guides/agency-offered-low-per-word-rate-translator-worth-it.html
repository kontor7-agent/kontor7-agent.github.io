Low Per-Word Rate From an Agency? The Knife Sharpener

2026-10-25

The man at the knife sharpener's stall has a queue of three. You are fourth, holding your grandmother's bread knife in a tea towel, when your phone buzzes in your coat pocket.

It's an agency you've never worked with. The subject line says Long-term collaboration. You open it standing there, between a woman with garden shears and a boy clutching a pair of scissors.

The offer is friendly. Steady volume. Technical manuals, German to English. A per-word rate that is lower than your usual, "in line with our standard terms." Then a line about "CAT tool leverage" and a "discount for repetitions," which is not quite a number and not quite an explanation.

The reply they want by Friday.

The sum you can't do in a queue

Your first thought is that's low. Your second thought is but is it?

Because steady volume sounds lovely. You think of the quiet weeks in February. You think of the invoice you're still waiting on from another agency, the one that pays when it pays. A regular client who sends work every week is not nothing.

But the rate looks small written out, and you can't tell how small. Small per word is one thing. Small per hour is another, and you've never been sure how far apart those two are. It depends on how fast you really translate when the text is dense. It depends on how much of it the tool has already seen. It depends on what a "discount for repetitions" does to the number at the bottom.

The man at the stall runs a blade along the stone. The sound goes right through your teeth.

You realise you've been nodding at nothing. You're about to answer an email with your gut, and your gut has been wrong in both directions before. Once you said yes to a rate you resented by the second page. Once you said no, and then spent a week wondering if you'd just turned down your own stability.

What it quietly costs

Nobody tells you about this part of working alone. It isn't the translating. You're good at that. It's the moment someone names a number and you have to decide, right then, whether you're worth it.

So you do what many of us do. You say "let me think about it," and carry the question around for two days. It sits next to the shopping list and the dentist appointment. It wakes you at 5:40 on Friday morning.

And underneath is a smaller, quieter worry. That you've been saying yes to work that pays less than it feels like. That a few of the agencies you're proud to work for are quietly the reason you're tired. You've never lined them up side by side. You just have a feeling about each one, and feelings are not much help when somebody wants an answer by Friday.

The knife comes back sharp

"Fourth in line," the man says, and takes the bread knife from the towel. He holds it up to the light, turns it once, and asks the only question you hear all morning that you can answer immediately.

"What do you cut with it?"

"Bread. Every day."

"Then you want it sharp where you use it," he says. "Not everywhere."

It's a small thing, said over the noise of the stone. But you stand there with it for a moment. You don't need to know everything about this agency. You need to know what the work really looks like where you use it: your pace, your tools, your hours. Then you need one honest number next to the one they gave you.

The feeling you've been calling dread is mostly just not having that number.

A different Friday

Picture the same email arriving on a different week. You read it at your desk with a coffee. You don't feel your stomach tighten, because you already know roughly what an hour of your work is worth and what your last dozen jobs actually paid once you counted the hours, the discounts and the rush fees. You know which agencies are quick and which are the ones you chase.

You put this offer next to all of that. It takes a few minutes, not a weekend.

Maybe it's fine, and you say yes with a calm heart because the repeat work really does balance the lower rate. Maybe it isn't, and you write back warmly with a counter-figure and a reason. Maybe you say a polite no, and you don't lie awake over it.

Whichever you choose, it's your answer, not a reflex. By lunchtime the email is sent, and you spend the afternoon on the manual you're actually paid for. At six the laptop closes. The question has stopped sitting at the table with you.

Back at the stall

The knife comes back wrapped in paper. You pay, thank him, and walk home with it in your bag. You write nothing yet. You just have a feeling you've been missing: that this decision can be made on something firmer than a Thursday mood.
