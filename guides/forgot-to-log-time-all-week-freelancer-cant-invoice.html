Forgot to Log Your Time All Week? The Tram on Friday

2026-11-04

The tram is late, and the window beside you is fogged up on the inside.

It is 8:50 on a Friday morning. Mara has her laptop bag on her knees and her phone in one hand. She is a freelance web developer, three years in, and today she has to send an invoice to a client who pays on the first of the month.

The invoice needs hours. She has none.

She opened the timer app on Monday. She remembers that much. It showed one entry, "Homepage fixes, 1h 12m", and a timer that kept running until Tuesday afternoon. She stopped it, felt silly, and never started it again.

Now it is Friday, and the whole week is just a fog.

The week you can't prove

She scrolls through her sent emails, looking for evidence of her own life.

Monday: a long thread about the checkout page. Tuesday: a call, but how long? Forty minutes, or the full hour with the small talk? Wednesday: she remembers a good stretch of work, but was it before or after lunch? Thursday is almost blank. She knows she worked. She knows her shoulders ached by four. She just can't say what she did for how long.

So she does what many people in her position do. She starts to guess.

Thirty-two hours feels right. Or thirty-five. Thirty-five sounds like a number a person makes up, so maybe thirty-three. She types it into the invoice, deletes it, and types thirty-one.

The tram stops. Nobody gets on. The doors hiss and close again.

This is the part nobody warns you about. It isn't only the maths. It's the small, sour feeling of not being sure you're being fair, to the client or to yourself. If you guess high, you worry about the awkward email asking for a breakdown. If you guess low, you give away an afternoon and quietly resent it by Sunday.

What the guessing costs

By the time she reaches the co-working space, Mara has spent forty minutes on a number that should have taken none.

She also notices a quieter cost. When she can't see her hours, she can't tell whether the project is going well. The client asked for "a few small changes" three weeks ago. Is it still a few? Or has it become a second job at the price of a first one? She doesn't know, so she says nothing, which is how undercharging starts. Not with a decision, but with a blank spreadsheet.

She makes tea she doesn't want. She opens the invoice again.

Then, because she is tired of the fog, she tries something different. She stops trying to remember hours and writes down what she can actually see. Which tasks were finished. Which were not. Which calls happened, and which days they landed on. It is slow, and it still involves some guessing, but she ends up with an honest range and a short, plain note for the client: "Hours below are reconstructed from my project notes and calendar."

It isn't perfect. But it is truthful, and that matters to her more than she expected.

She sends the invoice at 11:40. Her stomach doesn't unclench the way it should.

The turning point is small

What changes things for Mara isn't a big resolution. Nobody becomes a different person on a Friday.

It's a thought that arrives while she is waiting for the kettle to boil again: the problem was never that she forgot. Everyone forgets. Freelancers forget because the work itself is absorbing, and that is a good thing. The problem was that remembering was her only system.

A week of work should not depend on how well you can recall Wednesday.

What she wants is not a stricter version of herself. She wants one calm place where a client, a project, the hours and the invoice all live side by side. A place where a missed day is a gap she can see and fill in on Monday morning with her coffee, not a mystery she has to solve on invoice day. A place that tells her, without drama, what is billed, what is open and what is overdue.

A different Friday

Picture the same tram a few weeks later.

It is still late. The window still fogs. But Mara is scrolling something else: a quiet overview that she filled in as she went, a few minutes at a time. The week's hours are there. They are rounded the way she likes, and they match the project. The invoice number follows on from the last one. The due date is already worked out.

She checks one line, nods, and puts the phone away.

At the co-working space she sends the invoice at 9:10, not 11:40. The rest of the morning belongs to the actual work. And when the client asks, a month later, "Can you show me how those hours were spent?", she doesn't feel her pulse in her ears. She just answers.

That is the whole difference. Not more discipline, not a better memory. Just less fog.
