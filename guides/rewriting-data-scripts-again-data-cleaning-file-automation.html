Rewriting Data Scripts Again? The Cottage Week Off

2026-10-15

It's Wednesday, 10:20 in the morning, and the rain is coming sideways off the sea.

You're in a rented cottage with a wood stove, a kettle that takes forever, and one bar of Wi-Fi that only exists next to the bookshelf. This is supposed to be your week off. You told your clients so. You even wrote it in your email signature.

Then the message arrives. Hi, quick one: the supplier export is a mess again. Can you tidy it before Friday's report?

Your stomach does a small, familiar drop.

You have tidied this before. You know you have. Somewhere there is a script that trims the spaces, fixes the dates, drops the duplicates and renames the columns. You wrote it in spring. Or was it winter?

The laptop you didn't bring

You stand by the bookshelf, phone held up like an offering, and try to remember where it lives.

Is it in the project folder called final? Or final_v2? Maybe it's in the one named scripts_old, next to scripts_new, which contains almost nothing. Perhaps it only ever existed on the desktop machine at home, two hundred kilometres away.

Your partner glances up from a crossword. "Everything okay?"

"Yes," you say. "Just a tiny thing."

It is never a tiny thing. A tiny thing is something you can find.

The quiet cost of starting from zero

So you do what you always do. You open the laptop on the sofa, balance it on a cushion, and start again from a blank file.

You write the import line. You look at the columns. You remember, slowly, that this export puts dates in two formats and a stray space after every name. You rediscover the problem the way you'd rediscover a pothole by driving into it.

It isn't hard work. That's the strange part. Nothing here is difficult. It's just again.

Again the small decisions. Again the test on a handful of rows. Again the nagging thought that last time you handled some edge case you've now forgotten, and the report will quietly carry the mistake.

An hour goes. The stove ticks. Outside, a gull argues with the wind.

Nobody talks about this cost. It isn't the hour. It's what the hour does to the rest of the day: the low hum of resentment, the feeling that you're a person who builds clever things and yet keeps rebuilding the same one. It's the faint embarrassment of being the "data person" who can't find their own tools.

And under that, a smaller worry. If a client asked you to run something on their API folder, or to sort three thousand files with random names, would you be ready? Or would you be on a sofa again, starting from nothing?

The walk to the harbour

Around noon you give up and walk to the harbour for a sandwich. The rain has thinned to a mist.

On the way you think about a colleague who once said something offhand. She never fixes the same problem twice. When she solves something, she puts it somewhere sensible, with a name that means something, and a note to her future self. Next time it's a five-minute job.

You'd nodded politely. You'd thought: that's nice for people with time.

But standing at the harbour wall, eating a cheese sandwich with cold fingers, it lands differently. She isn't more disciplined than you. She just stopped treating every chore as new.

It doesn't have to be like this.

The tools aren't the problem. You can write them. You've written them a dozen times. What's missing is a place where they already are: checked, tidy, ready, so that a message like this morning's is a small task and not a ruined afternoon.

A different Wednesday

Picture the same cottage and the same rain.

The message arrives. You read it by the bookshelf, and the drop in your stomach doesn't come. You think: I have something for that.

You open the folder. You know what's in it. You run the cleanup on the export, glance at the result, and send it off with a short, friendly note. Eleven minutes, maybe. Then you close the laptop and put another log in the stove.

The rest of the day belongs to you. You read. You nap badly on the sofa. You argue with the crossword. When your partner asks if everything's okay, you say, "Yes. Completely." And it's true.

That's the whole difference. Not a cleverer you. A calmer one, because the repetitive things are already handled.
