Scared to Raise Salon Prices? The Laminated Price Card

2026-11-04

The price card by the till has curled at one corner.

You laminated it four years ago, on a slow Sunday, with a pretty font and a thin gold border. Cut and blow-dry. Colour, root touch-up. Toner. Children's cut. Every number is still the one you typed when you were nervous and just glad to have your own chair.

It's Thursday, 2:15 pm. Your Thursday regular is under the foils, flicking through a magazine she has read twice. The kettle clicks off in the back. You are standing at the till with a pen in your hand, and you are not writing anything.

The sentence that stops you

A few minutes ago, Marlene looked up from the magazine and said, warmly, the way you say it to someone you've known for nine years:

"Don't ever change, will you? I tell everyone you're the one place that's still fair."

She meant it kindly. It landed like a stone in your stomach.

Because last month the colour supplier raised their prices again. Your chair rent went up in spring. You've been quietly eating the difference, one foil at a time, and telling yourself it's fine, it's only a few euros here and there.

But you know how it feels when the day ends and the drawer isn't as full as the diary looked.

What you don't say out loud

You've rehearsed the conversation in the car. In the shower. In the supermarket queue, staring at the chewing gum.

What if she goes somewhere else? What if they all do? What if the regulars who've sat here through your divorce-haircut, your first Christmas alone in the salon, your "I think I'll go blonder" phase, decide you got greedy?

So you do what many people do. You say nothing. You do the long balayage for the same price as always, because it's Marlene, and because she brings you those little lemon biscuits.

And you go home a bit tired, a bit resentful, and a bit ashamed of the resentment. Which is a strange combination for someone who is genuinely good at her job.

The wrong question

That evening you try to think it through, and you notice something. The question you keep asking is: Will they leave if I charge more?

It's a question about their reaction. You can't answer it. Nobody can, from a kitchen table.

The question you haven't asked is much quieter, and much more answerable: What does each thing I do actually earn me?

Not what you charge. What's left. After the colour that went into the bowl. After the three hours the full head really took, not the two you wrote in the diary. After your share of the rent for the chair you were sitting in.

You realise you don't know. You have a feeling. A gut sense that the quick fringe trim is generous and the long colour days are not. But a feeling is a thin thing to carry into a conversation with someone you like.

The turn

Some weeks later, on a Monday when the salon is closed, you sit down with a coffee and look at your services one at a time. Each one gets its own small line. You see them side by side.

It takes less time than you feared. And something loosens in your chest.

It turns out that most of what you do is fine. Two or three things are quietly paying for the rest. One thing you've been offering as a favour has been costing you money every single time. Not a disaster, not a scandal. Just a number you hadn't looked at, and now can.

You are not greedy. You are a person with a small business who finally can see it.

A different Thursday

The next time you are at the till, the card has been reprinted. New paper, same gold border. No dramatic jump, no sweeping change. Just a few lines that now make sense to you, and that you could explain without your voice going high.

That Thursday, Marlene glances at it while you ring her up.

"Oh. That's gone up a little."

You don't rush to fill the silence. You don't apologise three times. You say, calmly, "It has. Colour costs more than it did, and I wanted to keep the quality where you like it."

She nods. Maybe she's surprised. Maybe she was expecting it. She puts the lemon biscuits on the counter anyway.

Can you be certain that everyone stays? No, and this story won't pretend otherwise. But you stand differently when the number is yours, when you know where it came from. A price you understand is a price you can say out loud.

And on the way home, the supermarket queue is just a queue.
