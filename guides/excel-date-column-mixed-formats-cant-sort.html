Excel Dates Mixed Formats, Can't Sort? The Print Shop

2026-10-23

The ticket number and the laptop

Thursday, 10:32. You're standing at the print shop counter with a paper ticket in your hand. Number 41. They're serving 36.

Your banners are still on the machine. Your laptop is open on the counter, balanced between a stapler and a bowl of free pens.

The event is on Saturday. All you wanted was one simple thing: a list of attendees in order of when they signed up, so you can see who needs a reminder.

You click the sort button on the date column.

Nothing looks sorted. 02/03/2026 sits next to "March 5th". Below it is 2026-03-01. Then a cell that says "last Tuesday", which a person typed into a form field. Then three dates that Excel has quietly left-aligned, which means it doesn't think they're dates at all.

You sort again. The mess just rearranges itself.

You think: it is a date column. How is this hard?

Three platforms, three ideas of a date

Here's the thing nobody says out loud. It isn't your fault, and it isn't really Excel's either.

The ticket platform writes dates one way. The newsletter tool writes them another way. The sign-up form your cousin built last year lets people type whatever they like. You exported all three, pasted them into one sheet, and now the sheet is trying to be polite to every format at once.

Is 04/05 the fourth of May or the fifth of April? It depends on who typed it, which country their laptop thinks it's in, and what mood the export was in that day.

You know this isn't a deep problem. That's what makes it so irritating. It's small, and it's stubborn, and it eats the exact half hour you didn't have.

What it quietly costs

The cost isn't the sorting. It's what happens around it.

You start fixing cells by hand. One, then ten, then you lose your place. You scroll back up and wonder whether you already fixed row 87. You change one date and aren't sure if it was wrong or just looked odd.

Then comes the quieter worry. If you send the reminder to the wrong people, or skip someone who signed up early, it lands with a real person. Someone who paid, who is counting on you, and who will never know it was a date column that went wrong.

So you check twice. Then a third time. The task that should have taken two minutes takes up the whole morning, and your attention, and a bit of your sense that you run something professional.

"Number 37," says the voice at the counter.

You don't look up.

The moment it doesn't have to be like this

The man next to you is waiting for a roll of stickers. He glances at your screen, sees the wall of dates and says, kindly, "That looks like my Thursday."

You laugh, a little too loudly.

He shrugs. "I stopped fixing mine by hand. Now I just run the file through something before I even open it."

That sentence stays with you. Not fix it better. Not learn the right formula. Just: don't open it dirty in the first place.

You realise you've been treating each messy export as a fresh emergency. Every week a different file, and every week you fix the same kinds of mistakes. Dates in five shapes. Names with stray spaces. Duplicates from people who signed up twice because the first confirmation email went to spam.

The mess is always the same mess. You've just never had somewhere to put the fix, so you've been rebuilding it by hand.

A different Thursday

Picture the same morning, with one thing changed.

The exports land in a folder. Before you look at them, a small routine tidies them: dates all in one form, duplicates flagged, odd spaces gone. It shows you what it would change before it changes anything, and it leaves a short report of what it did. You can read it in a minute. You can undo it, because your original file is untouched.

You open the clean sheet and click sort.

It sorts. First sign-up at the top, last at the bottom, in the order things actually happened.

You don't celebrate. You just feel your shoulders drop an inch, the way they do when a knot loosens in a shoelace you'd been cursing at.

Then you do the useful thing. You send the reminders. You check the banners. You have a coffee while it's still warm.

That's the whole difference. Not magic, just a Thursday where the data behaves and your attention goes to the people, not the cells.

And on a normal Monday

The quiet relief carries on. Monday morning, a new export arrives and you don't brace for it. You know roughly what it will look like, and you know what happens to it next.

You stop thinking of yourself as someone who is "bad with spreadsheets". You're someone who runs events, or a shop, or a practice, and who has a tidy place for messy files to pass through on their way to you.

There's a small dignity in that. The kind that doesn't show on the invoice but you feel it all the same.
