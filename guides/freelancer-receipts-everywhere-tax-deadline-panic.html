Receipts Everywhere, Tax Deadline Near? The Cherry Ladder

2026-10-21

It is Saturday, 11:20 in the morning, and Mara is standing on a stepladder in her mother's garden with a bucket of cherries hanging from her elbow.

Her phone buzzes in her back pocket. She knows what it is before she looks. It is the calendar reminder she set three weeks ago and has snoozed four times: Tax documents – accountant.

She is a freelance illustrator. Eight years in. Good clients, steady work, a name people recognise in a small corner of the children's book world. And right now, on top of a ladder, she is quietly doing sums in her head about a carrier bag.

The bag in the boot

The bag is in the boot of her car, behind the picnic blanket. It is from the art supply shop, the paper kind with rope handles. Inside it are receipts. Some are crumpled, some are faded to a ghostly grey. One is stuck to a pack of gum.

There are more in the zip pocket of her winter coat. A few are in the glovebox. A photo of one is somewhere in her phone, between a picture of a heron and a screenshot of a recipe.

She thinks, as she has thought every year: this time I'll do it as I go.

She did not do it as she went.

What it quietly costs

Nobody sees this part. Her mother is below her, holding the bucket steady and talking about the neighbour's fence. Mara nods in the right places. But half of her is somewhere else, in a small, tight room in her chest.

It is not that the amount of paper is huge. It is that she does not know. Is the new tablet in the bag? Did she keep the receipt for the printing run in spring? Was the software subscription charged monthly or yearly, and where did that email go?

The not-knowing is heavier than the work would be.

It shows up in odd places. She is short with her partner on Sunday evenings. She avoids opening the folder on her desktop called Taxes (do not open). She has started to feel, a little, that people who run real businesses have a drawer somewhere that she was never given.

She is not careless. She is just alone with it. There is no back office. There is only her, a ladder, and a date.

The sentence on the ladder

Her mother hands up a second bucket and says, without looking at her, "You've gone quiet. Is it work?"

Mara laughs, a little too fast. "It's receipts. I have a bag of receipts and a deadline."

"Well," says her mother, who ran the village bakery's books for thirty years in a ring binder, "what's in the bag is not the problem. The problem is that it's all still in the bag."

Mara stops with a cherry halfway to the bucket.

It is a simple sentence. It does not tell her what to do. But something loosens. The bag is not a verdict on her. It is just a pile of things that have not yet been given a place to live.

And a place to live is a small thing. It does not need a new career or a perfect system. It needs one calm spot where each receipt lands, one short list of what has to happen before the deadline, and a way to see at a glance how far along she is.

An ordinary Monday, later

Here is how she imagines it, up there among the leaves.

It is a Monday in the second week of the month. The kettle clicks off. She opens one file, and it is open for only a few minutes. A receipt from last Thursday goes in: date, shop, amount, what it was for. She does not have to think about where it goes. There is only one place.

A small bar at the top shows how much of the year-end list is done. It is a little more than half. She feels no panic, just a mild, pleasant sense of oh, good, that's handled.

The date her accountant gave her sits on the page where she can see it. Not hidden. Not screaming. Just there, like a train timetable.

And then she closes the laptop and goes to the market, because it is only ten minutes of admin and the rest of the day belongs to her.

The cherries are very red. The ladder wobbles a little. She climbs down.

The carrier bag, again

That evening she carries the paper bag into the house and puts it on the table, instead of leaving it in the boot. It is a small act. She does not empty it yet. But the bag is in the light now, in the kitchen, with the bowl of cherries next to it, and it looks smaller than it did in the dark.

Her mother, passing with a tea towel, says nothing. She just taps the table twice, the way she used to tap the counter at closing time.

If you have a bag like Mara's, or a coat pocket, or a folder called do not open, you are not behind in some secret race. You are a person working alone, and the work was never given a home. That can change quietly, one receipt at a time.
