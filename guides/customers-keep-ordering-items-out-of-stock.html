Customers Order Things You No Longer Have? The Parcel Locker Queue

2026-10-15

Wednesday, 8:10 am. You are third in line at the parcel drop-off, a box under each arm, and your phone buzzes against the cardboard.

New order. Two tins of the smoked black tea.

Your stomach drops before your brain catches up. You sold the last smoked black tin on Saturday. You are almost sure. Or was it Sunday? There might be one in the cupboard behind the kettle. You can't picture it.

The person ahead of you steps to the counter. You shift the boxes and open the shop on your phone. The listing says In stock, in cheerful green.

It isn't true, and it hasn't been for days.

The sentence you've written too many times

Let's call you Mara. You sell loose-leaf tea from a spare room that smells of bergamot and cardboard. It's a small shop, mostly you, a label printer and a supplier who answers emails when he feels like it.

You know the email you are about to write. You could write it in your sleep, and some nights you do.

Hi, I'm so sorry, it turns out that item is currently unavailable...

The word "turns out" is the painful part. It sounds as if the shop surprised you, and you are the person who runs it.

The stock numbers do exist. They live in three places: the shop dashboard, a notebook by the packing table and your head. Each one is a little bit wrong, in a different way. On a busy week they drift apart like boats on a lake.

So customers order things you no longer have. You apologise, refund and offer a discount code. Sometimes they are kind about it. Sometimes you get a one-line reply that says only ok, and that one stays with you all day.

What it quietly costs

The refund is the small part. The bigger cost is how you start to hold your shoulders.

You open every order notification braced for it. A sale should feel like a tiny celebration, and instead it feels like a pop quiz you haven't studied for.

You also begin to avoid things. You don't run a promotion, because what if it works and you run out? You don't post the lovely photo of the new blend, because you can't be sure of the old ones.

And there's the feeling you rarely say out loud: that a real shop would simply know what is on its shelves.

The queue moves

The counter is free. You hand over the boxes, get your receipt and walk out into the cold, thin morning light.

Sitting in the car, you don't start the engine. You open the notebook photo on your phone, last updated nine days ago. Beside "smoked black" there is a number, crossed out, with another number scrawled beside it, and then a coffee ring where a third number may once have been.

You laugh, a little tired. It isn't a disaster. It's just ridiculous that you have been running a business on a coffee ring.

Then a thought arrives, quieter than the panic. What if I never had to guess?

Not a giant system. Not software with forty menus and a monthly bill. Just one plain place where each product has a number, the number goes down when you sell and up when the delivery arrives, and something gently tells you this one is getting low before a customer finds out for you.

It sounds almost too simple. You decide that is a point in its favour.

A different Wednesday

Picture the same morning, a few weeks on.

You are third in line at the parcel counter again. The phone buzzes. New order, two tins of smoked black.

You glance at it and feel nothing at all. You know there are six on the shelf, and that the next batch is already on its way, because the reminder came last Thursday while you still had plenty of time. No cold drop in the stomach, no apology drafted in your head.

You put the phone away and look at the noticeboard on the wall. A lost cat. Someone's piano lessons. You notice that you are just standing in a queue, an ordinary person on an ordinary morning.

Back home, the packing table is clear. The notebook is still there, but now it holds ideas for a new blend rather than numbers you no longer trust. The dashboard says in stock and, for once, it means it.

You might even run that promotion.

The quiet part nobody mentions

What changes most is not the money, though fewer refunds help. It is the way you stand in your own shop. You stop bracing and start offering. You answer a customer's question about availability with a plain, confident "yes, it's here" and no hidden "I think."

That is what being a professional often feels like from the inside: not grand, just steady, with the numbers where you can see them.
