Client Wants an Hours Breakdown? The Glasshouse Bench

2026-10-25

It is Thursday, 11:15. You are sitting on a bench in the botanical garden glasshouse, because your sister's birthday walk starts here and you are early.

Your phone buzzes. It is Jonas, your client for the last four months.

Could you send a breakdown of the hours on the last invoice? Just so I can justify it internally. Thanks!

You read it twice. It is polite. It is reasonable. And your stomach drops anyway.

The breakdown you don't have

You know you worked those hours. You remember the long Tuesday of revisions. You remember the call that ran over. You remember the weekend you didn't really have.

What you don't have is a list.

You have a calendar with half the meetings in it. You have an inbox with timestamps. You have a notes file called "jonas_stuff" that stops in the middle of month two. And you have a number on an invoice that you arrived at by feeling your way back through the weeks.

The warm, damp air of the glasshouse suddenly feels a bit thick.

This is the worry nobody says out loud. It isn't that you cheated anyone. It's that the work is real, and you cannot show it. If someone asks "what exactly did you do?", you have to reconstruct the answer like a detective. And a reconstruction always sounds a little less sure than a record.

What it quietly costs

The request itself is small. The cost is everything around it.

You don't answer right away, because you want to answer well. So the message sits there. You think about it while the others admire the orchids. You think about it in the café afterwards, while someone asks if you want a slice of cake and you say yes without tasting it.

Later you will open the calendar and the sent folder side by side. You will add things up, round them, doubt the rounding, and add them again. You will write a reply that is a little too long and a little too apologetic. Then you will delete the apology and put it back.

And under all of it sits a smaller, meaner thought: a real business would just know.

You already know that isn't true. Plenty of capable people work this way. But in the moment it still feels like a verdict on you, not on your system.

The pause on the bench

So you sit there with your thumb over the reply button, and you try something different. You don't write anything. You ask yourself one plain question.

What would make this email easy?

The answer is almost boring. If, each day, you had written down a line, Tuesday, Jonas, landing page revisions, 3.5 h, then this message would take two minutes. You'd copy a list. You'd attach it. You'd feel the way you feel when someone asks for your phone number and you simply know it.

The problem was never your honesty or your effort. It was that the hours lived in your memory, and memory is a poor filing cabinet. It is lovely for faces and terrible for Tuesdays.

That is the turning point, and it is a gentle one. Nothing is wrong with you. A small thing was missing: one place where each client, each project and each stretch of hours sits next to the invoice it belongs to.

A different Thursday

Imagine the same message arriving, but into a different week.

The hours were entered as you went. Not perfectly, not every day, but mostly. A minute here, a minute there, usually while the kettle boiled. The invoice you sent was built from those entries, and the two have never been strangers.

Jonas asks for a breakdown. You open one file. You find his name, his project, the dates, the hours. You see which invoice they were part of and whether it has been paid. You send it with one friendly line: Of course, here it is. Let me know if anything needs clarifying.

Then you put the phone back in your pocket and look at the orchids.

The whole thing takes three minutes. And something more important happens. Jonas reads a clear list and thinks, this person is organised. The question that felt like suspicion turns out to be ordinary housekeeping, and you answered it like someone who runs a real business. Because you do.

A quieter way to work

None of this needs to be heavy. It doesn't take a complicated app or a new identity as a person who loves timesheets. It takes one calm place where clients, projects, hours and invoices live together, so the story of your work is already written when someone asks to read it.

Then "can you send a breakdown?" is just a request, not a small alarm. You close the laptop at six. Your evenings stay yours.
