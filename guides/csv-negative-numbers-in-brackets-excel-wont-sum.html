CSV Brackets for Negatives Won't Sum? The Pickup Line

2026-11-08

It is 14:48 on a Thursday and you are third in the school pickup line, engine off, laptop balanced on the steering wheel.

You have twelve minutes. Maybe fourteen if the bus is late.

On the screen is the CSV your accounting software exported this morning. You only want one number: what came in and what went out last quarter. You click the bottom of the column, glance at the status bar and read: Sum: 0.

Zero. You know that is wrong. You paid for that conference stand. You refunded two customers. There was a very real, very painful hosting bill.

You look closer. The refunds and costs do not have minus signs. They look like this: (120.00). (49.90). (1,240.00).

Brackets. Everything in brackets is left-aligned, a little too polite, and Excel is treating all of it as text. It will not add it up, and it will not tell you why.

The little number that won't behave

You try the obvious things. You retype one cell with a minus sign, and that one row starts working. There are 340 rows.

You think about doing it by hand. You think about the line behind you, the clock, and the fact that the person who asked for this number is waiting on a call at four.

Then comes the thought that always arrives in these moments, quieter than the rest: a real business would have this sorted.

It isn't true. Plenty of real businesses have exactly this column, in exactly this state, right now. Accounting tools write brackets because that is how accountants read a page. Excel reads that same page and sees a word. Neither one is wrong, and they simply never agreed on a language.

But at 14:51, with a pickup line creeping forward, it does not feel like a language problem. It feels like you are bad at this.

What it quietly costs

The number itself is rarely the real cost. The real cost is everything around it.

The Sunday you spend re-typing negatives so the quarter finally adds up. The month you hand your bookkeeper a file you privately don't trust. The small sinking feeling when a total looks lovely and you can't say whether it is right.

And the repeat. Brackets come back every export, with a slightly different shape each time. Last month it was a stray space after the bracket. This month there is a currency symbol hiding in the amount column. You fix it again, and it is back again in four weeks.

None of this is dramatic. It is just a slow tax on your attention, paid in twenty-minute pieces, in car parks and queues and the minutes before a call.

The doors open. Your child climbs in, bag first, talking about a lizard. You close the laptop with the total still wrong and say "Tell me everything," because that part, at least, you can do properly.

The moment it clicked

Later, with the kitchen quiet, you look at the file again. Calmer, you notice something you missed in the car.

The brackets are not random. Every single one follows the same pattern. The file is not messy in a creative way. It is messy in a boringly consistent way, and anything consistent can be handled by a machine, once, and then left alone.

That is the turning point, and it is small. You don't need to become someone who loves spreadsheets. You need the file to arrive already readable, the way a good colleague hands over a tidy folder. The same goes for the other quirks that came with it: the doubled rows, the dates that flip between day-first and month-first, the phone numbers written four different ways.

You also notice what you actually want, which is not clever technology. You want to see what changed before it changes. You want to know that nothing was quietly overwritten and that your original file is still sitting there, untouched, if you ever doubt it.

A different Thursday

Picture the same pickup line, three weeks from now.

The export went through its cleaning step this morning, before you had finished your coffee. A short report listed what it had touched: 62 amounts in brackets turned into proper negatives, 9 duplicate rows flagged, nothing deleted without showing you first.

You open the file at 14:48 out of habit. The column is right-aligned. The sum says what you expected, give or take a rounding thought, and you recognise the figure the way you recognise your own handwriting.

You send the number before the bus arrives. Then you put the laptop on the back seat, wind the window down a few centimetres, and listen to the engine tick as it cools.

Nobody congratulates you. Nothing about it is exciting. That is exactly the point: a piece of your week stopped being a worry.
