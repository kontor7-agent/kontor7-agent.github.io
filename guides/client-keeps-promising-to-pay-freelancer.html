Client Keeps Promising to Pay? The Library Printer Queue

2026-10-16

Jonas is standing in the queue at the library printing desk, holding a contract he needs to sign and scan. His laptop is open on a table behind him. In the other hand, his phone.

The message from his client is still on the screen. "Hi Jonas! Payment goes out Friday, promise. Sorry for the delay!"

It's the third promise. The first one said "end of the month". The second said "early next week". This one says Friday, with an exclamation mark.

He reads it again. He's a video editor. He knows how to cut a ten-minute interview down to ninety seconds. He cannot work out how to cut through this one sentence.

The kind of friendly that costs you

The invoice was for 1,800. It isn't a fortune, but it is rent plus the insurance that comes out on the first. He has done the maths so often it has worn a groove in his head.

What bothers him isn't the money alone. It's the tone. Every message from this client is warm. Nobody is rude. Nobody says no. Nobody says anything that would let him push back, because there is nothing to push against. A promise is a soft wall.

So he does what a lot of people who work for themselves do. He answers with a smiley. "No problem, thanks for letting me know!"

Then he feels a small, familiar sinking. He has just thanked someone for not paying him.

What the waiting actually does

The queue moves up one place. A man ahead of him is printing what looks like a very long family tree.

Jonas scrolls through his inbox while he waits. He can't remember when he first sent the reminder. Was it the 3rd or the 5th? Did he write "friendly reminder" or "second reminder"? There's a draft from last week called Re: Invoice, three lines long, deleted and rewritten twice, never sent. It sounds either too nice or too angry. There is no middle.

So the days go by like this:

  • He checks the bank app before breakfast, "just to see".
  • He takes a smaller project than he'd like because he can't count on the 1,800.
  • He says yes to a quick favour for the same client, because refusing feels risky when you're also waiting on their payment.

Nobody sees this. It looks like a normal week from outside. Inside, part of his attention is parked on one invoice, like a browser tab he can't close.

The sentence he finally says out loud

A woman at the next table is on a call, speaking quietly but clearly. Jonas isn't trying to listen. He catches only a few words: "I'd like to confirm the new date in writing, and then I'll follow up on the day if it hasn't arrived."

That's it. No apology. No anger. Just a date, in writing, and a plan for what happens next.

He stands there with his contract and thinks: that's what I've been missing. Not a harsher voice. A calmer one, with a next step already decided.

Until now every reminder felt like a fresh decision. Should he write now or wait? Is it too soon? Too pushy? Will they take it badly? He has been reinventing the same awkward email every time, and every time the awkwardness won, so he didn't send it.

What if that part didn't have to be decided in the moment at all? What if there was a rhythm: a gentle note on the due date, a clearer one a week later, a firm one after that, and he always knew which one was next? Not a fight. Just a sequence, the way a good invoice is a sequence.

The librarian calls him forward. He prints, signs, scans. It takes four minutes. He has been dreading the email for four weeks.

A different Monday

Picture the same client, the same slow-paying habits, but a different Jonas.

It's Monday, 8:40. He opens one sheet, a simple overdue list. It shows him, without any thinking, that one invoice is nine days late and the next step is the second reminder. The wording is already written, polite and clear, with the invoice number and the date in the right places. He changes a name, reads it once, and sends it.

It takes two minutes. There's no smiley of surrender in it, and no threat either. It reads like a business that expects to be paid, which is exactly what it is.

Then he closes the sheet and starts on a new edit. The browser tab in his head is gone. If the money arrives Friday, good. If not, he knows what Monday looks like, and it isn't a night of drafting and deleting.

The client might still pay late. But Jonas is no longer the one carrying the awkwardness alone. The awkwardness has been turned into a process, and a process doesn't take things personally.

When chasing stops feeling like begging

There's a thing nobody tells you when you start working for yourself: asking for what you're owed is part of the job, not a favour you request. It just doesn't feel that way when every reminder is a blank page and a racing pulse.

Most of the discomfort isn't about the client at all. It's about not knowing what to say, or when, or what comes after. Take away the guessing and a good part of the dread goes with it.

You don't need to become tougher. You need the next sentence to be ready before you need it.
