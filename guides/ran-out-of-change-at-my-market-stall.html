Ran Out of Change at Your Market Stall? The Bakery Dash

2026-10-27

It is 10:40 on a Sunday, and the Advent market is finally busy.

There are three people at your stall. One is holding a linocut print of a heron. One is turning over a pack of cards. One is waiting with a 50-euro note, politely, for a 4.50 card.

You open the cash box. Two-cent coins. A single five. A lot of receipts from last month that you never took out.

Your stomach does the small drop you know well.

The sentence you hear yourself say

"I'm so sorry, I'll just run and get change. Two minutes!"

You ask the woman at the next stall to watch your table. She smiles and says of course, but you can see her own customer waiting. You walk fast past the mulled wine and the bratwurst, toward the bakery on the corner, with your face arranged into everything is fine.

The baker looks at the note and sighs. "I'm low on change myself, it's Sunday." He gives you what he can. You buy a pretzel you don't want, just to make the exchange feel fair.

When you get back, the heron print is gone from the table. So is the person who was holding it. The woman with the 50 is still there, patient, and kind about it. The pack-of-cards person has wandered off.

You sell the card. You say thank you twice too often. And for the rest of the morning, a quiet voice keeps up a running commentary.

A real business would have had a float.

It was never just the change

Here is the part nobody says out loud at a market. Running out of change is rarely only about change.

It is about the Saturday before, when you packed the car at 22:00 and told yourself you'd count the float in the morning. It is about the notebook in your bag that has some of the sales, and the phone notes that have the rest. It is about not quite knowing how many of the heron prints you brought, so you couldn't say whether the one that left was your last.

It is about a customer at 11:30 who says, "Could you do a custom one, a print of our dog, for a gift?" And you hear yourself answer "Oh, around thirty?", a number that comes from nowhere, from a feeling, from fear of sounding expensive.

You drive home at 16:30 with a tired back and a cash box that feels heavy, but you honestly don't know if it was a good day. There was money. Of course there was money. But the stall fee, the paper, the ink, the card sleeves, the two hours you spent carving last Tuesday, the petrol, the pretzel: where do they go? You tip the coins onto the kitchen table and count them, and the number looks fine and tells you nothing.

That is the quiet cost. Not the lost sale. The not knowing.

The neighbour with the small tin

Three weeks later, you are at a smaller market. It is drizzling. The stall next to yours belongs to a man who sells hand-bound notebooks, and you have been nodding at each other since March.

At 9:10, before the first customer, he does something you've never noticed before. He opens a tin, counts the float into neat little stacks, glances at a single page on his tablet, and closes both. It takes about two minutes. Then he pours the tea.

"You always look like you're braced for something," he says. He isn't unkind about it.

You laugh. Then you ask what's on the tablet.

"Just where everything stands," he says. "What I brought, what I'd like in the till, what each thing actually earns me once I count the stall fee and my time. Saves me the Sunday-night maths."

He doesn't try to sell you anything. He doesn't give you a lecture. He just says it the way you'd say you keep spare keys in the same drawer.

But something turns over in your head. It doesn't have to feel like this. The braced shoulders aren't a personality trait. They're what happens when the numbers live in six places and your memory has to hold them together.

A different kind of market morning

Picture it a few months from now.

It is 9:00. The stall is up. The float is counted, with small notes and coins in the right quantities, because you decided that on Thursday, not at the bakery counter. You know how many heron prints you brought, and you know which two designs always go first, so those are at the front.

A customer asks about a custom dog portrait. You smile, take a breath, and give a number you can stand behind. You're not guessing. You know what an hour of your carving is worth, and the number already includes it.

The woman with the 50 comes by at 10:40. You give her change without ceremony and wrap the card in tissue.

At 16:30, you pack the car and you know whether it was a good day. Not roughly. Actually. You still carry the tired back, because markets are tiring. But the heavy feeling isn't there.

That is what a simple system changes, mostly. Not the work. The weight of carrying the numbers in your head.
