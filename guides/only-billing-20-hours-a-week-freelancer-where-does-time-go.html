Only Billing 20 Hours a Week? The Weekly Summary Ping

2026-11-07

The tram is crowded on Monday mornings, and Mara has the standing spot by the doors, one hand on the rail, one thumb on her phone.

A notification slides down. Your weekly summary is ready.

She almost swipes it away. Then she taps it, because some part of her already knows.

Seven hours of screen time on Tuesday. Six on Wednesday. Saturday, which she remembers as a day off, shows two hours and forty minutes.

Then she thinks of the invoice she sent on Friday. Twenty hours. A tidy number, a tidy total.

The tram brakes. Someone's shopping bag bumps her knee. Mara stares at the two numbers and thinks: Where did the rest go?

The week that felt full

Mara is a freelance web designer. Four years in. She would tell you she is busy, and it would be true.

She is not lazy and she is not disorganised, whatever the small voice says at 2 am. She answers emails before breakfast. She takes calls in the stairwell. She fixes a client's contact form on a Sunday because it was only ten minutes.

But when she tries to remember the week, it comes back in pieces. A long call with a new lead who never replied. An hour hunting for a logo file a client swore they had sent. A quote that took most of Wednesday morning. A "quick question" that turned into a quick answer, then a quick fix, then a quick revision.

None of it was on an invoice. All of it was work.

The part nobody says out loud

What stings is not the maths. It is the feeling underneath.

If you are only billing twenty hours, are you really working full time? Is it your fault? Should you be faster, sharper, more professional? A friend with a salaried job once said, lightly, "Must be nice to work just half the week." Mara laughed along and then thought about it all the way home.

So she does what many of us do. She works more. She skips lunch. She says yes to the small job at a low price because at least it is something. The twenty hours stay twenty hours, and the week gets longer around them.

There is a quieter cost too. When you do not know where your time goes, you cannot tell a client why your rate is what it is. You say the number and your voice drops at the end, like a question.

Ten minutes on a Friday afternoon

Nothing dramatic happens. No guru, no sudden revelation on a mountain.

On Friday, a few days after the tram, Mara sits in the café two doors from her flat. Flat white, laptop, the window open a crack. She does one small, slightly uncomfortable thing: she writes down the week as it actually was.

Not as it should have been. As it was.

Billed client work in one column. Everything else in another. The sales call. The file hunt. The quote. The invoice she sends and the one she forgets to send. The bank-holiday email thread.

She expects to feel ashamed. Instead, something odd happens. The knot loosens a little.

Because the second column is not laziness. It is a job. It is the part of running a business that nobody pays you for directly, and it has been quietly sitting there the whole time, unnamed. It is real. It takes real hours. And the price she charges for the billable hours has to carry it, along with the weeks off, the slow month, the software, and the tax she has to put aside.

She had been pricing as if twenty billable hours were the whole story. They were only the part you could see.

What a normal Monday looks like now

The following Monday, Mara is on the same tram, same spot by the doors.

The summary pings again. This time she does not flinch. She knows roughly what it will say, and she knows what the numbers mean. Some of those hours are client work. Some are the business itself. She has a rough idea of how many of each she needs, and what her hour has to be worth for the whole thing to add up.

On a call that afternoon, a new client asks, "And what's your rate?"

Mara says the number. Her voice stays level at the end of the sentence. There is a short pause, the kind that used to make her rush in with a discount, and she lets it sit. She is not guessing. She has done the sums, in a calm hour, for herself.

The invoice goes out that evening, clean and printable, with the due date right there on it. She closes the laptop at six and walks to the shop for bread, and nothing in her head is still running.

If you recognised yourself

Mara is an illustration, not a real person, and the numbers in her week are made up to make a point. But if the weekly summary on your phone has ever made you wonder where your time went, you are in good company.
