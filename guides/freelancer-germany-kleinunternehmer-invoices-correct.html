Kleinunternehmer Invoice Correct? The Question on Line 2

2026-11-03

It is Tuesday, 11:05. You are standing at the window with your phone, because the call came from a number you don't know.

"Hi, I'm from accounting at Brandt & Co. Quick question about your invoice from last month. Could you tell me why there's no VAT line?"

You say something calm. "Of course, one moment." Inside, a small alarm goes off.

You are a Kleinunternehmer. You know that. You chose it, or your accountant suggested it, or you read about it at 1 am in your first month. But now someone is asking about it out loud, and you realise you can't picture the invoice in your head. Did it have the right note? Is the number correct? Was it 2026-014 or 2026-015?

Let's call you Lena. She's an illustrator in Leipzig. This isn't a real person, just a scene many freelancers in Germany would recognise.

The folder with six versions

Lena opens the laptop while the accountant waits politely on the line. The invoices live in a folder. Rechnung_Brandt.docx. Rechnung_Brandt_neu.docx. Rechnung_Brandt_final2.pdf.

She finds the PDF. The numbering has a gap. She isn't sure whether that's a mistake or a draft she never sent. The layout looks like the one from two years ago, back when she copied an old template from a friend.

"I'll send you the corrected version today," she hears herself say. She doesn't even know yet whether anything needs correcting.

She hangs up. Her hands are slightly cold.

The worry nobody says out loud

It isn't really about this one invoice. It's the quiet thought underneath: what if I've been doing this wrong the whole time?

So she scrolls through the rest. Which invoices went out? Which are paid? She thinks Brandt paid in September, but then she sees an open amount in her bank app that might be theirs, or might be someone else's.

It's the feeling of not being a real business. You make good work, and you still can't answer a plain question about your own paperwork without a pause.

The afternoon goes to this. The illustration she was supposed to finish stays half-done. In the evening she's tired in the way that has nothing to do with drawing.

The turning point is a boring one

The next morning she doesn't look for a better word processor. She asks herself a simpler question: why does every invoice start from a different place?

Nothing was wrong with her care. She cared a lot. What was missing was one home for it all: one list where every invoice gets its number automatically, where the due date sits next to the amount, where "open", "paid" and "overdue" are visible at a glance. And where the hours she worked live in the same place as the invoice they ended up on.

She also remembers something sensible. What exactly belongs on a Kleinunternehmer invoice is a question for the Finanzamt or a Steuerberater. A template can keep things tidy and consistent, but it can't be her tax professional. She writes the question on a note for her next appointment, and the knot loosens a little. She knows who to ask now, and where to find her own numbers when she does.

A different Tuesday

Fast-forward a few weeks. Same Tuesday, same 11:05, same unknown number.

"Quick question about your invoice from last month."

Lena doesn't go to the window. She stays at her desk, a cup of tea at her elbow. One click, and the invoice is right there: number, date, amount, status. The hours behind it are one tab away. She reads out what she sees, calmly, in a normal voice.

If the accountant needs something added, she knows what to ask her Steuerberater and she knows what to send. If not, the call takes four minutes.

Afterwards she finishes the illustration. She sits for a moment before opening the next file, because nothing is quietly burning in the background.

Monday mornings feel different too. She opens one sheet, sees what's open, what's due this week, and who is a bit overdue. Ten minutes, a glance at the overview, and the week starts. At six the laptop closes. The evening is hers.
