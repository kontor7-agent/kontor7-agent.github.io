Customer Wanted What You're Out Of? The Touchline Flask

2026-10-25

It is Saturday, ten past ten, and it is drizzling on a school football pitch.

You are holding a flask of tea in one hand and your phone in the other. Your nephew's under-nines are losing 2-1, and a dad beside you is shouting something kind and useless about "shape".

The phone buzzes. It's a message from a regular customer, the one with the two beagles.

"Hi! Can I collect three bags of the liver and sweet potato treats tomorrow? Visiting my sister, they're a hit with her dog too."

You smile. Then you stop smiling.

You picture the shelf in the back room. You picture the gap where the liver treats go. You are almost sure there are some left. Or you sold the last ones on Thursday. Or you meant to reorder them, wrote it on the back of an envelope, and the envelope is somewhere in the van.

The answer you can't give

The easy reply is "Of course!" Your thumbs are already over the keyboard.

The true reply is "Let me check." That means a trip to the back room and a count you will do slightly wrong because your mind is on the game.

You type, "Yes, no problem!" and delete it. You type, "Let me look and come back to you." You delete that too, because it sounds like you don't know your own shop.

That is the quiet part nobody mentions. It isn't only the lost sale. It is the small, hot feeling of not being a real business. A real business knows what is on its shelves. You know roughly. Roughly is fine until someone is standing at your door tomorrow.

On the pitch, someone scores. You miss it.

What roughly costs

You have been here before. Last month it was the chicken jerky, and you apologised for two days. The month before, you promised a café a bulk order and found out on Sunday night that the supplier needed ten days.

None of these were disasters. Nobody shouted. But each one left a small mark:

  • an apology you didn't want to write
  • a customer who stopped ordering ahead
  • a Sunday spent counting boxes instead of walking the dogs you sell treats for

And there is the money you never see. Treats sit unsold in a box you forgot about, while the favourite runs out. You don't know which is which, because the knowledge lives in your head, in a notebook, and on that envelope.

You finish the tea. It has gone cold and tastes of the flask.

The sentence on the touchline

The game ends. The kids are handed orange slices. Your sister-in-law is laughing at something, and you realise you haven't heard a word of it.

You walk to the car with your phone still unanswered, and a plain thought arrives. It isn't dramatic.

It shouldn't be this hard to know what I have.

Not "I should be more organised", which you have told yourself for years and which never fixed anything. Just: the information exists. Every sale, every delivery, every box. It is only scattered. If it lived in one calm place, with a gentle nudge when something runs low, the question "Do I have three bags?" would take four seconds. It wouldn't take a trip to the back room and a guess.

You answer the customer from the car park. "Checking the shelf, back to you in ten minutes!" It is honest, and it buys you the time you need.

There are two bags. You offer her those, plus the chicken ones as a second option. She says, "Perfect, thank you!" and that is that. But you notice how much of your Saturday that one message had taken.

A different Monday

Picture the same week, with the stock sorted.

Monday, quarter to nine. You open one file with your coffee. Most things sit in a quiet colour. Two items are flagged: the liver treats and the small pouches. That's all it takes to see that the supplier order should go out today, not whenever you remember.

You send the order. You close the file. It took the time of a coffee.

On Saturday, the beagle owner's message arrives while you are on the touchline. This time you glance at your phone, already know the answer, and write, "Three bags, ready from noon tomorrow." Then you look up, because the kids are about to take a corner, and you actually see it.

Nothing about the shop changed except that it stopped surprising you.

That is the whole point. You don't need a warehouse system or a new hobby. You need to stop holding the whole shelf in your head.
