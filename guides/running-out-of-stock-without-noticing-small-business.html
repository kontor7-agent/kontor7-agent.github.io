Running Out of Stock Without Noticing? The Hill Climb

2026-10-19

You are standing at the top of a hill with your bike against a fence, and your legs are still shaking.

It is 10:40 on a Sunday. The view is exactly what your friend promised: fields, a church tower, a thin line of river. You have been looking forward to this ride for two weeks.

Then your phone buzzes in your jersey pocket.

It is not a big buzz. It is a customer email. "Hi, just checking on my order of the smoked chilli sauce, it still says processing?"

Your stomach drops before your brain catches up. You know the answer. You think you know the answer. The last bottles went out on Thursday, didn't they? Or was it Friday? You remember seeing a half-empty box in the spare room. Or you remember meaning to look.

The view you couldn't enjoy

Your friend is talking about the descent. You nod. You are not there anymore. You are in the spare room, counting shelves in your head.

This is the part nobody talks about when they say "I run a small shop." It isn't the big disasters. It is the low hum of not quite knowing. Is there enough? Is the supplier's lead time two weeks or three? Did that wholesale order take more than you thought?

So you hold the whole shelf in your head. Everything. All the time. Even on a hilltop.

And when your memory is off by a few bottles, a customer finds out before you do. That is the worst version of it. They paid, they waited, and they are the one writing to you.

What a quiet mistake costs

You ride down slower than you wanted to. At the bottom, at a petrol station, you sit on the kerb with a coffee and open your laptop bag, because there is no way you can leave this until tomorrow.

The count is worse than you thought. Not zero, but the order in the queue needs six bottles and you have four. The two customers who ordered Friday are the ones who will wait. You write the email. You rewrite it. You offer a refund before they ask, because it feels less embarrassing than making them ask.

It isn't a catastrophe. It is just a small leak, repeating:

  • the apology you write on a day that was meant to be yours
  • the refund that eats the margin on an order you worked for
  • the quiet feeling that a "real" shop would have seen this coming

That last one is the heaviest, and the least fair. You are one person. You pack, photograph, answer emails and cook the sauce. Of course the shelf slips out of your head sometimes. A head is a bad place to keep a shelf.

The question your friend asks

Back home, your friend, who has never run a shop in her life, asks the simple question while you are locking the bikes.

"Can't it just tell you when you're getting low?"

You open your mouth to say it's more complicated than that. Different lead times, different suppliers, a jar that takes three days and a label that takes three weeks. Then you close it again.

Because actually, the answer is: yes. That is all you have ever wanted. Not a big system. Not software with twelve menus and a monthly bill. Just one calm page that knows what you have, knows what usually takes how long to come back, and quietly says this one, soon before the customer's email does.

A Monday that feels different

Imagine a Monday where you don't start with dread.

You make your coffee. You open one sheet. You look at it for about two minutes. Two items are marked for reorder, one is fine, and the rest are comfortably full. You send the supplier message you would have sent anyway, just before the shelf was empty instead of after.

Nothing dramatic happens. That is the whole point. Orders go out. The spare room is only a spare room again, and not a place you mentally patrol.

The next time you are on a hilltop and your phone buzzes, it is just a customer saying thanks. Or it is nothing, and you actually look at the view. You can see the river, the tower, the long line of the road you just climbed.

The shelf stops surprising you. You notice how much room that frees up in your head.
