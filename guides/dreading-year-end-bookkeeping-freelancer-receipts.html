Dreading Year-End Bookkeeping? No Idea Where Your Receipts Are

2026-10-14

The Lobby Nobody Warns You About

It is 9:52 on a Thursday morning in mid-November. You are sitting in a video call lobby, waiting for a client who is, as usual, four minutes late.

Your face looks back at you from the preview window. Behind it, a notification slides in from your calendar:

Year-end: gather receipts???

Three question marks. You typed them yourself in October, when you were feeling brave. Then you snoozed it. Then you snoozed it again.

The lobby music is a thin little piano loop. You tap Dismiss before the client joins, and a small, cold thought arrives uninvited: I have no idea where half of them are.

The Map in Your Head

You try to build the map, right there in the lobby, while the piano loops.

The laptop bag has a few. You're fairly sure of that. The software subscription receipts are somewhere in your email, though you can't say which folder, or whether you ever made one. There was the external monitor in spring, so a box, a paper slip, maybe a PDF, and honestly maybe all three in different places.

Then there are the train tickets to that one client in another city. The printer ink. The conference you paid for and half attended. The coffee-shop afternoons you could never quite decide counted as work.

Some receipts are photos on your phone, between a picture of a dog and a screenshot of a recipe. Some are crumpled in a coat pocket you haven't checked since March. Some you never kept at all, and you know it, and you push that thought down with the rest.

The client joins. You smile and say, "Good morning, good to see you." Your voice is perfectly steady.

Underneath it, the knot sits where it always sits this time of year.

What the Dread Actually Costs

The call goes fine. It's the dread that is expensive, and in small ways nobody sends you an invoice for.

It costs you a certain kind of Sunday afternoon, the one where you are not working but not resting either, because a vague job is hovering. It costs you a little confidence, because a real business, you tell yourself, would know where its paperwork is. It costs you the quiet feeling of winging it.

And in January it usually costs you a whole weekend. You know the one. Every drawer opened, every bag emptied onto the floor, the phone camera roll scrolled with a thumb that gets slower and sadder. You find things you'd forgotten. You don't find things you're sure existed. You promise yourself, with total sincerity, that next year will be different.

Then next year arrives and the reminder says receipts???, and you snooze it.

The Small Realisation

Here is the thing you notice, a few days later, while standing in the queue at the post office with a parcel under your arm.

The problem was never that you're disorganised. You were never careless. You were busy, and you had no single place for any of it to go. Every receipt arrived at a different door, and nobody was standing at any of them.

The queue shuffles forward. You think about how you don't dread your client work this way. You know where it lives. A project has a name, a status, an end. It's the loose, homeless things that follow you around.

What if the receipts simply had one home? Not a grand system, just a place where each one lands the moment it appears, so that the end of the year is a glance and not a dig.

It is a small thought. It makes your shoulders drop about an inch.

A Different Thursday in November

Picture the same Thursday, a year on. You are in the same lobby with the same thin piano loop.

The reminder slides in, and this time it says something plain: Year-end checklist: 6 of 14 done. You aren't panicking. You just know. The receipts from the year are already in one place, with dates and amounts and a note on what each was for. The things still to do are a short list with deadlines beside them, not a fog.

The client joins four minutes late. You say good morning and mean it.

When the call ends you don't feel the knot, because there is no knot. You make tea. You answer two emails. In January, the weekend you used to lose is simply yours. Maybe you spend an hour with your accountant or tax professional, who gets a tidy list instead of a carrier bag.

Nothing dramatic happened. A homeless pile got a home, and you stopped carrying it in your head.
