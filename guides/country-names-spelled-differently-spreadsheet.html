Country Names Spelled Differently in Excel? The Globe

2026-11-06

It's Tuesday, 14:05. You're in a rented phone booth at the coworking space, the kind with a door that doesn't quite close. On the shelf behind the screen sits a small, slightly dusty globe someone left there years ago.

Your client, a small tea importer, has joined the call. You share your screen. You've built them a sales-by-country overview, and you were proud of it this morning.

Then she leans forward on camera and says, "Why are there four Americas?"

You look. She's right. USA. United States. U.S.A. United States of America. Underneath them, in the same column, sit Germany, Deutschland, DE and one that is just germany with a space after it, invisible to the eye and very visible to the pivot table.

Your stomach does the small drop you know well.

"That's… the export," you say. "I'll tidy it up."

She nods kindly. That's almost worse.

The column nobody chose

Nobody typed those spellings on purpose. The shop's checkout let customers type their country by hand for two years. A marketplace export used two-letter codes. A newsletter tool preferred full names. A customer in Vienna wrote Österreich, another wrote Austria, and a third wrote Autria and still got her parcel, because the postman is cleverer than any spreadsheet.

So the data is honest, but it isn't consistent. And a total by country is only as good as the weakest spelling in the column.

You finish the call. You say the right things. You promise a corrected version by the end of the day.

Then you sit in the booth for a moment longer, looking at the globe, feeling like someone who was handed a very tidy-looking mess.

What it quietly costs

The fix itself isn't dramatic. It's the afternoon it eats.

You filter the column and scroll. You find the one with the trailing space. You find Untied States. You find a cell that says UK above one that says United Kingdom and one that says England, and you stop, because that last one is a decision, not a typo, and you don't want to make it alone at 15:30 with a deadline.

You do a find-and-replace, then another. You notice that the totals shift slightly each time, and you can't quite remember what you changed first.

That's the part nobody mentions. It isn't just the hours. It's the small doubt afterwards. Did I fix everything? Did I break something? If she asks again, can I say exactly what I touched?

By five you have a clean-looking report. You send it with a cheerful note. You also close the laptop with a faint worry that it'll come back next month, when the next export arrives with fresh creative spellings.

And it will. Exports always do.

The quiet turning point

A week later you're talking to a friend who works with data, at a kitchen table with two mugs of tea going cold.

You tell her the story, half laughing, about the four Americas. She doesn't laugh. She nods the way people nod when they've been there.

"Do you still fix those by hand?" she asks.

You say yes, and that it's just part of the job.

"It doesn't have to be," she says. "The same mess, the same cleanup, every month. That's something a small script can do while you drink your tea. And it can show you what it would change before it changes anything."

That last sentence stays with you. Not fast. Not clever. Just: before it changes anything. The thing you were missing wasn't speed. It was being able to see the changes and trust them.

An ordinary Tuesday, later

Picture the same Tuesday, a few months on.

The export lands in your folder at 08:50. You run your cleanup the way you'd start a kettle. You glance at the change report: a short list of what was merged, what was trimmed, and what it wasn't sure about and left for you to decide.

One line says England was left alone. Good. That one is yours to decide, and now you're deciding it with a coffee, not with a deadline breathing down your neck.

At 14:05 you're in the same phone booth, the same globe on the shelf, the same client on the screen. You share your overview. One America. One Germany. One Austria, even the one from Vienna.

She scrolls, says "That looks right," and moves straight on to the question she actually called about: which markets are worth a bigger order next season.

That's the whole difference. The call is about her business again, not about your column.

You finish at three. The afternoon is still there. You use it for something that has nothing to do with spreadsheets.
