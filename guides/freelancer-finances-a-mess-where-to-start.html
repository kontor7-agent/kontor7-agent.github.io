Freelance Finances a Mess? The Tyre Fitter's Waiting Area

2026-10-16

The plastic chair is bolted to the chair next to it. The coffee machine hums and gives out something brown. Through the glass, a man in blue overalls is lifting your car as if it weighs nothing.

The fitter said forty minutes. So you take out your phone and, for once, you decide to Deal With The Money.

Let's call you Mira. You're an illustrator, four years in. This is an illustration, not a real person's story. You might be a writer, a developer or a coach, and you'd still recognise the next ten minutes.

Where do you even begin?

You open your banking app. Then your email. Then a folder called "Invoices (new)", which sits next to "Invoices" and "Invoices OLD DONT DELETE".

You look for one simple thing: who still owes you money?

You think it's two clients. Maybe three. There was that logo job in spring. You're fairly sure it was paid, but you can't say which day, or whether it was the full amount. Somewhere there's a PDF you sent. Somewhere else there's a spreadsheet you started in January with real enthusiasm. The last row is from March.

The knot is not dramatic. It's low and constant, like a fridge you've stopped noticing until it goes quiet.

You close the folder. You open your notes app instead and type: start with the money. Then you look at it. Start what with the money? Invoices? Taxes? Prices? Your hours? Every thread leads to another, and the whole ball feels too tangled to pull on.

So you do what most people do. You scroll something else.

The quiet cost of "I'll sort it soon"

Nobody sees this part of freelancing from outside.

It isn't only the mess. It's what the mess does to the rest of your week.

You hesitate before replying to a client who's late with a payment, because you're not sure how late. You say yes to a new job quickly, partly because you can't tell whether next month is safe. You quote a price and feel a small flinch, because you have no idea what an hour of your work actually costs you.

And there's the smaller, sadder thing. When someone asks how business is going, you say "busy, good!" and feel slightly like a fraud. Because you are busy. You just couldn't prove it was going well if they asked for a number.

A real business, you think. Not someone winging it.

The tyre machine whines. Someone at the counter laughs about a puncture. You put the phone face-down on your knee.

The sentence that changes the size of the problem

A woman sits down two chairs along, with a laptop bag and a lanyard from some co-working space. She's on a call, quietly, and you hear half of it without meaning to.

"No, it's fine, I just look at it on Mondays," she says. "Ten minutes. Who owes me, what's coming, what I charged. That's it."

Then she laughs and says something about her accountant, and the call moves on.

You don't know her. You'll never see her again. But that sentence stays with you in the plastic chair.

Ten minutes. On Mondays.

You had been treating your finances as one enormous thing that needed a free weekend and a stronger personality. You had never thought of it as three small questions you could look at once a week.

Who owes me? What can I charge? Where does my time go?

That's not a mountain. That's a short list.

What Monday could feel like

Imagine it, just for the length of this coffee.

It's Monday, a quarter past nine. The kettle is on. You open one file, and it already knows the clients you've entered. You see two invoices still open and one that's a week late. You feel something small and almost unfamiliar: you're not surprised.

You write the reminder in a calm voice, because you can see exactly what you're asking for. It doesn't feel like begging. It feels like tidying up.

Then you glance at your rate and your hours for the week. You're not doing maths on a napkin. You can see which project is quietly eating your time and which one pays you well. When a new client asks for a quote on Wednesday, you don't flinch, because the number comes from somewhere.

By ten past nine you close the file. That's it. The rest of the day belongs to the actual work, the part you became self-employed to do.

At six, you shut the laptop. The evening stays yours.

Nothing about that Monday is glamorous. That's what makes it good. Calm money looks boring from outside and feels like exhaling from inside.

Starting small, still in the waiting room

"Mira?" The fitter holds up your key. "All done. Rear left was getting thin, so you're good for a while."

You pay. You get in the car. For a moment you sit there with the engine off and think: I didn't fix anything. I didn't open a single spreadsheet.

But something has shifted anyway. The problem has a different size now. You don't have to understand all of your finances today. You only have to give them a place to live, with a few simple questions that get answered the same way every week.

You open your notes app and change the line. It no longer says start with the money. It says: one place. Ten minutes. Monday.

You pull out of the garage into a grey afternoon. It's the lightest you've felt about the subject in months.
