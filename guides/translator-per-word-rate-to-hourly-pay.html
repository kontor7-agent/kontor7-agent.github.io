Translator Per-Word Rate to Hourly Pay? The Bike Shop

2026-10-17

It's Thursday, 10:35 am, and you're standing in a bike shop that smells of rubber and chain oil. The mechanic has your back wheel in the stand. He says it'll be twenty minutes.

Your phone buzzes. It's the project manager from the agency you've worked with for two years, and she's friendly, as always.

Hi! Small medical manual, about 6,000 words, same rate as usual. Could you deliver by tomorrow noon? Thank you so much!

You read it twice. Your thumb is already hovering over the reply field.

You know the answer you're about to type. It's "Sure, no problem." It's what you always type.

The number you only know in cents

You know your per-word rate. You could recite it in your sleep. It's the number you gave when you started, nudged up once, a little shyly, two years ago.

What you don't know is what it turns into. How many euros an hour is it, really?

The rate looks fine on paper. But you remember the last manual. The agency's CAT tool applied its discount for repetitions, so a chunk of the text you'd mentally counted as full price was suddenly paid at a fraction. Then there was the terminology list nobody had sent, which took your whole Tuesday morning. And there were the three queries to the client that sat unanswered for two days, while the deadline didn't move.

At the end you got a payment for a number of words. You never worked out how many hours it took.

You tell yourself it was probably fine. You tell yourself that a lot.

What "same rate as usual" is hiding

The mechanic taps the tyre and says something about a spoke. You nod without hearing him.

Because here is the thing you don't say out loud. For a while now, a small, quiet part of you has suspected that some jobs pay you well, some pay you badly, and you can't tell which is which. The rush ones feel exhausting but you never added a rush fee. You were just glad they trusted you. The long, dull, repetitive ones feel easy, but the discount takes half of what you thought you'd earn.

And then there's the other thing. The agency that pays at sixty days. Or was it ninety? Was that this one, or the other one with the nice project manager and the strange invoicing portal? You'd have to open three folders and a spreadsheet you stopped updating in spring to find out.

So you say yes, because saying no feels risky. Saying yes feels safe, even when it costs you a Friday morning and a tight chest.

The cost of not knowing is never one big thing. It's the yes you give by reflex. It's the weekend that slowly shrinks. It's the feeling, on a good month, that you can't explain why the account balance doesn't match how hard you worked.

The pause you didn't expect

The wheel goes back on. The mechanic spins it and it runs quiet and straight. It's strangely satisfying, watching something go from wobbling to just right.

You realise you'd like that for your work. Not a different job. Not a higher rate pulled out of thin air. Just to see the thing straight, once.

What would it be like to look at a job like this one and know, before replying, roughly what it would pay per hour? To know that repetitions would be discounted, that one-day turnaround deserves a different number, and that this particular agency has a habit of paying late?

Not a negotiation tactic. Not a speech. Just a quiet fact in front of you.

You look at the message again. You type: Thanks for thinking of me. Let me check my schedule and come back to you within the hour.

It's a small sentence, but it feels like stepping off a moving walkway.

A different Thursday

Imagine the version of this morning where it's already sorted.

Every job you've finished sits in one calm place: words, discounts, hours, the date you invoiced, the date they paid. You don't have to remember anything. You glance at it the way you'd glance at a weather app.

And so a message like this one lands differently. You see that the agency's last two jobs came out lower per hour than your direct clients'. You see that rush work, when you ask for the surcharge, makes the numbers look the way you'd always hoped. You see that the nice project manager's company pays late, again and again, and that it's a pattern, not bad luck.

None of it tells you what to do. You still decide. But you decide with your eyes open, and that changes the tone of your voice. You can say "I can do it by Monday" or "For a one-day turnaround my rate is different" without your stomach tightening, because you're not guessing.

You close the laptop at six. Not because you worked less, but because you stopped carrying the unanswered questions around with you.

Back at the counter

You pay the mechanic. He hands you the bike like it's no big deal, and it isn't, really. You wheel it out into the thin October light.

You don't have to rebuild your whole business before lunch. You can start small: one place where a job becomes a real hourly number, where discounts and rush fees have a column, where the late-paying agency can't hide behind a pleasant email signature.
