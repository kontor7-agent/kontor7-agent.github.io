Client Wants a Rush Job for Free? The Food Truck Friday

2026-10-18

Friday, 12:20. You are fourth in the queue at the food truck by the canal, and the smell of grilled halloumi is the best thing that has happened all week.

Your phone buzzes. You glance at it the way you glance at a pan that might boil over.

Hi! Small thing: could you turn the whole deck around by Monday 9? Our investor call moved up. It's mostly done anyway, so no need to make it a big thing 😊

Mostly done. You have seen this deck. It is not mostly done. It is a document with a lot of slides and a lot of opinions.

The person in front of you orders two wraps and a lemonade. You do not hear a word of it.

The sentence you start typing

Your thumbs move before your head does.

Sure, I'll see what I can do.

You delete it. That is a yes wearing a coat.

Unfortunately that's tricky because… Delete. That sounds like an excuse, and you'd have to justify it.

Happy to help! There might be a rush fee… Delete. Might. You hate might. It sounds like asking permission to have a price.

Let's call you Mira, a designer who has been on her own for four years. You are good at the work. It's the other part that makes your stomach tighten: the moment someone you like asks for something you can't afford to give, and says it smiling.

There's a quiet arithmetic running under all of it. Saturday gone. Sunday gone. The hiking plan with your sister, gone. And the client would say thanks, genuinely, and next month the "quick favour" would arrive again, because last time it worked.

Nobody tells you that bit. A free rush job isn't free once. It becomes the way things are done.

"Next, please"

The man in the truck window is looking at you. You order the halloumi wrap you were dreaming about, and then you stand at the side with your phone and the reply still unsent.

You are not angry at the client. That's what makes it hard. She's kind. She's under pressure. She isn't trying to take anything from you. She simply doesn't see what a weekend is made of on your side.

So you do what you always do. You tell yourself it's only one weekend. You tell yourself that being easy to work with is part of the brand. You tell yourself that real professionals just absorb this.

Then you remember a thing you noticed last spring: the clients who got the quickest yes were also the ones who asked for the most.

You put the phone in your pocket and eat the wrap. It's good. It's slightly wasted on you.

The pause that changes the shape of it

Walking back along the towpath, a small thought arrives, not dramatic, just clear.

I don't have to answer this standing in a queue.

The message came in at 12:20. Nothing in it needs a reply by 12:25. The urgency belongs to her calendar, not to your nervous system.

You also realise something that feels almost silly. You've never actually had a no that was yours. Every time, you wrote it from scratch, in the worst possible mood, with the client's face in your head. Of course it came out as an apology.

What if a no could be warm and plain at the same time? Something like: yes to the project, no to the free part, here is what a Monday delivery looks like, here is what a Wednesday delivery looks like. Both are real options. Neither is a punishment.

That is not rudeness. That is a menu.

What Monday could feel like

Picture the same Friday, only slightly different.

You read the message at your desk, with a coffee, after the halloumi. You take ten minutes. The reply you send is friendly and short. It names what she's asking for, says what's possible and at what price, and leaves the choice with her.

She might say yes. She might say Wednesday is fine. She might grumble for a day. All of those outcomes are survivable, and all of them leave your weekend intact.

On Saturday morning you are on a train, not hunched over slide 31. You're not rehearsing a speech. You are not quietly resenting someone you actually like.

And on the following Monday, when the next "quick favour" shows up, you don't feel the old drop in your stomach. You feel something closer to recognition. Ah, this one. I know what I say.

That is the real change. Not toughness. Familiarity. The words are already there, in your voice, so you aren't inventing courage on the spot.
