Freelancer Quarterly Taxes Panic? The Quiet Coach, 8:40

2026-10-31

The quiet coach has a rule about phones. Mara has broken it three times already.

It is 8:40 on a Tuesday. She is a freelance illustrator on her way to a client workshop. A calendar reminder has just slid down her screen: Tax payment due in 12 days.

She had forgotten that. Or rather, she had known it the way you know about a dentist appointment you've decided not to think about yet.

She opens her banking app. One balance, one number. It has to cover rent, the new tablet she financed in the spring, and something else she can't name. Somewhere in there is money that isn't hers. She just can't see which part.

The thought arrives, loud and simple: How much was I supposed to set aside?

The question nobody answered in your first year

Nobody gave her a talk about this. She went freelance, the first invoices were paid, and it felt like a miracle. Then the first tax letter came, and the miracle got a footnote.

Since then she has had a system, of a kind. In good months she moves "something" into the savings account. In bad months she moves nothing and tells herself next month will make up for it. She has never been sure whether "something" was enough.

Now she scrolls up through the months. Which invoices were actually paid? Which ones is she still waiting on? Was that big job in March counted already, or does it belong to the next period?

She doesn't know. She can't answer the simplest question because the answers live in six places: the bank app, her invoice folder, a note on her phone, and a few email threads.

The train slows for a station. A man across the aisle unwraps a sandwich. Mara's chest feels tight and a little ridiculous. Nothing has happened yet. It is only a date and a number she can't see.

What the panic is actually made of

It isn't really about the money. It's the not-knowing.

Some of the dread is arithmetic you haven't done. But most of it is the feeling that a real business would have this under control, and you are winging it with a nice portfolio and a good attitude.

That feeling costs more than the tax does. It costs the Sunday afternoon you spend half-relaxing. It costs the sleep where your mind drafts imaginary letters to an authority that has been perfectly polite so far.

And it costs you quietly with clients. When you're anxious about cash, it's harder to say your price out loud. You hear yourself offering a discount nobody asked for.

Mara puts the phone face-down on her knee. She is not going to solve this at 8:40 in a train carriage. Still, she makes one small decision: this week she will find out what she is dealing with, not just fear it.

The ordinary version of the fix

That evening, in her kitchen, she does something unglamorous. She opens a blank sheet and sits with the shoebox of receipts and the folder of invoices. For the first time she writes the dates down in one place. The deadline. The invoices paid since the last one. The expenses she can prove and the ones she can only remember.

It takes less time than she expected. It's not exciting, but the knot loosens as the page fills.

She still doesn't know exactly what she owes. That is a question for her tax professional, and she makes the appointment instead of avoiding it. The difference is that she will walk in with a tidy page instead of a carrier bag. The conversation can be about her actual situation, not about finding things.

And she has learned something. The panic never came from the tax. It came from having no picture of her year.

A different Tuesday

Picture the same carriage three months later. The same man might even be there with the same sandwich.

The reminder slides down the screen. Tax payment due in 12 days. Mara reads it, thinks "yes, I know," and goes back to her coffee. She knows where her receipts are. She knows which invoices are paid. She knows roughly what to bring to her next appointment, and the date is already on her list.

Nothing about her income has changed. What has changed is that the reminder is no longer a surprise. She walks into the workshop with her shoulders down and talks about the project, not about her bank balance.

That is all calm is, most days. It's not wealth or a perfect system. It's a year you can see.
