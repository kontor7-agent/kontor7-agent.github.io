Cancelled Lessons, Lost Make-Ups? The Lightbulb Aisle

2026-11-01

It is 11:40 on a Wednesday, and you are standing in the lightbulb aisle of a hardware shop, holding two boxes that look identical.

Your phone buzzes in your coat pocket. It is a message from a parent: "Hi! Just checking, are we still on for Saturday for Lena's make-up?"

You read it twice. Make-up for what?

You put both boxes back. The aisle goes a bit quiet around you.

The week that fell apart

Let's call you Mara. You teach maths and some English to about fourteen students. Some are school kids and some are adults retaking an exam. You work from your kitchen table and from a few living rooms across town.

Last week you lost your voice. Not a little. You sounded like a creaking gate, and by Tuesday you could barely whisper. You cancelled everything, five days of lessons, in a flurry of short messages sent from under a blanket.

So sorry, I'll make it up. Let's find a slot.

You meant every word. You still do. But "I'll make it up" is a promise, and promises need somewhere to live. Yours are scattered. Some are in a thread with a dad called Tobias. A couple are in a notebook by the kettle. One is half-written in your calendar as "Sam?? move". Another was a voice message you answered while brushing your teeth.

Now a parent is asking about Saturday, and you honestly do not know if Saturday is a make-up, a regular lesson, or something you invented in a fever.

The small, quiet cost

Here is the part nobody says out loud. It is not really about the lessons. It is about what the not-knowing does to you.

You type "Of course! Let me just double-check my calendar" and then stand there, because "double-check" means ten minutes of scrolling through threads while someone waits for a bulb in the next aisle.

You worry you owe three lessons to a student who is only owed one. Or one to a student who is owed three, and who will be too polite to mention it. That second worry is the sharper one. A student who is quietly owed something, and a tutor who forgets, is how a good relationship slowly goes sour.

There is a money side too, and you feel it more than you admit. Some of those cancelled lessons were yours to cancel. Some were cancelled by students last-minute, the week before, and you never wrote down whether those were free, charged, or "just this once". You are fairly sure you let at least two slide. You are not sure how many, or what they added up to.

So you do what many people do. You say yes to everything and promise yourself you'll sort it out properly on Sunday.

The turning point (it happens next to the extension leads)

You reply to the parent with something vague and kind. Then you stop in front of a wall of extension leads and ask yourself a different question.

Not "How do I remember better?" You have tried that, and your memory has a long record of letting you down in exactly this spot.

The question is: "Why is this living in my head at all?"

A lesson taught is a fact. A lesson cancelled is a fact. A lesson owed, a lesson paid, a lesson that was a free cancellation: all of them are facts. They are small and boring and they happen on a date. They do not need to be remembered. They need to be written once, in one place, in a way that quietly adds itself up.

You buy the bulb. You are almost smiling in the car park, which is odd.

A Monday that feels different

Picture a Monday a few weeks from now.

It is 8:15 and you are at the kitchen table with your tea. Not hot-hot, just the right temperature. You open one file. Fourteen names, each with a quiet line beside it.

Lena: one make-up owed, Saturday booked. Tobias's son: all paid, nothing owed. The adult learner who tends to pay late: two lessons behind, and you can see it before the lesson, not three weeks after.

You don't have to hunt or guess. You glance, and you know.

When a parent asks about Saturday, you answer in one line, with a date, and you sound like exactly what you are: a professional with a calm, orderly practice. When someone cancels late, you note it in ten seconds, and at the end of the month you can see what those gaps actually cost you. Not as a guilty feeling, but as a number you can look at calmly. Perhaps one day that number helps you decide it is time to raise your rate. Perhaps not. The decision is yours either way.

The evening that used to go on "sorting things out" is just an evening. You close the laptop. The kettle goes on again, and this time it is for you.
