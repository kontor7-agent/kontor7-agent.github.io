Never Tested Your Backup Restore? The Spare Tyre

2026-10-28

The tyre goes at 2:15 on a Thursday afternoon, on a country road with no phone signal worth mentioning.

You ease onto the verge. The hazard lights tick. You're twenty minutes from a client meeting, a laptop bag on the passenger seat, and a feeling you know too well: this is going to cost me more than the tyre.

The boot you haven't opened in two years

You lift the boot floor. There it is, the spare, exactly where it's supposed to be. A small relief.

Then you press your thumb into the rubber. It gives. It's soft, and probably has been for a long time. The jack is there too, but the wheel brace is not. You stand on the verge looking at an object that was there the whole time and did nothing for you.

The recovery truck takes fifty minutes. The driver is friendly and unhurried. While he swaps the wheel, he says what he must say twenty times a week: "Everyone has a spare. Hardly anyone has checked it."

You laugh politely. Then you stop laughing, because a thought has just walked into your head and sat down.

The backup.

The other thing that's "there"

You have one. You're fairly sure you have one. There's a drive, or a folder, or a cloud thing that was set up on a calm afternoon, back when you told yourself you were becoming a proper business.

You can picture the little green tick. You can't remember when you last saw it.

And you have never, not once, restored anything from it.

You've never pulled a file back to see if it opens. You've never imagined the laptop gone and asked what the first hour would look like. You've just trusted it, the way you trusted the spare. It was in the boot, so it counted.

The worry isn't loud. That's why it lasts. It sits under the invoices and the proposals and the client emails: if the laptop died tonight, would it all come back? Or would I find out it was soft all along?

You don't talk about it. A freelancer who runs everything alone isn't supposed to admit that the safety net is a guess.

What the guess quietly costs

It costs small things. A flicker of dread when the laptop fan screams. A held breath when the update bar sits at 94%. A slightly too-careful way of closing the lid.

It costs the reasonable question you avoid asking yourself, because the honest answer might ruin your afternoon. Better not to know. Better to keep the tick and skip the test.

And underneath, a quieter cost: the feeling that you're running a real business on hope. Clients hand you their files, their drafts, their deadlines. You say "of course, it's safe with me," and you'd like that sentence to be true because you checked, not because nothing has gone wrong yet.

You make the meeting with ten minutes to spare. You're perfectly professional. Nobody can tell your mind is half in the boot of your car.

The turning point is a little boring

That evening, you don't panic and you don't buy anything. You do something much smaller. You sit down and ask the plain question: what do I actually have, and when did it last run?

You look. It turns out the answer is partly fine and partly a shrug. Something ran, probably. Nothing tells you what. There's no record you can read in thirty seconds, just a tick of unknown age.

That's the real discovery. The problem isn't that backups are hard. It's that you can't see yours. A spare you can't see is a spare you can't trust.

So the idea changes shape. You don't need something clever. You need something dull and visible: a backup that keeps a few earlier versions, tidies up after itself, leaves a note every time it runs, and lets you rehearse a run without touching anything. Then a restore stops being a scary event and becomes an ordinary little check, like pressing a thumb into a tyre.

A normal Monday, afterwards

Picture it a few weeks on.

It's Monday, 8:40. Coffee. The inbox is waking up. Before you open it, you glance at a short log: last night's run, finished at 02:10, nothing odd. Ten seconds. You pull one random file back from a snapshot, the way you'd check a tyre at a petrol station, and it opens. You close the folder.

Then you get on with your day.

The laptop fan screams at 11:00 and you simply notice it. The update bar sits at 94% and you go and refill your water glass. When a client says, "you do have backups, right?", you say yes, and for the first time you mean it with your whole voice.

Nothing dramatic has happened. That's the point. The knot you didn't know you were carrying has loosened by one notch, and it stays loosened.
