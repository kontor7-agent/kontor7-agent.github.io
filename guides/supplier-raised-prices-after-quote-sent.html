Supplier Raised Prices After Your Quote? The 3:10 Queue

2026-11-09

It is 3:10 on a Tuesday. Marta is in the school pickup queue, engine off, window cracked, one eye on the gate.

Her phone buzzes on the dashboard. It's the email from her pipe and fittings supplier. The subject line is polite: Updated price list, effective immediately.

She doesn't open it. She doesn't have to. She knows what it means, because on Monday morning she sent a bathroom quote to a couple in a terraced house two streets from here. A nice couple. They replied with a thumbs-up and Great, can you start in two weeks?

She wrote that quote from memory, in the van, outside the house. Copper, fittings, the shower valve, the tray. She had the old prices in her head, the way you know the way home.

The sum you do with your stomach

The queue creeps forward. Marta opens the email after all.

The valve is up. The pipe is up. The fittings she buys by the dozen are up. None of it is dramatic on its own. But she starts adding it in her head, and the number she gets feels like a small stone dropping into water.

Then the second thought arrives, the one nobody says out loud: Did I even leave enough room in that quote for this?

She honestly can't tell. The quote is a PDF. The materials are in a notebook in the glovebox, some of them on receipts, some of them on the back of receipts. The labour was a feeling. She remembers thinking about three days, and then rounding to something that sounded fair.

A child taps on the window. Her nephew. He climbs in with a drawing and a lunchbox that smells of banana. She says hello, love, how was your day? and means it. But half of her is still standing in that bathroom, doing arithmetic.

What it quietly costs

By Wednesday she has three options, and she dislikes all of them.

She could swallow it. Do the job at the quoted price and call it "keeping the customer happy". That works until it doesn't, usually around the third job in a row.

She could write to the couple and ask for more. That email has been open in her drafts for an hour. Every version sounds like an apology or an accusation.

Or she could say nothing, work a little faster, and hope the next job makes up for it.

None of these is really about copper. Underneath, it's the old feeling of not being a proper business, just someone doing sums in a van and hoping. A real company would have a line that says materials, a line that says margin, and a calm way of knowing what changed.

She has the receipts. She has the notebook. What she doesn't have is the picture.

The turn

That evening a friend who fits kitchens drops round to borrow a ladder. Marta tells him the story over the fence, half joking, the way you do when it isn't a joke.

He doesn't offer advice. He just says, I don't hold prices in my head anymore. I used to. It's why I dreaded every supplier email.

Then he says something she keeps turning over: a price list changing isn't the problem. Not knowing what a job is built from is the problem. If you can see what's in it, a change is just a change. You look, you decide, you say one calm sentence to the customer, and you carry on.

She doesn't ask him how. It's the end of the day and the ladder is heavy. But the idea stays with her: the worry wasn't the supplier. It was the fog around the quote.

A different Tuesday

Picture the same queue, a few weeks on.

The phone buzzes. Updated price list. Marta glances at it and sets it face down on the dashboard.

She isn't ignoring it. She knows that tonight, or tomorrow with her coffee, she'll update a few lines and see exactly what it does to the jobs already out there. Which quotes still hold. Which one needs a short, friendly note. Which one she can simply honour, on purpose, because she chose to and can see what it costs.

The gate opens. Children pour out. Her nephew waves a painting that looks suspiciously like a boiler.

She is just a person in a car, waiting for a child, with nothing in her stomach but lunch.

That is the whole difference. The prices still move. But she is no longer the last to find out what they mean for her.
