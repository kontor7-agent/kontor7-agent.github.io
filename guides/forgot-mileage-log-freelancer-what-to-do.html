Forgot to Keep a Mileage Log? The Odometer in the Lay-by

2026-11-02

Ines is parked in a lay-by on the way back from a client's garden, a flask of tea between her knees. It is 13:20 on a grey Wednesday in early November. She is only looking at the dashboard because the fuel light blinked.

The odometer says 61,204.

And then the thought arrives, quiet and cold: I never wrote any of it down.

Not one trip. Not the site visits in March, not the nursery runs in May, not the long drive to the client who wanted a courtyard "with a bit of Italy in it". She meant to. In January she even bought a small notebook with a green cover. It is somewhere in the glovebox, under the parking permits and a map nobody has opened since 2019.

The notebook with three entries

She opens the glovebox. The notebook is there. Three entries, all from the first week of January, in her tidy, hopeful handwriting. After that, blank pages.

It is a very particular kind of embarrassment. Nobody is shouting. Nobody even knows. But she sits in a lay-by feeling like someone who is not quite running a real business, someone who started the year with good intentions and let them go by February.

She thinks about the number of kilometres she must have driven. She has no idea. A lot. Probably a lot more than she'd guess. And the part that stings is not only the paperwork. It is the quiet suspicion that she has been doing a good chunk of her work for free, because the driving was never counted anywhere.

The tea goes cold. A lorry thunders past and shakes the car.

What the dread is really about

It is rarely about the mileage itself. It is about what the gap stands for.

If the log is missing, what else is? The receipt for the stone supplier. The invoice she thinks she sent in August. The thing the accountant will ask about in January, in that polite voice that still somehow sounds like a question about your character.

So the mind does what minds do. It takes one blank notebook and builds a whole story around it: you are behind, you are disorganised, you will never catch up.

That story is loud in a lay-by. It is a lot quieter once you look at what is actually in front of you.

The small, ordinary turn

What Ines does next is not clever. She puts the notebook on the passenger seat and opens her calendar on her phone.

The year is all there. Client names. Site visits. A "nursery, 10:00" in May. She scrolls, slowly, and each entry brings back a day: the rain at the Hartmann job, the wrong gate, the client's dog who adored her boots.

It is not a perfect record, and she knows it. It is a starting point. A rough, honest outline of a year she can see again. Whether a rebuilt picture like that is good enough for her situation is a question for her tax professional, and she makes a note to ask. She doesn't need to decide anything today.

But the knot in her stomach loosens a little, just from the looking. Because the year is not gone. It is just unsorted.

What a calm November looks like

Imagine the same Wednesday a different way.

It is still November. Still grey. But the paperwork has a home. The receipts from the year sit in one place instead of three carrier bags. There is a short list of what needs doing before the year closes, and most of it already has a tick next to it. The deadlines that matter are written down once, not carried around in your head like loose change.

You are in the same lay-by, and you glance at the odometer, and nothing happens in your chest at all. You just think: lunch.

That is the whole difference. Not a perfect year. Not a flawless log from January. Just a place where the year-end things live, so they stop living in your stomach.

And on Monday morning, instead of a vague dread that follows you from the kettle to the desk, there is a calm little page that says what's done and what's next. You open it, you do one small thing, you close it. By ten you are designing a garden instead of apologising to yourself.

Starting from where you are

If you recognise Ines, here is the gentle truth: it is not too late to begin sorting the rest of the year. November is still early enough to be kind to yourself. You can't go back and fill in what you didn't write down, but you can stop the pile from growing and give everything else a proper place.

That starts with the stuff you do have: receipts, dates, the things your accountant will ask for. Put it somewhere you can see it. Give the year an ending that feels deliberate.
