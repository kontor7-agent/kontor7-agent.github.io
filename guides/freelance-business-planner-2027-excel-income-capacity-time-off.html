Plan Your 2027 Freelance Income and Time Off: The Question at Lunch

2026-10-13

It's a Thursday in October, a little after one. You're sitting outside a small bakery with an old friend, two coffees going cold between you.

"So, next year," she says. "We should finally do that week in the mountains. When can you take time off?"

You smile. You open your mouth. And nothing useful comes out.

"Let me check," you say, and reach for your phone. But you already know there is nothing to check. There is no place where next year lives. There are a few client emails, a vague feeling about March, and a folder called Ideas.

The answer you didn't have

You are a freelance illustrator, or a developer, or a copywriter. It doesn't matter much. What matters is that you've been running your own business for a few years now, and you are good at the work in front of you.

But the year ahead? That's a fog.

You know roughly what you'd like to earn. You've said the number to yourself in the shower. You have never written it down next to the weeks you can realistically work, the projects that might land, and the days you'd like to be somewhere else.

So when your friend asks about a week off, a small knot tightens. Because the honest answer is: I don't know if I can afford to stop.

And underneath it, a quieter one: I don't know if I'm running a business or just reacting to whoever writes next.

What the fog quietly costs

Nobody sees this part.

It's the holiday you book and then half-spend checking your inbox, because you never planned the income around it. It's the month you say yes to a project you don't want, because the calendar looks empty and you can't tell whether that's a problem or just a Tuesday.

It's the December evening when you wonder whether the year was good. Not felt good. Was good. And you can't say, because the targets only ever lived in your head, and your head moves the goalposts.

It's the slow suspicion that you work more than you earn, and rest less than you should.

None of that is a failure of discipline. You've simply been steering without a map.

The walk home

After lunch your friend hugs you goodbye. "Think about it," she says, kindly. "No pressure."

You walk home the long way, past the school, past the tram stop, and you notice something. You're not anxious about the mountains. You're a bit embarrassed that you couldn't answer a simple question about your own working life.

Then another thought arrives, quieter and more useful: What if next year didn't have to be a fog?

Not a rigid plan. Not a corporate strategy deck. Just one calm place where you can see the year. The income you're aiming for. The weeks you'll realistically be working. The time you've decided to take off, written down as if it were a real appointment. Because it is.

A place where you check in every quarter, look at what actually happened, and adjust without drama.

A different Monday in 2027

Picture a Monday in late February.

You make coffee. You open one file. Not six, not a pile of notes. One.

You see the first two months of the year laid out. Where you are against the target you set. Which projects are firm and which are still maybes. A gentle picture of how full your weeks really are.

There is a blocked stretch in June. Five days, already marked. Nobody has to ask whether you can afford it, because you planned for it back in autumn, when the year was still blank and you had the time to be sensible.

When a new enquiry arrives, you don't panic-reply. You glance at the plan and know whether you have room. Sometimes the answer is "yes, happily." Sometimes it is "not before April," and you can say that in a steady voice.

At the end of March, you sit down for a short quarterly look back. Ten honest minutes. It tells you what worked and what didn't. No guilt, no verdict. Just information you can use.

And when your friend texts in spring, asking about the mountains, you write back in under a minute: "June, the second week. Booking it."

Why now, in October

There is something kind about planning before the year starts. You aren't behind on anything yet. There are no overdue invoices clouding your judgement, no January receipts panic.

Just a clear stretch of calendar and a chance to decide what you want it to hold.

You don't need a consultant, or a new app to learn, or a weekend of building a spreadsheet from scratch. You need a simple structure that's already thought through: targets next to actuals, a view of your pipeline, a place for time off, a dashboard that shows how the year is going.

Something you fill in over a quiet evening and then actually use.
