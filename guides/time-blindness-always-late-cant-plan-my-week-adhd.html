Time Blindness and Always Late? Can't Plan Your ADHD Week

2026-10-31

14:11, and the Call Has Already Started

The meeting link is open in one tab. In the other tab, your own calendar says the call is at 15:00.

It is 14:11. The client's message from this morning, still unread under three others, says: "See you at 14:00!"

You have one sock on. Your laptop is on the stairs, because that is where you were when you last looked at the time and thought, plenty of room. Then the room was gone.

So meet Ines. She is an illustrative character, a freelance illustrator, and she is not a real person. If you are reading this, you may know exactly how her stomach feels right now.

She clicks Join. Her camera comes on a second too early, showing the stairs and the sock. Three faces wait on the screen, politely.

"Sorry, sorry, so sorry," she says. "I had the wrong time."

She didn't have the wrong time. She had no time at all, just a soft fog where the afternoon should be.

It Was Never About Caring

The client is kind about it. That almost makes it worse.

After the call Ines sits on the stairs with the laptop closed. She does what she always does and builds the case against herself. Other people manage a week. Other people look at a clock and the clock tells them something. For her, ten minutes and two hours feel like the same size until one of them has already gone.

Nobody sees the cost, because it never arrives as one big bill. It shows up in small pieces:

  • the apology she has a standard opening line for
  • the project that was "almost done" for nine days
  • the quiet dread when a new request lands, because she can't picture where it would fit
  • the sense that she is the only one who has to be this vigilant just to look normal

She has tried planners. There is a drawer of them, hopeful and mostly empty after the second week. They had a box for every hour, every habit and every colour of ink. When she missed one day, the whole page felt like evidence.

Time blindness doesn't mean you don't care. It means the hours don't announce themselves. The week stays a blur, so every day starts from nothing.

The Question That Was Too Big

That evening, Ines isn't looking for a system. She is trying not to open the folder of unfinished work.

Her sister is visiting, and she watches Ines stare at the wall. "What's actually on tomorrow?" she asks.

It is a simple question, and Ines can't answer it. The calendar has appointments, but not the real shape of the day: the drawing that has to ship, the invoice that has to go, the call to prepare for. It's all in her head, and her head is full.

Her sister takes a sheet of paper from the printer tray, draws a line down the middle, and says, "Just give me three things. Not ten. Three."

It takes Ines almost a minute. Then she says them out loud: finish the cover art, send the invoice, reply to Daniel. They look small on paper. They look possible.

She looks at them for a long moment. For the first time that week, tomorrow has edges.

What Changed Wasn't Willpower

Nothing dramatic happens. Ines doesn't become a different person. Her sense of time doesn't suddenly start working like a clock.

The change is smaller. The day is no longer a fog she has to push through. It is three lines on a page she can see from across the room.

On Sunday evening she spends a few unhurried minutes looking at the week as one thing, not as seven separate surprises. She notices that Thursday is already crowded, and moves something before it becomes a crisis. She doesn't have to remember all of it, because it lives on the page.

And when a day goes sideways, as days do, the page doesn't punish her. There is no row of empty boxes. There are three things, and tomorrow there will be three new ones.

A Tuesday, Later

It's 13:40 on another Tuesday. Ines is making tea.

The call is at 14:00, and she knows, because it's the first thing she saw that morning, written in her own handwriting at the top of the day. She has twenty minutes. She uses five of them to open the file she wants to show, and a few more to sit with the tea before the screen lights up.

When the others join, she is already there. Camera on, sock on, no apology needed.

It isn't a miracle. It is just what an ordinary Tuesday feels like when you are not running behind it. Her shoulders sit lower. She hears what the client says instead of half-listening while rehearsing an excuse.

Afterwards she closes the laptop, and it's still afternoon. That used to feel impossible.
