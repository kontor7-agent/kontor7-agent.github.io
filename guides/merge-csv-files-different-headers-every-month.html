Merging Dozens of CSV Files Every Month? The Dentist Queue

2026-10-20

It is 10:20 on a Tuesday, and you are sitting in the dentist's waiting room with your laptop on your knees.

You are not here for a check-up. You are here because your daughter has an appointment, and the report is due Friday. So you opened the laptop to "just start the merge."

The folder has forty-three files. One from each supplier, each branch, each tool that somebody once set up and nobody remembers why.

You open the first one. The column says Customer. The second says customer_name. The third says Kunde, and the fourth has a blank column in front of everything, as if it were shy.

The nurse calls a name. It isn't yours. You sigh and scroll.

The monthly ritual nobody sees

Nobody asked you to be good at this. It simply landed on you, the way these things do.

Once a month the files arrive. Once a month you copy, paste, rename a header, delete a row that says "Total" in the middle of the data, and wonder why the dates look like numbers like 45231.

You tell yourself it takes an hour. It never takes an hour.

Some months it is a whole afternoon. Some months it is the afternoon, plus the next morning, because you found a file saved with a strange encoding and every ü turned into a little box with a question mark inside.

And there is the worry that sits underneath all of it, the one you don't say out loud. What if I missed one? What if file thirty-one never made it into the total? What if a name appears twice and you counted that person twice?

Nobody would notice. That is exactly what makes it uncomfortable. You are the only safety net, and you are tired.

What it quietly costs

The hours are the obvious part. The other costs are quieter.

You start dreading the first week of the month the way some people dread the dentist. (You see the irony. You are sitting in the waiting room.)

You put off the real work, the work you are actually good at, because the merge has to come first. Then the merge takes longer, so the real work gets squeezed, so you take it home.

And when someone asks, "Are these numbers solid?", you hear yourself say "Yes, I think so," a little too fast. You don't like that think.

It makes you feel like someone winging it. A professional, you feel, would have a system. A professional would not be hunting for a stray comma in row 8,412 on a Tuesday morning between the posters about flossing.

The sentence that changed the Tuesday

In this story, the person next to you is a woman in a green coat, waiting for her husband. She glances at your screen, the forty-three tabs, the half-colored cells.

"That looks like my brother's job," she says. "He used to do something like that every month. Now he just drops the files in a folder and goes for a walk."

You laugh politely. Then you stop laughing, because you realise you have never asked the question she just answered. You never asked whether the merge had to be done by you, by hand, every single time.

You assumed the mess was part of the job. Different headers, different formats, different habits in every file. You assumed that was simply what data looks like.

But the mess is predictable. It is the same mess, every month. The same renamed columns, the same odd dates, the same duplicates. Something that repeats can be taught once.

That is the turning point. It's not dramatic. Your name gets called, and you close the laptop with a feeling that is almost embarrassing in its smallness: relief.

The same month, a different Monday

Now imagine the first Monday of next month.

The files arrive, the way they always do. You make coffee first. That alone is new.

You drop the folder into place and run the cleanup. Before anything is changed, you get to look at what would change. A short report: these headers were matched, these dates were fixed, these rows were duplicates, and this one file needs a human look.

One file. Not forty-three.

You check it, it takes two minutes, and you move on. The merged sheet is in front of you, the columns in a sensible order, the ü where it belongs.

When someone asks, "Are these numbers solid?", you say, "Yes," and you mean it, because you can see what was done and what was left alone.

By ten you are working on the thing you were actually hired for. The afternoon is yours. Nobody will ever know how much of your Friday you got back, and that's fine. You know.

A small thing, then a bigger one

It is a modest change, honestly. A messy folder becomes a clean table. A day becomes a coffee.

But the bigger shift is the one you feel in your shoulders. The first week of the month stops being something you brace for. You stop checking whether you missed a file at midnight. The work starts to feel like a real, calm process rather than a pile of favours you do for your future self.
