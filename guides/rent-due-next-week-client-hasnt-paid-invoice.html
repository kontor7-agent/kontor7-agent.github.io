Rent Due Next Week, Client Unpaid? The Standing Order

2026-11-04

Monday, 7:50 in the morning. Ines stands at the bus stop with her phone in one hand and a paper cup in the other. The bus is two minutes away, and she is not looking at the timetable.

She is looking at her banking app. The balance is one number. The rent, due on the first, is another. She does the subtraction the way you press on a bruise, to see if it still hurts.

It does.

The invoice she sent to the agency, for a set of twelve illustrations, is now 23 days past its due date. She knows the number because she checked it on Friday. And again on Saturday. And once more, quickly, in the bathroom at her sister's birthday lunch.

The email that stays in drafts

The bus arrives. She sits by the window and opens her mail, but not to work. She opens the draft folder.

There is a message there from last Wednesday. Hi Tom, just a quick reminder about invoice 0412... It has sat there for five days, because every time she reads it, it sounds like one of two people. Either a pushover who is sorry to exist, or someone about to start a fight.

She doesn't want to be either. She wants to be a person who sends an email, gets an answer, and goes on with her day.

But that isn't how it feels. It feels like standing outside a friend's door with your hand raised, knowing you're owed something and wondering if knocking is rude.

Nobody warned her about this part of working for yourself. The drawing is fine. The clients are mostly kind. It's the asking that wears her down, the small arithmetic of how many days is too soon, how many is too late, what if they take it badly and don't book me again.

What it quietly costs

The unpaid invoice isn't only money. It leaks into everything.

It's the supermarket on Saturday, when she put the good olive oil back and told herself it was because she didn't need it. It's the small flinch when her landlord's name appears on her phone, even though he has only ever been polite. It's the way she's been saying yes to a new, badly paid job just to have something coming in.

And it's the sleep. Not dramatic insomnia, just waking at 5:40 with the number already in her head, before she has even found her slippers.

She tells herself it's a good thing she's careful. She wonders, in the quieter moments, whether other freelancers have this same knot and just don't mention it.

Wednesday, on a different footing

Here's the part that surprises her when it finally happens: nothing dramatic changes. There's no windfall and no lawyer. Just a small shift in how she looks at the problem.

A colleague, a woman who builds websites and shares a studio day with her on Thursdays, hears her sigh at the kettle and asks what's wrong. Ines almost says nothing. Then she says it all, quickly, in the voice of someone confessing.

The colleague doesn't gasp. She doesn't tell her to be tougher. She just says, "Oh, that. Yes. I used to rewrite that email for days. Now I don't write it at all. I just send the next one."

"The next what?"

"The next one in the order. It's already written. Friendly first, then clear, then firm. I only change the name."

It sounds almost too simple. Ines thinks about all the hours she has spent trying to find the perfect sentence, when the sentence was never really the problem. The problem was that every reminder felt like a brand-new decision, made alone, with her heart in her throat.

What if it weren't a decision at all? What if it were just the next step on a list that someone calm had already thought through?

An ordinary Monday, afterwards

Imagine the same bus stop, a few weeks on.

The cup is the same. The cold is the same. But when Ines opens the app, she isn't pressing on a bruise. She already knows which invoices are open and how many days each one is late. She knows which one is due a friendly nudge today, and which can wait until Thursday. She knows what she would say, because it's already written, in words that sound like her on a good day.

She sends the one email before the bus pulls in. It takes two minutes. She doesn't feel brave and she doesn't feel small. She feels like someone running a business, which, it turns out, she is.

Maybe the client pays that week. Maybe it takes another reminder. Either way, the knot has loosened, because the waiting is no longer silent and shapeless. It has a next step, and a date, and a calm voice.

The rent still comes out on the first. But now she sees it coming without dread. And the evening after, the one she used to spend rereading drafts, she spends drawing something just because she wants to.
