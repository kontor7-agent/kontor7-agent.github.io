Quoted a Job, Profit Gone After Materials? Patio Gate

2026-10-20

Marta stands at the garden gate with a mug of tea she didn't ask for. It's Friday, a little after four. Behind her, the new patio is finished: straight lines, tight joints, a faint smell of wet stone.

The client is thrilled. She says so three times. She is already talking about the borders next spring.

Marta smiles and says the right things. Meanwhile a small, cold thought is running underneath the conversation.

Did I actually make anything on this?

The number you said on the doorstep

Six weeks ago she stood in that same garden with a tape measure and a notebook. The client asked, "Roughly what would it come to?" and Marta did what most of us do. She looked at the space, thought about the work, and said a number.

It felt fair. It felt like a number a real professional would say. It was also built mostly from memory and a good feeling.

The quote went out that evening, and the client said yes within the hour. You know that small flush of relief when someone says yes quickly. It sometimes hides a second feeling: maybe I was too cheap.

What the glove box knew

After the handshake, Marta walks to her van. The passenger seat is a landscape of paper: a receipt from the stone yard, one from the builders' merchant, a fuel slip, a crumpled note for the extra bags of sand.

The second delivery wasn't in the quote. Neither was the skip hire, or the day the old base turned out deeper than expected. Nor was the afternoon she drove across town for the one missing edging stone.

None of it felt like a disaster while it was happening. Each thing was small and reasonable, and each got paid for on the spot with the card from the business account. Together they leaned on the job like a quiet weight.

She doesn't add it up in the van. She doesn't want to know yet. She drives home, makes pasta, and lets Friday be Friday.

The supermarket queue, Saturday

It happens on Saturday morning, in the queue at the supermarket, with a basket of ordinary things. Her phone is in her hand, and she opens the banking app just to check something else.

There, in a tidy list, are the weeks of outgoing payments. Stone yard. Merchant. Merchant again. Skip hire. Fuel.

She scrolls and does the sum in her head. Then she does it again, because the first answer can't be right. She thinks about the days on site, the evenings she spent cutting and carrying, and the Saturday she gave up. She thinks about the hourly figure that's left when it all comes out, and it is not one she'd ever say out loud to a client.

The cashier says, "Anything else?" and Marta says, "No, thanks," in a voice that sounds almost normal.

The worst part isn't the money. It's the feeling underneath it: I'm good at the work. Why am I so bad at this part?

It isn't a talent problem

Here is what she realises, slowly, while she packs the bags.

She isn't bad at business. She's been doing the one thing no one can do reliably from memory: guessing the whole job in her head, then discovering the real cost only after the money has already left.

The quote lived on a doorstep and in a notebook. The materials lived in the glove box. The hours lived nowhere at all. Nothing ever sat in the same place at the same time, so there was never a moment when she could see the job.

She thinks, what if the quote and the receipts were looking at each other?

She doesn't need a grand system. She needs one calm place where the price she promised, the materials she bought and what's left over all show up together, ideally before the patio is poured and not after.

A different Monday

Imagine the same job, six weeks later.

Monday morning. Marta is in a client's side passage, tape measure out, looking at a muddy slope that would make a nice terrace. The client asks the familiar question: "Roughly what would it come to?"

But this time she says, "Give me until this afternoon, and I'll send you something solid."

That one sentence is a small change, and it matters. She isn't nervous, because she isn't guessing. She has the measurements, the materials with their real prices, her own time, and the knowledge of what a job like this really takes. She can see what the work needs to earn her before she names a figure.

When the extra sand shows up halfway through, she doesn't flinch. She notes it, sees what it does to the margin, and knows whether it needs a conversation with the client. The receipt goes into the same place as everything else, and the glove box stays a glove box.

On Friday she shakes the client's hand at the gate. The thought underneath is quieter this time: this one was worth doing.

Then she goes home, and her weekend is actually hers.
