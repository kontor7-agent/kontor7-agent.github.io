Tax Deadline Near, Receipts Everywhere? The Tote Bag Call

2026-10-24

Wednesday, 10:40. Ines is on a video call with a client about a podcast soundtrack. Her camera shows a tidy bookshelf and a good lamp.

The camera does not show the desk below the frame.

On it sits a canvas tote bag from a conference, still half full of paper. Next to it is a phone with 214 unsorted photos, and a laptop with a tab called "receipts??" that she opened on Monday and has not touched since.

The client is saying something about the second mix. Ines nods. Her mind is somewhere else entirely.

The deadline is next week. Next week. I don't even know where to start.

The tote bag

She has been self-employed for four years. She is good at her work. People send her thank-you notes for it.

And yet every year there is this one week. The week when she turns into someone who loses things.

The headphones she bought in spring: was there a receipt? The software subscription that renews quietly in the background: which card, which month? The train ticket to the studio in another city. She remembers the train. She remembers the studio. She cannot picture the ticket.

She doesn't talk about this with other people. Everyone else seems to have a system. A friend of hers has colour-coded folders. Another mentions her "monthly admin hour" as if it were a yoga class.

Ines has a tote bag.

What it quietly costs

The call ends. She says thanks, smiles, clicks "Leave", and her shoulders drop an inch.

Then she tips the tote bag out onto the desk.

It isn't a disaster, and that is almost the worst part. It's just a slow, grey pile of small decisions. A faded thermal receipt with no shop name. A card statement she'd printed "just in case". A coffee from a day she can't place.

An hour passes. Then another. Lunch turns into a cracker standing at the counter.

The cost is not only time. It's the low hum under everything: the feeling that a real business would know this by now. That she is winging it, and that someone will eventually notice.

She also knows, quietly, that she is probably missing things. Not because anyone is cheating her, but because what she can't find, she can't count. And she is not going to dig through three email accounts at 4 pm on a Wednesday for a forty-euro cable.

So she shrugs, and a small piece of her year goes unrecorded.

The sentence from the sister

That evening her sister calls, mostly about a birthday. Ines mentions the tote bag, half as a joke.

"Do you have one place where everything goes?" her sister asks. "Even a simple one?"

"I have a bag," Ines says.

"That's a place," her sister says, kindly. "It's just not a list."

It's a small remark. But Ines turns it over for the rest of the evening while she does the dishes.

The pile on her desk was never really the problem. The problem is that every receipt is a little unfinished question, and she's been carrying two hundred of them in her head.

What if each one had a row, with a date, an amount and a short note? And what if the bigger picture, the "what's still left to do before the deadline", sat on a single page she could glance at with her coffee?

Not clever. Not complicated. Just somewhere to put things down.

A different kind of Monday

Picture the same desk some months later.

It's Monday, 9:05. The kettle is clicking off. Ines opens one file, and she knows what she'll see: the last few receipts she dropped in on Friday, a short checklist with most of the boxes ticked, and a bar that shows how far through the year-end tasks she is.

There's a date on it, too. Not a scary one, just a visible one. It's the kind of date you can look at calmly because you know what's between you and it.

When a client asks, mid-call, about a licence fee she paid on their behalf, she doesn't go quiet. She says, "One second, I'll find it," and she does. Eight seconds, not eighty minutes.

The tote bag is back to being what it was meant to be: a bag for carrying lunch.

Nobody gave her a medal. She doesn't feel like a different person. She just feels like someone whose year is in order, and that turns out to take up a lot less room in her head than she'd expected.

If you're in the tote-bag week

If you're reading this with a pile somewhere nearby, a bag, a drawer, a phone gallery of blurry photos, you are not behind in some special way. Most people who work for themselves have no back office. The pile is what a one-person business looks like when nobody has made it a home yet.

You don't have to fix the whole year this afternoon. You just need somewhere for things to go, and a short list that tells you what's left.
