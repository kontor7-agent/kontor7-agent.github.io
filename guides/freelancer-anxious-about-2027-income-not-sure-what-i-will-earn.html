Anxious About 2027 Income as a Freelancer? The Allotment Gate

2026-10-14

The kettle in the allotment shed has just clicked off. Your neighbour Marta is waving a pair of secateurs at you from two plots down, and she asks the question people ask in October: "So, how's next year looking?"

You smile. You say, "Good, good. Busy."

You are holding a mug of tea and a bag of garlic cloves for planting. And inside your head, a small voice says: I have no idea.

This is a story about that voice. The people in it are made up, but the feeling might not be.

The question you can't answer at the gate

Let's call you Jonas. You are a freelance illustrator, six years in. Your year has been decent. Two regular clients, a book cover, a few lucky referrals.

But "decent" is a feeling, not a number. When someone asks about 2027, you reach for it and find nothing under your hand.

You know one client has gone quiet about renewing. You know another one "might have something in spring." You know January is always strange. That is the entire forecast.

So you do what many self-employed people do. You carry it around. You carry it to the shop, to the bus, to bed. A low hum, not quite panic. Just a question you keep not asking, because you're afraid of how small the answer will be.

What the hum quietly costs

It doesn't cost money, exactly. It costs something harder to name.

It costs you the easy yes. A client offers a small, badly paid job in November and you take it, because "you never know." Not because you looked and decided. Because the fog made everything feel urgent.

It costs you the holiday you keep half-booking. Is a week off in August responsible? You can't say, because you don't know what the rest of the year looks like. So you don't book it, or you book it and spend it checking email.

It costs you a calm voice on the phone. When you can't see your own year, it's hard to say your price like you mean it. Every quote feels like it carries the whole twelve months on its shoulders.

And it costs you the plain, ordinary feeling of being a business. Not someone winging it. Someone who has a plan, even a small one.

The blank page on Sunday morning

That night, nothing dramatic happens. You don't have a breakthrough. You wash up, you let the dog out, you go to bed with the hum.

But the next Sunday, with coffee and no deadline, you do a small, brave thing. You open a fresh page and write one line at the top: 2027.

Then you just look at it.

You realise something. You never actually needed to know the future. Nobody does. What you needed was to see the shape of the year. How much you would like to earn. How many working weeks you really have once you subtract the holidays, the sick days, the slow January. What is already half-promised, and what is only hope.

That is a different question. And unlike "what will happen," it is one you can answer.

You don't need a crystal ball. You need a table with honest rows in it, and a place to put them where they won't blow away.

The same gate, a year later

Now imagine October 2027, same allotment, same garlic.

Marta waves the secateurs. "How's the year been?"

And you say, "Pretty close to plan, actually. One quarter was thin, one was better. I took my August week, properly."

Not because everything went perfectly. Because you had a rough map. When spring came in quieter than hoped, you saw it in March, not in June. You had a quiet evening to think about it instead of a cold sweat. You reached out to two old clients, calmly, with a clear sense of what you needed.

A normal Monday in that year feels small and steady. You open one page. You see the target, what has come in, what is likely, and how many weeks of capacity are left. It takes a few minutes. Then you close it and start drawing.

The hum is gone, or at least much quieter. Not because the future got certain. Because you stopped facing it with nothing in your hands.
