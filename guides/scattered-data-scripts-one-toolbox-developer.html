Scattered Data Scripts? The Shoe Repair Counter

2026-10-16

It's 8:40 on a Monday, and you're third in line at the shoe repair counter. You have a boot with a loose heel in one hand and your phone in the other.

On the phone is a message from a client. Can you send the cleaned customer list by lunch? Also, could you take a quick look at the new API before Thursday?

The list is a CSV export with dates in three formats and names that carry trailing spaces. You know you have a script for that. You wrote it in spring, or maybe last winter.

The man ahead of you is explaining to the cobbler what is wrong with his shoes. He is very sure. You scroll through your phone notes, looking for a file name you don't remember.

The script you know you wrote

Here is the quiet part nobody mentions. The work itself isn't hard. You've done it a dozen times.

What costs you is the hunt. Was it in the project folder, the old laptop backup, or that repo you named something clever and then forgot? You will find a version eventually. It will be 80% right, and it will quietly assume a column that this client's file doesn't have.

So you'll patch it. You'll patch it again for the file renaming job, which lives somewhere else entirely. Then there's the API review, which is a loose list of checks in your head and a half-finished note from a previous launch.

None of this is dramatic. It's three small jobs, each starting with the same sigh.

"Next," says the cobbler.

What it quietly costs

You hand over the boot. He turns it over, taps the heel, and says, "Ten minutes."

Ten minutes. He doesn't search his bench for a hammer. Everything he needs is within reach, in the place he left it, because he works with the same tools every day.

You stand there with that thought for a moment.

Your tools are scattered, and so is your attention. Every chore begins with rebuilding something you've already built. You lose an hour here and forty minutes there, and the work you're proud of gets pushed later in the day. You're also a little embarrassed. You're meant to be the person who automates things, and here you are doing the same chore by hand again.

There's a smaller cost too. When you answer the client with "I'll have it by two," you add a buffer in case the old script misbehaves. You pad every promise a little, because you don't fully trust your own toolbox.

The turning point, over a pair of boots

The cobbler hammers, and the sound is steady and unhurried. You think: what if my chores had a bench like this?

You don't need a bigger idea or a new framework. You need the data cleaning tools in one place, the file chores in the same place, and a calm, repeatable way to look over an API without inventing the checklist from scratch.

The tools would be made once and checked, then picked up on Monday morning without ceremony. You wouldn't have to remember where you left them.

You realise it doesn't have to be like this. Nobody has to rebuild the same tool every season.

A different Monday

Picture the same morning with that bench in place.

The client's message arrives. You read it, feel no flicker of dread, and open one folder. The messy CSV goes in and a tidy one comes out. You glance over it, the way you'd glance at a heel that has just been fixed.

The file-naming job takes a few minutes, not a whole afternoon. The API question goes onto a list you already trust, so Thursday stops feeling like a cliff edge.

You reply before lunch, and you don't pad the estimate. You say, "Done, and I've checked it."

Then you eat lunch away from your desk, because the hour you used to spend hunting for scripts is now yours.

The pleasure isn't speed so much as steadiness. You stop bracing yourself every time a messy file lands in your inbox. Nothing about the work is glamorous. It simply stops being a source of low-level worry.

Collecting your boots

"Ten minutes" turns out to be eleven. The cobbler slides the boot across the counter. The heel is firm, and the whole thing looks quietly good. You pay, and he nods and moves on to the next customer.

On the walk back you open your notes and finally delete the line that says find that CSV script?? It's been there for months.
