Promised Stock You Didn't Have? The Security Tray Queue

2026-10-22

Your phone buzzes just as you reach the grey plastic trays.

You are in the security queue at 6:40 on a Friday morning. Belt in one hand, boarding pass between your teeth. This is a long weekend away, the first in two years, and you have told everyone you will be "mostly offline".

The message is from Katrin, who runs a small pet shop two towns over. She stocks your leather dog leads. Hi! We're doing a little opening event on Saturday. Could I get twelve of the olive green? Do you have them?

You answer before you think. Yes, of course! I'll ship today.

Then you put your phone in the tray, and the tray goes into the machine, and in the quiet while it slides away, a small cold thought arrives.

Did the olive green go out in the last big order?

The shelf that lives in your head

You try to see it. The shelf in the spare room, the plastic boxes, the labels in your handwriting. You can picture the tan ones clearly. The black ones, probably four or five. The olive green...

You can picture a box. You cannot picture what is in it.

That is the strange part of running a small business alone. Your stock lives in two places: on the shelf, and in your memory. Most days the two agree. Then there is a day like this one, when you are standing in socks on a cold floor and they quietly don't.

The man behind you says, "You can go through." You walk through the arch. Nothing beeps. Your heart does something else.

What it costs, without ever showing up on an invoice

You collect your tray and sit on a bench near gate 14, and now the weekend has a small stone in its shoe.

You think about what happens if you are wrong. You write to Katrin on Friday afternoon from a hotel room: so sorry, I miscounted. She has already told her customers there will be leads at the event. She is kind about it, of course. But you know the sentence she is not writing. I thought you had this under control.

And you think about the cost you never put a number on. Not the lost sale, though that stings. The other thing. The small crack in how professional you feel. A real business knows what is on its shelf. You are not sure you do.

So you do what you always do. You open your notes app and scroll to the list called "stock??". Last edited five weeks ago. Half the lines are crossed out and the rest are question marks.

The gate agent calls the first row. You stay on the bench another minute, because a decision seems to be forming and you want to let it finish.

It did not have to feel like guessing

Here is what you notice, sitting there. The problem is not that you are careless. You counted properly in September. You wrote things down, mostly. The problem is that between the counting and the question, there is nothing. No place where "twelve went out Tuesday" quietly turns into "nine left, and that is getting low."

You have been using your memory as a warehouse system. And your memory is lovely, but it was never built for that.

A calmer way would not need you to be more vigilant. It would just be a single page that already knew. One you could open on your phone in a security queue and see, in plain colours, what is fine and what is running thin, before anyone has asked you anything.

You do not solve it on the bench. But you do something small. You text your sister, who is staying at the flat: Could you check the olive green box in the spare room and send me a photo?

Ten minutes later, a photo arrives. Seven leads. Not twelve.

You write to Katrin from the boarding queue, honestly and without panic: I have seven green ones ready to ship today. I can send the other five in a week, or add tan ones for the event if you'd like. She replies with a thumbs up and, a minute later, Seven is great, thanks for telling me straight!

The stone gets smaller. It does not disappear. You still know that this time it was luck and a sister, and not a system.

A different Monday

Picture the same kind of message, a few weeks from now.

It arrives on a Monday morning while you are standing at the kettle. Do you have twelve of the olive green? And you don't reach for the memory of a shelf. You open one sheet, you glance at one number, and you answer in the time it takes the water to boil. Yes. Or: not twelve, but seven now and more by the 20th.

Either answer is fine. What changes is that you give it calmly, with the voice of someone who knows their business. The reorder comes before the empty box, not after the apology. Saturday's opening is just an opening.

That is all a simple stock sheet really offers. Not cleverness. Just the end of the little cold thought at the grey trays.
