Client No-Shows Costing Me Money at My Hair Salon: The 10:15 Gap

It's Tuesday, 10:22 in the morning. The towels are folded. The colour is mixed in its little bowl, and it is starting to look tired.

Your phone lies face up next to the scissors. No message. No missed call. Just the booking app, still showing her name at 10:15.

You tell yourself she's stuck in traffic. You wipe the counter that is already clean. You check the phone again.

At 10:40 you stop pretending. She isn't coming.

The shrug you've practised

You say it out loud to the empty salon: "It happens." You've said it so many times that it almost sounds true.

But it sits somewhere under your ribs. Because you know that chair is yours. You rent it, or you pay for the room, or you carry the whole studio on your own. Either way, the clock keeps running whether anyone sits down or not.

The colour you mixed is wasted. The hour is gone. And somewhere in your head a quiet sum starts, one you don't really want to finish.

What did that cost? Forty? Sixty? More, if she was the highlights?

You don't know. That's the part that bothers you most.

The notebook that doesn't tell you

You do have a system, of sorts. The booking app shows who was meant to come. A notebook by the till has cash tips and the odd note. Your banking app shows card payments, a day or two late.

No-shows live in none of them. They're just a blank where a person should have been. A gap doesn't write itself down.

So on Sunday evening, when you try to add up the week, the numbers don't quite meet. Cash from memory. Card from the app. A few empty slots you remember only because they annoyed you.

You close the notebook with a small, tired feeling. The feeling that you're running a real business and flying it by feel.

The conversation you keep avoiding

Then there's the other thing. The message you've typed and deleted three times.

Hi! Just a gentle reminder that missed appointments...

You delete it. You don't want to sound harsh. She's a nice client. Most of them are. You'd rather swallow the loss than feel like the bad guy.

But swallowing it isn't free. It comes out sideways: a little resentment at the next booking, a sigh when a certain name pops up, a worry that you're not being fair to the clients who always show up.

Here's what you can't say to anyone: you're not sure whether the no-shows are a few unlucky mornings or a real hole in your month. And until you know, you can't decide anything calmly.

The small turn

Imagine the same Tuesday, a few weeks later.

She still doesn't come at 10:15. That part hasn't changed. People are people.

But at 10:40 you don't spiral. You open one sheet, tap in one line, and go make a tea. The gap has a name and a number now. It isn't a vague ache anymore. It's one row among others.

On Friday afternoon you glance at the month so far. Cash on one side, card on the other, and they agree with each other. Beside them, a plain figure: what the empty slots cost you so far. Not a verdict. Just something you can see.

And you notice something odd. The number is smaller than the dread was. Or maybe bigger. Either way, it's real, and real is easier to think about than fog.

What a normal Monday feels like

Monday, 8:50. The chair is clean. You open the week, not to panic, but to look.

You see which services are actually earning their time. You see that the long colour appointments pay differently than you assumed. You see which days tend to have holes.

None of it shouts. It just sits there, quiet and clear, like a well-lit mirror.

Maybe you decide to send a reminder the evening before, with words you chose when you weren't annoyed. Maybe you ask for a small deposit on the long appointments. Maybe you decide it's fine as it is. The point is that it's your decision, made with your eyes open, not made at 10:40 with a cold bowl of colour in front of you.

Sunday evening gets lighter too. The cash from the tip jar and the card payments line up without you reconstructing the week from memory. You close the laptop. You eat dinner.

The chair is still sometimes empty. But you're no longer the only one who doesn't know what that costs.
