Paid Late Once, Now Ignoring Invoice 2? The Key Counter

2026-10-19

The man behind the counter is holding your key up to the light, squinting at it like it owes him something.

"Two minutes," he says.

It is Tuesday, 11:15, in the hardware shop on the corner. You came for a spare key to the studio. Now you are standing between a wall of screws and a rack of garden gloves, and your phone is open to the one thing you promised yourself you would not look at before lunch.

Invoice number two. Sent twelve days ago. Due eight days ago. No reply. No payment. Not even the little grey tick that says someone opened it.

Let's call you Jonas. You build websites for small companies, mostly alone, mostly from a desk by the window. This client is a friendly one. That is what makes it so strange.

The first invoice, remembered

You remember the first invoice well. It was due on the 14th. On the 14th, nothing. On the 20th, you wrote a polite note and deleted it twice before sending. On the 27th, the money arrived with a one-line email: "Sorry, got buried! Thanks for the great work."

Thirteen days late. Smiley face.

You told yourself it was fine. It happens. Everybody gets busy. You did not say that the rent was due on the 1st, and that you had quietly moved things around to cover it.

So you did the next job, because they asked nicely and because you like them. And you sent invoice two.

Now here you are, holding your phone like a hot cup.

The thoughts that show up at the key counter

They are not dramatic thoughts. That is the odd part. They are small and tired.

Maybe they paid and it is just not showing yet.

Maybe I should wait until Friday.

If I write again, will they think I don't trust them?

If I don't write, will they think it doesn't matter?

There is a third thought underneath, quieter than the rest. Why does asking for money I have already earned feel like asking for a favour?

You have felt it before. The email drafted in your head while you brush your teeth. The mental arithmetic in the supermarket queue, checking whether the card will go through if the transfer has not landed. The way your shoulders rise a little every time the phone buzzes, and fall a little when it is only a newsletter.

Nobody sees any of this. From the outside, you are a person buying a key.

What the silence costs

The invoice is not the only thing that is open. It is also the half-hour you will lose this afternoon, rereading your own sentences. It is the slight coolness you will feel toward a client you actually like. It is the next quote you will write a bit too low, because you are tired of being the one who has to bring up money.

And it is the not-knowing. Is this a pattern, or a blip? Is this the moment to write firmly, or the moment to be gentle? What do you even say the second time, when the first time was already awkward?

You do not have a plan. You have a feeling and a very polite draft that says "just checking in!" and means "please, I'm worried."

The turn

The key machine whirs. The man blows metal dust off the key and sets it on the counter.

And you think of a friend who used to run a small print studio. She never seemed to dread this. One afternoon you asked her how. She shrugged and said she did not decide anything in the moment. She had already decided, ahead of time, what happened on day one after the due date, what on day seven, what on day fourteen. The words were already written. Friendly first. Clear second. Firm third. She did not have to feel brave. She just had to follow the next step.

At the time, you thought it sounded a bit cold.

Standing here, you realise it is the opposite. It is what lets you stay warm. The decision about tone was made on a calm day, not in the middle of a Tuesday knot.

A different Tuesday

Picture the same morning, with the plan already in place.

You open one sheet. The overdue invoice is highlighted, and next to it, in plain words, is today's step: a friendly reminder, ready to send. The email is already written, in your voice, polite and clear. You change the project name, press send, and close the laptop.

The whole thing takes four minutes. You do not rehearse it in the shower. You do not wonder if you are being rude. The next step, if nothing comes, is already waiting for day fourteen, and it is a little firmer, and that is fine, because that is simply what the plan says.

Then you go for lunch. You taste the lunch.

The client may pay that afternoon, or the next week. Either way, you are no longer carrying it around all day. You have done the grown-up thing, calmly, and it is out of your hands.

What you take home

You pay for the key and put it in your pocket. Outside, the air smells of rain on warm pavement.

You do not send the "just checking in!" email. Not yet. You decide that this evening, you will spend a little time building the thing you wish you had today: a short set of reminders for each stage, written once, so you never again have to improvise your dignity.
