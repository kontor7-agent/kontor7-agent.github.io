My Freelance Rate Is Too Low and No One Pushes Back: The Yes

2026-10-13

The yes that came too fast

It is 11:20 on a Thursday morning, and you are standing in the queue at the pharmacy with your phone in your hand.

The email arrived while you were choosing between two kinds of cough drops. Subject line: Re: Quote for the brochure project. You open it, already tensing your shoulders.

"Looks great, let's go ahead! Can you start Monday?"

That's it. No question. No "could you do a little better?" No awkward silence on a call.

You should feel good. Instead, something small and cold turns over in your stomach.

Four seconds. That is how long it took them to say yes. You can almost hear it.

The person ahead of you pays for a prescription. You step forward and don't hear the cashier the first time.

The thought you don't say out loud

On the walk home, the thought arrives, the one you've been politely ignoring for months.

Maybe I'm too cheap.

Nobody has ever pushed back on your rate. Not once, in a long while. You used to think that meant you were good. Lately it feels like the opposite, like a shop where everything sells out on the first day because the price tag is a typo.

Let's call you Mira, a freelance illustrator, three years in. (Mira is made up, but you may know her.) She tells herself that clients are simply happy. That her work speaks for itself. That a lower rate is how you build a base.

But there is a quieter version of the story, and she knows it. The rate she quoted two years ago was a number she picked on a nervous afternoon. It was "not scary." It was "probably fine." She has never really changed it, because changing it means a conversation, and a conversation means the chance of hearing no.

So the number stays. And every easy yes adds one more small grain of doubt.

What it quietly costs

Here is the part nobody puts in a portfolio.

Undercharging doesn't announce itself. It shows up as a Sunday afternoon that feels a little heavier than it should. As a project you resent halfway through, though you loved it at the start. As the faint irritation when a client sends "just one more small change," and you smile and do it, because the hourly maths behind the project has long since stopped working.

It shows up as saying "sure, no problem" with a tight jaw.

It shows up as the evening you stay at the desk until eight, because the fee only makes sense if you work faster than is reasonable.

And it shows up as a strange kind of loneliness. You can't tell a friend "my clients agree with everything I ask," because it sounds like bragging. But it isn't pride you feel. It is the suspicion that you've been giving something away without anybody noticing, least of all you.

The question under the question

That Thursday afternoon, Mira opens a blank sheet and stares at it.

She doesn't ask, "What should I charge?" That feels too big, too much like a verdict on her worth.

She asks a smaller, kinder question: What does my working year actually need to cover?

Not what's fair. Not what the market tolerates. Just: the rent, the health insurance, the software subscriptions, the weeks she won't be working, the days that vanish into admin and emails nobody pays for. The plain, boring arithmetic of keeping a one-person business alive.

It's the first time the rate stops feeling like a judgement and starts feeling like a fact.

A fact is something you can say out loud. A fact doesn't need an apology.

She doesn't decide anything that afternoon. There is no dramatic raise, no triumphant email. But something loosens. For the first time in a long while, the number has a reason behind it, and it isn't "I hope this is okay."

A different kind of Monday

Fast-forward a few weeks. Not to a fairy tale, just to an ordinary Monday.

Mira makes coffee. She has a new quote to send, a fresh client, a new project. She opens her file and the rate is there, steady, with a reason sitting behind it. She doesn't hover over the send button for twenty minutes. She doesn't pad the email with "I know it's a lot, but..."

She writes two clear sentences. She sends it.

Maybe the client says yes. Maybe they ask for a smaller scope. Maybe, once in a while, they say it's out of budget, and she finds, to her own surprise, that she can live with that. A "no" at a fair price stings less than a "yes" at a price that makes her resentful.

At six, she closes the laptop. The evening is hers. The knot from the pharmacy queue has gone quiet.

Nothing about her talent changed. She simply stopped guessing.
