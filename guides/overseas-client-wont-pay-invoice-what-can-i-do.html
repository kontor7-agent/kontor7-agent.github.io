Overseas Client Won't Pay Your Invoice? The Second Clock

2026-11-08

The second clock on your phone says 06:40 in Vancouver. It is 15:40 where you are, standing in the queue at the post office with a parcel under your arm.

You added that clock in March, when the project started. It felt exciting then. A client on another continent. A company with a proper logo and a proper budget.

Now you look at it the way you look at a pot that refuses to boil.

The invoice is 38 days overdue. You know the number without opening anything. You know the amount too, and what it was supposed to cover: the rent, a chunk of the tax money you set aside, the new monitor you keep not buying.

The email you start in your head

The queue moves one person forward. You begin drafting.

Hi Daniel, just a gentle reminder...

Too soft. He has already ignored two gentle reminders.

Dear Daniel, as per our agreement...

Too stiff. You sound like a letter from a solicitor, and you are an illustrator in a cardigan.

So you try the other voice, the one that has been building quietly for a week. This is the third time I'm asking. You delete it before it even reaches the screen, because the last thing you want is to burn a client who might hire you again.

This is the part nobody talks about when they say "just chase it". The distance makes everything harder. You can't call at a sensible hour. You don't know if the silence means we forgot, accounts payable is slow, or we've decided you're not worth the effort. You don't know whose desk the invoice is sitting on. You don't know if "the payment run" is a real thing or a polite fog.

You only know that every hour you spend wondering is an hour you don't spend drawing.

What it quietly costs

It costs you the small things first.

You check your banking app at red lights. You say "fine, fine" when a friend asks how work is going. You turn down a little job from a local café because you're afraid to start something new while the old thing is still hanging open.

Then it costs you the bigger thing: the feeling that you run a real business. A real business has a way of dealing with this. A real business doesn't wait three weeks for the courage to write one paragraph.

You reach the counter. The woman weighs your parcel and says something about stamps. You nod. You are somewhere else, composing a sentence that begins "I wanted to check in" for the fourth time.

The sentence you didn't have to invent

Here is what changes the afternoon, though you don't know it yet.

That evening, a friend of yours, a copywriter who works with clients in three countries, mentions something offhand over a video call. She isn't giving a lecture. She's chopping onions with the camera propped against the kettle.

"I stopped writing those emails from scratch," she says. "I've got them ready. Day one after due date, a friendly one. Then a firmer one. Then the one that says what happens next. I just look at my list, it tells me which one is next, and I send it."

You ask, a little too quickly, "And you don't feel like you're begging?"

She laughs and wipes her eyes with her wrist. "I used to. Then I realised the begging was the improvising. When the words are already calm, and you already know the next step, it's just... admin. Boring, polite admin."

Boring, polite admin. You turn the phrase over for the rest of the night.

A different Monday

Picture a Monday a few weeks later.

It is 09:15. You have a coffee that is still hot, which is already a small miracle. You open one overview and see, in plain rows, who owes you what and for how long. The overseas invoice is there, marked as due for the next step. No guessing, no dread-scrolling through old threads to remember what you wrote last time.

You open the matching email. It is warm and clear, and it mentions the invoice number, the date, and a simple request for a payment date. You change the first line to sound like you, press send, and close the laptop lid halfway.

It takes six minutes.

The second clock on your phone still says it is the middle of the night in Vancouver. You smile at it. Let them wake up. The message will be waiting, polite and unmistakable, and you will be at your desk drawing something you actually enjoy.

Nothing about the client has changed. They may pay on Thursday, or they may need one more nudge. But you have changed. You are no longer waiting for permission to ask for what you are owed. You have a rhythm, and the rhythm doesn't care what time zone anyone is in.

That is the quiet relief: not that every client suddenly behaves, but that you stop carrying the whole thing in your chest.
