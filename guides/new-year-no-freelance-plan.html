New Year, No Freelance Plan? The Availability Question

2026-10-24

The call comes on a grey Tuesday between Christmas and New Year. You pick up in the hallway, still in your coat, one shoe off.

It's a client you like. She says the spring project is a go, and then she asks the question: "So when could you start? And how much room do you have in Q1?"

You hear yourself say, "Let me just check my calendar."

You don't need to check. You already know what you'll find.

The calendar that tells you nothing

You walk to the desk and open it. January is a few scattered appointments, a dentist and a reminder to renew something. That's all. It doesn't say who is booked, who owes you money, or how many weeks you can really afford to work for this price.

The truth is that you have no plan. You have a feeling, and the feeling is a low hum that has run since October: I should sit down and think about next year.

You kept meaning to. You thought about it in the shower, in the supermarket queue, and on the walk where you promised yourself you'd start tomorrow. Then December filled up with deadlines, family and a client who wanted "just one more small thing".

So now you stand in a half-lit room with a friendly voice in your ear and no honest answer.

"Mid-January should work," you say. It comes out smoothly, and you aren't sure it's true.

What the hum has been costing you

After you hang up, you stay in the chair and the hum gets louder.

It isn't dramatic. It's small and ordinary. You say yes to things because you can't see why you should say no. You quote a price on the spot because you haven't decided what a good month looks like. You work a Saturday and can't say whether it was needed or just habit.

It also costs you something quieter. When someone asks what your year looks like, you'd like to answer like a person who runs a real business. Instead you answer like someone holding a lot of loose ends.

Nobody tells you that part. They tell you to "set goals". They don't say that goals feel unreachable when you can't remember which invoices are still open, or whether last March was slow because of the season or because you stopped showing up for yourself.

You open a blank document and type 2027. You look at it for a minute. Then you close it.

The page that didn't ask for much

Two days later, on the 30th, a friend you share a coworking desk with leans over. You haven't heard her say a word about resolutions. She's flipping through a printed booklet with a pen tucked into the spine.

"What's that?" you ask.

"Just my year," she says. "On paper. I got tired of it living in my head."

She shows you a monthly page, a plain grid with space on the side for what matters that month. She shows you a weekly page with room for three things, not thirty. At the back is a page for clients and a page for what you've sent and what has come in.

There's nothing clever about it. It doesn't try to fix you. It just gives everything a place to land.

"I fill it in on Fridays," she says. "Ten minutes, with tea. That's all it takes."

You realise you've been waiting for a big, serious planning session that would never come, because it always needed a free weekend you never had. What you needed was a small, kind structure that could survive a normal week.

A different Monday

Picture a Monday in the middle of next February.

You sit down with your coffee and open the planner to this week. You already know who is waiting on you, because you wrote it on Friday. You know which invoice should have landed by now. You know the Thursday afternoon is free, because you protected it.

Then the phone rings and a new client asks, "Are you free in April?"

You turn a page. You see the month, with its shape and gaps. You say, "I have room from the second week. Let me tell you what that would look like."

Your voice doesn't wobble. You're not guessing, because you can see it.

The feeling is hard to name until you've had it. It's something like standing in a room where the lights work, and nothing about the room has changed except that you can see it. You close the laptop at six without a low-level dread about what you forgot.
