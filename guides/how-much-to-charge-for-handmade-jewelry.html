How Much to Charge for Handmade Jewelry? The Deli Queue

2026-10-31

It's Saturday, a quarter past eleven, and you are third in line at the deli counter.

You're holding a ticket, a reusable bag and a vague plan for lunch. The woman in front of you turns around and looks at your hand.

"Sorry, is that ring handmade? It's lovely."

It is. You made it in March, hammered silver with a small green stone. You nod and say thank you, and you feel the little warm flush that never gets old.

"Do you sell them? What would one cost?"

And your mouth opens before your head has caught up.

"Oh, um. Thirty-five? Something like that."

She smiles and says she'll look you up. Then the man behind the counter calls your number, and you order a hundred grams of ham as if nothing happened.

The number that came out of nowhere

You carry the ham home. The number comes with you.

Thirty-five. Where did that come from? It wasn't a calculation. It was the amount that felt like it wouldn't make anyone frown. It was the price of not being embarrassed.

You think about the silver, which went up again. You think about the stone, the little packaging boxes, the stall fee at the last market and the payout from your online shop that was somehow smaller than the sales. You think about the three evenings that ring really took, because the first two attempts went in the bin.

You don't think about those evenings in euros, though. You never have.

That's the quiet thing about pricing handmade work. It rarely feels like a business decision. It feels like asking a stranger to judge you, and then guessing what they'll say before they say it.

What guessing costs

Back in your kitchen, you open the notebook where the prices live. Some are crossed out. Some have a question mark. One says "maybe 28??" in your own handwriting, and you can't remember why.

The bestsellers sell well, and that has always felt like proof. But sitting there with the ham still in the bag, a small, uncomfortable thought arrives.

What if they sell well because they're cheap?

Nobody warns you about this one. A product that sells out isn't automatically a product that pays you. It might just be the one where you're doing the most work for the least return, with a smile on your face.

So you do what many makers do. You promise yourself you'll sort it out properly soon. After the autumn markets. After the holiday orders. After you've found a free afternoon, which, if you're honest, you haven't had since June.

The sentence you didn't have

The lunch queue replays in your head on Sunday too.

You imagine a different version. The woman asks, "What would one cost?" and you say, calmly, "Sixty-eight. I can make it in your size." No flush, no apology tucked into the end of the sentence, no "something like that."

What would you need for that? Not courage, exactly. Courage is what you use when you're not sure. What you'd need is to know.

To know what that ring really costs you: the silver, the stone, the box, the share of the stall fee, the cut the platform takes. To know what's left over after all of it, and whether that leftover pays for your time or only for your materials.

To see it in one place, for every piece, instead of in your head at 23:00 while you're trying to sleep.

A different Saturday

Picture it three weeks from now. Same deli, same ticket in your hand.

Someone admires your earrings and asks what they cost. You tell them the price in an ordinary voice. They nod, or they say "oh, that's more than I expected," and it doesn't land in your stomach the way it used to. You know the number isn't a mood. It has reasons behind it, and you can see them.

That Sunday you sit down with the market cash box on one side and last week's online orders on the other. Instead of two notebooks that never agree, you enter both in the same place. It takes you about ten minutes. The picture fills in: which pieces earn their keep, which ones quietly don't, and where a small adjustment would change a lot.

You don't become someone who loves spreadsheets. You become someone who isn't afraid of the question anymore.

And the evening is yours. You make something new because you want to, not because you're trying to out-produce a price you're not sure about.
