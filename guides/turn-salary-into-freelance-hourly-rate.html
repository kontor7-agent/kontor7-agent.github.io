Salary to Hourly Rate as a Freelancer? The Farewell Cake

2026-10-21

The question over the cake

It's Thursday, 15:20, in the office kitchen. There's a supermarket sponge on a paper plate, a card with too many signatures, and a coffee machine that still groans before it pours.

It's Mira's last day but one. Eight years as a UX designer, and on Monday she starts on her own.

She's holding a plastic fork when Jonas from finance leans on the counter and asks it, in the friendliest way possible.

"So what will you charge? Per hour, I mean."

She laughs. She says, "Still working on it."

The truth is she has been working on it for three weeks. And she has not got anywhere.

The number on the back of the envelope

There is a number. Of course there is. On the back of an envelope in her bag, she divided her yearly salary by the hours in a work year and got a figure that looked almost reasonable.

Then she stared at it for a long time.

Because something about it felt wrong, and she couldn't say what. Was it too high? Nobody she knew would pay that. Was it too low? It was her whole salary, after all, so it couldn't be.

Then came the quieter thoughts. Her salary came with paid holidays. It came with sick days, a laptop she never had to buy, and a payroll department that simply made things happen on the 28th. Somewhere in that number, all of that was hiding. And she had no idea how to find it.

So she did what many of us do. She searched for the answers other people gave, found five different ones, and closed the tab.

The fear nobody mentions at farewell parties

The fear isn't really about maths.

It's the picture of a first client call, three weeks from now, when someone asks about her rate and she hears her own voice go thin. She'd say a number, then quickly add "but that's flexible" before the other person has even reacted.

It's the picture of saying a number that's too low, getting an instant "great, yes," and feeling her stomach drop. Because now it's the number, and everyone knows it, and raising it later means a conversation she'd rather not have.

It's the thought, quiet but persistent: what if I get this wrong and don't notice for a year?

She had a salary for eight years. Someone else did the hard thinking about what her time was worth. Now it's hers to decide, with no one to check it.

That's a heavy thing to carry over a slice of sponge cake.

What Jonas said next

Jonas didn't give her a number. He's in finance, but he isn't her accountant, and he said so.

What he said was smaller. He said, "When I started consulting on the side, I made the mistake of treating my salary as the finish line. It's really more of a starting point."

Then someone called him back to a meeting. He took a last bite of cake and left.

Mira stood there with that sentence. A starting point.

It shifted something. She had been asking, "How do I turn this number into that number?" as if there were one correct conversion, like euros to dollars, and she was scared of missing it.

But maybe the question was different. Maybe it was: What does a working week of mine actually have to cover? The days she won't bill. The weeks off she'd like to take. The software she'll pay for herself now. The tax she should set aside, with a professional to check the details.

That's not one scary calculation. It's a handful of small, ordinary questions, and each one has an answer if you sit down and look.

Sunday evening, a different kind of quiet

Three days later, Mira sits at her own kitchen table with a mug of tea and a laptop that is, for the first time, only hers.

She isn't panicking. That's the surprise.

She has her yearly figure, her honest guess at how many weeks she wants to rest, and her real costs, written down where she can see them. She puts them in one place and watches a minimum rate appear. It isn't a perfect number and it isn't a promise. It's a floor, the amount below which she'd be quietly paying to work.

It's higher than the envelope number. She notices she isn't alarmed by that. It's just what's true.

She takes a breath and says it aloud once, to the empty kitchen. A number, in her normal voice, with no "but it's flexible" tacked on the end.

It sounds strange. It also sounds like a person who runs a real business.

On Monday, when the first prospective client asks what she charges, she won't have to improvise. She'll know where the number came from. And knowing that makes a quiet difference. The call goes slower and calmer. She finishes it and doesn't replay it in the shower.

A starting point, not a verdict

A number you worked out calmly is not the end of the story. Rates move. You'll learn what your work is really worth after a few projects, and you can adjust. But a calm starting point beats a guess made in a panic, every time.
