Stall Cash Doesn't Match Sales? The Tailgate at 5:10

2026-10-18

The last customer leaves at ten to five. You start folding the tablecloth, and the cash tin sits open on the tailgate, waiting.

Tomas makes leather wallets and keyrings. He is an illustrative character, but you may know him. He has stood behind the same trestle table for three seasons, and he has a pencil behind his ear and a small notebook with a rubber band round it.

Today was a good day. He knows it was a good day because his feet hurt and his voice is hoarse.

So why does the tin say something different from the notebook?

The count that won't land

He counts the coins into little towers. Then the notes. Then he counts them again, because the first time he lost his place when a neighbour asked if he had a spare bag.

He starts the float: 60 in the morning, small notes and coins, the same as always. He subtracts it. He looks at the notebook, where the day is written in a handwriting that got faster and messier as the queue grew.

The two numbers are not close enough to call the same. Not wildly apart, just apart. Enough to notice and not enough to be sure.

Maybe he gave wrong change at lunchtime. Maybe someone paid by card and he also wrote it in the cash column. Maybe the keyring bundle he sold at a discount was never written down at all.

There is a card reader payout coming in a few days, which will say a third number. He knows it will not match either.

The part nobody says out loud

The missing amount is not what bothers him. It is the small, cold thought underneath.

A real business would know.

He has sold things for years. People tell him his work is beautiful. And yet at the end of every market day he stands at the back of a car, doing sums on a phone calculator with leather dust on his thumbs, and he cannot say what the day was worth.

Not the takings. What the day was worth. After the pitch fee, the fuel, the card charges, the hide he bought, and the hours he spent cutting and stitching on weeknights.

He has never worked that out. Somewhere he suspects that the wallets he loves making might be the ones that earn him the least, and he would rather not look too closely.

So he does what makers do. He rounds the number up, writes "good day" on the page, and drives home.

Monday morning, same shoebox

On Monday the receipts are in a carrier bag in the hall. The notebook is on the kitchen table next to a half-eaten slice of toast. A customer messages to ask if the navy wallet is still available.

He thinks it is. He remembers selling one. Or two? He goes to the shelf in the spare room and counts, and the count does not agree with what he told her last week either.

Nothing dramatic happens. No disaster. Just a low hum of not-quite-knowing that follows him from the stall to the kitchen to the spare room, and sits beside him when he tries to price the next batch.

That hum is what costs him. Not the few euros in the tin. The Sunday evening he spends with the notebook instead of with his family. The quiet unease whenever someone asks, "So, is it going well?"

The moment it shifts

Then one Saturday, a woman at the next stall, who sells hand-printed cards, is packing up beside him. She snaps her tin shut at 5:05, jots two lines, and says she's off.

He asks how she does it so fast. She shrugs. "I stopped trying to remember the day. I just put it down as it happened, in one place, and the end of the day is mostly a check."

That is all. No grand system, nothing he has to be clever about.

He thinks about it the whole drive home. What if the float, the sales, the stock and the costs all lived in the same place, so that closing the tin was a two-minute look instead of a forensic investigation? What if the number at the end simply agreed with itself?

What a calmer end of day feels like

Picture the same tailgate, a few markets later.

It is ten to five. The last customer leaves. Tomas folds the tablecloth, and the tin sits open on the tailgate, and he is not bracing.

He counts the cash once. The number sits where he expects it to. If it is a little off, he knows roughly where to look, because the day was written down as it happened, not rebuilt from memory afterwards.

He knows which wallets are running low before the customer asks. He knows what the day left him with after the pitch fee and the materials, and he is allowed to feel good about it, or to notice it was thin and adjust, without any guilt.

He is home by half past six. The notebook stays in the bag.

On Monday morning, when someone messages about the navy wallet, he answers in under a minute. He knows.

That is all it is. Not riches. Just a stall day that ends when the stall ends.
