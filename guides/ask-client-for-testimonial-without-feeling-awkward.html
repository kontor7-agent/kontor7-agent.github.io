Ask a Client for a Testimonial Without Feeling Awkward

2026-10-24

It's Monday, 9:12. Your coffee is still too hot to drink.

On the screen: a blank email addressed to Marta, the client whose website you finished on Friday. She wrote back within minutes. "This is perfect, thank you so much!" Two exclamation marks. A smiley.

This is the moment everyone says to use. Ask now, while the glow is fresh.

So you type: "Hi Marta, I was wondering if you might possibly..."

You delete it.

The sentence that won't write itself

It's not that you don't want a testimonial. Your own page has a gap where three kind sentences from a client should be. You know that a stranger deciding whether to hire you would feel calmer with them.

But asking feels like holding out a hat.

You think: She already paid. She already said thanks. Isn't that enough? Who am I to ask for more?

Then the other voice arrives: What if she says yes and writes something lukewarm? What if she says no? What if she just never answers, and now every future email carries that little silence?

So you do what many freelancers do. You close the draft. You tell yourself you'll ask next week, when the project feels less fresh. Or more fresh. You're not sure which.

What the waiting quietly costs

Next week becomes next month. Marta's glow fades. Another project ends, another "thank you" arrives, and another draft joins the first one in the folder.

Nothing dramatic happens. That's the trouble. Nobody sends you an invoice for the testimonials you didn't collect.

But it shows up in small places:

  • A new enquiry arrives, and you scroll your own site looking for proof that you're good. You find your own words, saying so.
  • You hesitate over your rate, because there's nothing outside you to lean on.
  • You feel a little less like a real business. Real businesses have clients who say things about them. You have clients who say things to you, in emails you never use.

And underneath it all, the odd feeling that you're being polite to a fault, at your own expense.

The call that changed the angle

Three weeks later you're on a video call with a different client, Tomasz, wrapping up a small branding job. He says, unprompted: "Honestly, if anyone asks me who to use, I'll send them to you."

You smile. You say thanks. And after you hang up, you sit there with a thought that arrives quietly:

He just offered. He wants to help. He'd be glad to.

It hadn't occurred to you before. You'd been picturing the request as a favour you were extracting. But for Tomasz, and probably for Marta too, a testimonial is a small, easy kindness. A way to say thanks in a form that actually helps. Most people like being asked. They just like it more when the ask is clear, short and easy to say yes to.

The awkwardness was never in the asking. It was in the wording you couldn't find at 9:12 on a Monday, with a hot coffee and a cursor blinking at you.

An ordinary Monday, after

Now picture the same Monday, a few months on.

Project done. Client says thanks. You open a message you already have the shape of: warm, two sentences about what the work was, one clear question, an easy way out if they'd rather not. You change the name, add one detail about the project that only you would remember, and read it once.

It sounds like you. On a good day.

You press send, and you do not refresh your inbox.

The coffee is the right temperature. You start the next task.

A day or two later, there might be a reply with three sentences that make you a little pink in the face. Or there might be a polite "I'm swamped, maybe later," which is fine, because you left them room, and the relationship is exactly as good as before. Either way, the knot isn't there. Asking has stopped being an event. It's just part of finishing a job properly, like sending the final files.

Your page slowly fills with real words from real people. And the next time someone asks your rate, you answer in a steadier voice.
