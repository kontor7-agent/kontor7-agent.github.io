How to Politely Ask a Client for Late Payment: The Tuesday Lift

2026-10-13

The lift between floors

It is 11:20 on a Tuesday, and you are standing in the lift of a co-working building, phone in hand, thumb hovering.

The invoice was due eleven days ago. You know the number by heart: 1,850. You know the client's name, the project, even the day you sent it. What you don't know is how to ask.

You open your email app. A draft is already there, from last week. Hi Marta, I hope you're well! Just a quick note to say that, um...

The lift doors open. You close the app and walk out as if nothing is wrong.

The sentence you can't write

You are good at your work. Clients tell you so. Nobody has ever had to chase you for a deliverable.

But this one small email feels like standing in a doorway with your hand out.

So you do what many people in your position do. You wait. Maybe it's an oversight. Maybe accounting is slow. Maybe if you give it one more day, it will simply arrive and you won't have to say anything at all.

The quiet thoughts keep going in the background:

What if she thinks I'm pushy? What if she's having a hard month? What if I ask and the next project doesn't come?

And under those, the one you don't say aloud: a real business wouldn't have to ask.

That last one isn't true. Real businesses send reminders all the time. They just have the words ready.

What waiting costs

Nothing dramatic happens. That is the trouble with it.

You check your banking app at the supermarket queue, in the pause between two calls, and once at 6:50 in the morning before you are properly awake. You do the mental arithmetic of rent, the next tax payment, the software subscriptions that renew on the 15th.

You are a little shorter with your partner at dinner. You accept a small extra request from the same client, because you don't want to be "difficult" while you are owed money.

The invoice is not only late. It is taking up room in your head, and that room was meant for your work.

The draft you finally sent

Picture a Thursday instead. Same lift, same phone, a little different.

A friend, a translator who has sat in this exact spot, said something offhand over coffee: "Honestly, the reminder isn't the hard part. Finding the first sentence is."

That landed. You have never been short of effort. You were short of a starting line.

So that afternoon you try something plain. You write down, in a few words, what is true: the invoice number, the due date, that you'd like to know when to expect payment. No apology. No drama. Then you shape it into a tone that sounds like you on a good day: warm, steady, clear.

It takes four minutes. You read it once. It does not sound like begging. It sounds like a person who runs a business and assumes the other person does too.

You press send. Your stomach does a small flip, then settles.

Marta replies the next morning: So sorry, it got stuck on our side. Paying today.

That is it. No tension, no awkward silence. Just a normal reply from a normal person.

A Monday when it's handled

Now imagine a Monday a few weeks later.

You make coffee. You open your inbox. One client is two days past due. You know which one, because you glance at the list instead of digging through folders.

You don't brace. You pick the gentle version of your reminder, change a name and a number, read it once, and send it before the kettle clicks off.

Then you go on with your day. A design brief. A call at ten. Lunch outside, maybe.

The money may arrive Wednesday or the following Monday. Either way, it is not sitting on your chest. Asking has become something you do, like replying to a meeting invite. Ordinary. Polite. Done.

The relief isn't about the cash alone, though that helps. It is about no longer arguing with yourself in a lift.

Words are a skill you can borrow

Nobody teaches freelancers how to write these messages. You learn it by getting it wrong, by over-apologising, or by going quiet for too long.

But the right wording does exist, and you don't have to invent it from scratch each time, at the worst moment, with your pulse in your ears. There are gentle first nudges, firmer second ones, and notes for the client who always "never got the invoice". You can keep the ones that sound like you and ignore the rest.

The tone stays yours. The blank page just stops being the problem.

If you recognise yourself

If you have an unsent draft sitting in your email right now, you are not behind and you are not unprofessional. You are someone who cares how they come across, which is a good quality in a person you'd want to work with.
