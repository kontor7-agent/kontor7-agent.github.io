Quoted a Job, Barely Making Money After Materials?

2026-10-22

The pile of receipts on the passenger seat

It is 12:40 on a Wednesday, and you are parked outside a bakery you have no intention of entering. The engine is off. The window is down an inch.

On the passenger seat sits a paper bag from the builders' merchant. Inside: a crumpled receipt for tile adhesive, another for grout, a third one that has gone soft from a coffee you spilled in March.

You finished the bathroom for the Hendersons on Monday. They were lovely. They said "it looks fantastic" twice, and the older one shook your hand.

You quoted the job on a Thursday, standing in their doorway, with a tape measure still in your hand. You said a number. It felt fair. It felt even a little bold.

Now you are doing a sum on the back of an envelope, and the number at the bottom does not look like a number you would have said out loud.

The sum you did not want to do

Materials first. Then the second trip, because the first load was short. Then the petrol. Then the day you went back to fix the corner that was not quite right, which you did not charge for, because who charges for that?

Then you try to count the hours. You were sure it was four days. Was it four and a half? You remember a Tuesday that seemed to vanish.

You put the pen down.

The unkind thought arrives, the one you rarely say to anyone: I worked a full week and I might have earned less than a part-time shift at the supermarket.

It is not that you are bad at the work. The work is good. The grout lines are straight. It is that the money part happened in your head, on a doorstep, in about forty seconds, and your head was being polite.

What it quietly costs

The cost is not only the missing money, though that is real. It is the small tightening in your chest when the next enquiry comes in. It is the way you now hesitate to say a price at all.

It is the Sunday evening when you tell yourself you will "sort the paperwork properly" and then watch a film instead, because the paperwork feels like a verdict.

It is the receipts in the bag. You know you will need them. You also know that finding them again in January, when everything is cold and urgent, will not be a pleasant afternoon.

And it is a quieter feeling underneath. That you are good with your hands but somehow not a real business. That other people have a system, and you have a bag.

The call that changed the question

You are still in the car when a friend rings. She runs a small gardening round, two people and a van. She is only calling about a borrowed ladder.

But you mention the Hendersons, half joking. "Great job. No idea if I made anything."

She is quiet for a second, then laughs, kindly. "I used to say that every month," she says. "Then I stopped asking whether the job was good, and started asking what was left at the end of it."

She does not lecture. She says she just writes it all in one place now, before she names a price and after the job is done, so the two can look at each other.

You hang up. The envelope is still on your knee.

It is a small idea, almost embarrassingly plain. But it lands. The problem was never that you misjudged one bathroom. It was that nothing was there to catch you, so every quote was a guess made in a doorway.

It did not have to feel like this.

A different Thursday

Picture the next doorway instead.

You are measuring up a kitchen splashback. The client is hovering. You are not rushing, and you are not guessing. You have the materials roughly priced, your own day rate in front of you, and a clear sense of what this job needs to leave behind for it to be worth doing.

You say the number. It comes out calm. It is not a bold number or an apologetic one. It is just the right one.

A week later the job is done. The receipts went into one place as you bought them, not into a bag. You can see what you spent, what you charged, and what is actually left. If someone still owes you, you know who, without opening three apps and a notebook.

On Friday you finish at five. Not because you rushed, but because there is no second shift of guilt waiting at the kitchen table.

The margin is no longer a rumour you hear about weeks later. It is something you can see, in ten quiet minutes, with a cup of tea.

If this is your envelope
