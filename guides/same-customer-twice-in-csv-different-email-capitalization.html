Same Customer Twice in Your CSV? The Two Name Badges

2026-11-05

The badge printer at the back of the bookshop makes a small, steady grinding sound. You are standing next to it at 17:40, forty minutes before doors open, watching the first sheet come out.

Two badges say Anna Weber.

You pick them up and hold them side by side. Same name, same publisher, same table number. You turn back to the laptop balanced on a stack of folding chairs and scroll the guest list. There she is on row 14, and again on row 61.

The emails look almost identical: anna.weber@kleinverlag.de and Anna.Weber@KleinVerlag.de.

The thing your eyes skipped

You had checked this list. You are sure you had. You sorted by surname, scanned it, nodded at it. But a sort puts those two rows forty lines apart, and your eye doesn't read capital letters in an email address. It reads Anna, publisher, fine.

So the list lied politely, and now you are holding two badges and doing sums in your head. If Anna is in twice, who else is? If thirty badges are printing, is it really thirty guests? And what did the invitation mailing do last week? Did she get it twice? Did she wonder why?

The printer keeps grinding. Nobody has noticed yet, and you feel that small hot knot under your ribs.

What it quietly costs

It's one duplicate. It isn't a disaster. Nobody will be hurt by an extra badge.

But it takes something. Your calm, mostly. You stop trusting the list, and once you stop trusting it you start checking everything by hand: name by name, row by row, with a pen and the printed sheet, while the caterer asks where the extra tables go.

It costs the next morning too. You will open the same export, a bit tense, and you'll wonder what else is hiding in it. Is the headcount you gave the venue right? Did the thank-you mail go out to some people twice and to others not at all? Does the person who looks after your list for you, which is you, actually know who is in it?

None of that is dramatic. It's just a low hum of I'm not sure, and it sits in the room with you at every event.

The rest of the evening

You do what anyone does. You pull a pen from the cash desk and go through the list line by line. You find Anna. You find a second pair, Tomasz and a tomasz with a lowercase initial, which turns out to be the same person. You delete three rows and are left with a number you aren't sure you believe.

The doors open at 18:30. A woman with a lanyard and a tote bag walks in and says her name. You find her badge on the first try, and your shoulders drop an inch.

But you spend the first hour glancing at the guest sheet whenever someone new arrives, half expecting it to catch you out again.

The moment it doesn't have to be like this

The turning point comes the next Tuesday, in a much plainer place than a bookshop: a coffee on your desk, a quiet inbox, the same export open on screen.

You think about Anna. You think: the computer can tell that these are the same person. It only needs to be asked to look past the capital letters.

That's all it was. Not a big system, not a database, not a weekend of learning something new. A small, boring check that treats Anna.Weber@KleinVerlag.de and anna.weber@kleinverlag.de as one address. It runs before you print, before you mail, before you count. And it shows you what it would change before it changes anything.

You realise the knot in your stomach was never about Anna. It was about not having that check.

A normal Monday, afterwards

Picture the next event. The export lands in your downloads folder on Monday morning, a bit untidy, as exports are. You open it, run the cleaning step, and read a short note of what it found: two pairs of the same person, one phone number with a stray space, a date written the wrong way round. You glance down the list, nod, and keep going.

The guest count is the guest count. You tell the venue a number and it stays the number.

On the day, the printer grinds out thirty badges for thirty guests. You don't hover over it. You have time to put a hand-written sign on the signing table, and to talk to the baker about where the tray of cardamom buns should go.

When Anna arrives, she gets one badge, one welcome email in her inbox, and one version of you who looks like you know what you're doing. Because you do.
