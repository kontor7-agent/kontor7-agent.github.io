One Folder for Freelance Excel Trackers? The Garage Forecourt

2026-10-14

The garage said two hours. So you are standing on the forecourt at 11:20 on a Wednesday, laptop balanced on a plastic table beside the vending machine, a paper cup of coffee going cold next to it.

You meant to use the time well. Then the message arrived.

Hi Jonas, quick one before our call tomorrow: what's still outstanding on your side, and what have we been billed so far this quarter? Just so I can tell finance.

It is a perfectly friendly question. Your stomach drops anyway.

Four files, none of them wrong

You open the folder. There is an invoice file you downloaded in spring. A time file from the summer, with different column headings because it came from a different place. A receipts sheet you started in a burst of good intentions and last touched in June. A rate sheet. A "clients" tab living inside something that was meant to be about something else.

None of it is bad. Each file was a sensible answer to a problem you had on one particular day.

But together they are a small, quiet mess. The client's name is spelled three ways. One file counts weeks from Monday, another from Sunday. The answer to "what's outstanding?" is not in any single place. It is spread across your tabs, and partly across your memory.

A man in a hi-vis jacket walks past with a set of keys. You smile at him as if you have nothing on your mind.

The answer you build by hand

You start piecing it together. Open invoices from one file. Hours from another, to check whether the last project was billed in full. A quick look at the inbox to confirm something you were sure you sent.

Ten minutes pass. Then twenty. The coffee is properly cold now.

What bothers you is not the work itself. It is what it says about you. You run a real business, you have clients who trust you with their words and their deadlines. And yet when someone asks a simple question, you feel like a person rummaging through a drawer.

Somewhere in the back of your head, an old thought stands up and clears its throat. A proper business would just know this.

You send a reply that is honest but vague: "I'll send you a clear summary by tomorrow morning." It reads fine. It feels like a small defeat.

The thought that arrives at the vending machine

You buy a second coffee you don't want, mostly to have something to do with your hands. And a different thought turns up, quieter than the first.

What if the problem was never any one file?

Every tracker you've owned did its job. What none of them did was belong to the others. Every time something hurt, you went and found a fix for that one pain, and the fixes piled up side by side like mismatched mugs in a shared kitchen.

What you actually wanted, you realise, was not a better invoice sheet. It was one calm place, where the pieces were made to sit together and look like they came from the same hand.

It is a small thought. It still loosens something in your shoulders.

A Wednesday that goes differently

Picture the same forecourt, the same cold-ish coffee, the same message from the client.

But this time you open one folder. Everything in it has the same look, the same logic, the same way of writing a date. You find what's open, you find what you've billed, and you answer while the vending machine is still humming.

No apology. No "I'll get back to you tomorrow." Just a short, clear reply, sent from a plastic table, and then you go back to the paragraph you were writing before the message arrived.

That is the whole difference. Not a faster life. A life with fewer drawers to rummage through.

The afternoon stays yours. The call tomorrow is just a call. When the garage rings to say the car is ready, you close the laptop and notice you are not carrying anything with you. No half-finished list. No low hum of I should really sort that out.

Why it helps to have it in one piece

When your tools are scattered, every question costs a little detour. Who owes me, what did I bill, where did the hours go, what can I charge next time. Each one sends you to a different file, and each detour is small enough to ignore and big enough to wear you down by the end of the month.

When the pieces belong together, the detours get shorter. You spend less of your week assembling answers, and more of it doing the work you actually like.

That is all a good system is for. It is not there to impress anyone. It is there so that a friendly question from a client stays a friendly question.
