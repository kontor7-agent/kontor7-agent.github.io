New Client Onboarding Chaos? The Reception Chair at 9:50

2026-10-15

It is 9:50 on a Tuesday, and you are sitting in a reception chair that is slightly too low.

The kickoff meeting starts in ten minutes. Your laptop is open on your knees. A plant that nobody waters stands next to you. On the screen are three email threads, a WhatsApp message with a password in it, and a half-finished note called kickoff??

You are a freelance web designer. The client is a physiotherapy practice with a new owner, a tight budget and a launch date they have already told their staff.

The thought in your head is simple: What exactly did I agree to?

The scope that lived in three places

You remember the first call. You remember saying "yes, of course, we can include that." You do not remember whether "that" meant the booking page or only a link to the booking page.

The quote says five pages. An email from two weeks ago says "plus a small blog section." A voice message says the owner's sister will "just send the texts."

None of it is dramatic. That is the problem. It is a pile of small, friendly, unwritten things. Each one is fine alone. Together they are a project that has already started drifting before you have shaken a hand.

You scroll faster. You are looking for the email with the domain login. It is not there. You are fairly sure someone promised it.

What it quietly costs

Nobody warns you that the start of a project is where the most money slips away.

It does not look like money. It looks like a Thursday spent waiting for a login. It looks like a gentle "quick question" that turns into a rebuild. It looks like the moment, three weeks in, when you think I should have written that down and then feel too awkward to bring it up.

It costs something else, too. When you start a project on guesswork, you start it a little apologetic. You ask for things in a small voice. You say "whenever you get a chance" about the one thing you cannot work without.

You are good at your craft. But in a lobby chair, with a scattered inbox, you do not feel like a studio. You feel like someone improvising.

The meeting you run on memory

The owner arrives, warm and a bit rushed. She shakes your hand. In the meeting room, she asks the very reasonable question every client asks.

"So, what do you need from us?"

You answer from memory. Photos, yes. Texts. Opening hours. Logo files. You will think of something else at 4 pm. You always do.

The call goes well, honestly. She likes you. But on the walk back to the station you feel the familiar low hum. Which of the things you said were promises? Who is sending what, and by when? Did anyone actually say who approves the final design?

You already know that tonight you will write a long follow-up email to cover it all. You will rewrite the first line twice, because you want it to sound friendly and not like a contract.

The turn

A few weeks later, a different project begins. This is the part that changes the story.

The night before, you do not scramble. You send one short welcome note. A simple form goes along with it, the kind that asks the client the questions you used to ask in pieces across a dozen messages. Where are your files? Who decides? Who has the logins?

By morning it has come back, mostly filled in. A few gaps remain, and the gaps are useful. They are the things you would have found out on day nine.

You realise something quietly: it was never that you were bad at starting projects. You were starting them from a blank page every single time.

It did not have to be that way. The first week of a project can be a repeatable thing, with the same calm shape every time, even when the client is new and the job is different.

A normal Monday, afterwards

Picture the same kickoff, a little later in the year.

You are in a meeting room, or on a video call. The agenda is one page, and everyone can see it. You walk through what is included. You say what is not. You say it kindly, because it is already written down, so it does not feel like you are pushing back. It feels like you are being clear.

At the end, the client reads a short summary of what was agreed. She nods. Maybe she adds one line. That one line, caught on day one, saves you a very awkward email on day thirty.

The access list is ticked off as things arrive. You can see at a glance who still owes you what, which is a much gentler way to chase than "just checking in again!"

And on the day the work is done, there is a handover that is tidy rather than a frantic folder of files sent at midnight. The client feels looked after. You feel like a professional. Those two things turn out to be the same feeling.

Nothing about this is flashy. It is simply the sound of a project starting quietly and correctly.

What you get back

Mostly, you get your first-week energy back. You stop spending it on remembering and start spending it on the actual work.

You get the small dignity of saying "here is how I work" without having to improvise it. And you get to walk out of a reception area at 11:30 with nothing nagging at the back of your mind.
