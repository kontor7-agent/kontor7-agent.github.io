Busy Salon, Not Sure It Pays? The Baby Shower Question

2026-10-31

Saturday, 2:15 pm. A garden, paper lanterns, a table of tiny sandwiches. Your cousin's baby shower.

You have been on your feet since seven. Four colours, two cuts and a bride's trial before you locked up and came here with foil still under one fingernail.

An uncle you see twice a year balances a plate on his knee and looks at you kindly.

"Mara, your sister says you're fully booked for weeks. Business must be booming!"

You laugh. You say, "Can't complain."

Inside, a small cold thought: I have no idea.

The answer you didn't have

You are busy. That part is true. The chair is full, the phone buzzes, and regulars book the next appointment before the cape is off.

But "busy" and "doing well" are not the same word, and you know it. You have simply never had a quiet hour to check.

So you do what you always do. You carry a loose feeling instead of a number. Some weeks feel good. Some weeks feel thin. Why, you couldn't say.

You know what goes into the chair rent on the first. You know what you owe the wholesaler when the card machine beeps. After that it gets blurry.

Tips sit in the jar. Cash goes in your apron pocket and then in a drawer. Card payments land in the bank in lumps that never match the days they came from. Somewhere in a notebook is a page that says Thurs: 3 colours?

The jar, the notebook, the gap

On the walk home from the shower you try to add up last week in your head.

Monday was quiet. Tuesday was full. Wednesday, Mrs. Brandt cancelled with a text at 8:50 for a 9:00 appointment, and you stood there with the bowl already mixed. Friday, someone simply didn't turn up. You told yourself it didn't matter, because you filled the afternoon.

But did it matter? What did that empty hour cost? What did the colour you mixed cost to make? Is a blow-dry, the thing you do ten times a week, actually earning anything, or just keeping the chair warm?

There's a quiet shame in that. You run a real business, and you can't say whether it works.

You don't say it out loud to anyone. It sounds ungrateful when the diary is full.

The Sunday you stayed in the kitchen

Sunday morning your sister drops by with leftover cake, sees the shoebox of receipts and the notebook open on the table, and doesn't say anything clever. She just sits down, takes the kettle, and asks one plain question.

"What would you want to know, if you could know anything?"

You think about it. It turns out the list is short.

Does the cash I take match what I earned? What do the no-shows really cost me in a month? Which services pay for themselves, and which ones am I doing out of habit and kindness?

Three questions. Not a hundred. And none of them need a degree. They need the numbers to live in one place, instead of in your apron, a jar and your memory.

That's the turning point, and it's small. You realise it doesn't have to stay a feeling. It could be something you look at, once a week, with a cup of tea, and then close.

An ordinary Monday, afterwards

Picture a different Monday. The salon is closed, the radiator ticking, the light coming flat through the window.

You sit down for ten minutes with a coffee. You put in the week: what came in as card, what came in as cash, who didn't show. It's not a project. It feels more like tidying a counter.

Then the page tells you something. Not a verdict, just a calm picture. The Tuesday that felt exhausting was good. The Friday gap cost you more than you assumed. One service you've been doing at the same price for two years turns out to leave very little behind for the hour it takes.

You don't panic. For the first time you can see it, and anything you can see you can decide about.

Maybe you move a time slot. Maybe you ask for a deposit on long appointments. Maybe you finally raise that one price by a small, quiet amount. None of it is dramatic. It's just yours, chosen with your eyes open.

The cash in the drawer stops being a mystery. The jar is just tips. When someone asks how business is, you can say "good, actually," and know why. Or "tighter than I'd like, but I know where," which is its own kind of strength.

And the next time an uncle with a plate of sandwiches says you must be booming, you smile, because you aren't guessing anymore.
