Clients Skip Appointments, Is Your Salon Making Money?

2026-11-05

The question at the bakery counter

Tuesday, 11:20. Ines flips the little sign on the studio door to Back in ten minutes and walks next door for a coffee she doesn't really want.

Her eleven o'clock lash client hasn't come. No message, no call. The chair is made up, the fresh tape is out, the room smells of the vanilla candle she lights before every first appointment. She waited twenty minutes before she let herself admit it.

At the bakery counter, Marta asks what she asks every week. "Busy?"

"Always," says Ines, and smiles the smile she keeps for this.

Then she stands there holding the coffee, thinking: Am I, though?

Her diary looks full. It has looked full for months. But a full diary and a full account are not the same thing, and lately she can't say which one she is living in.

What the diary doesn't show

Back in the studio, she opens the booking app. Tuesday is a row of coloured blocks. The eleven o'clock one is still bright, as if nothing happened. The app doesn't flinch. It simply shows what was planned.

What it doesn't show is what actually happened.

Somewhere there is the client who cancelled forty minutes before. The one who moved twice, then vanished. The friend of a friend who got the "mates' price" and came back for her fill with no mention of it. Some payments came in by card, some as cash tucked into her apron pocket and swallowed by the week, and some as a transfer she meant to note down and didn't.

She tells herself she'll sort it out on Sunday. But Sunday has become the day she sits with a pen, a bank statement and a feeling in her stomach, adds things up, gets a number, adds them again and gets a different one. Then she closes the notebook and decides it's probably fine.

Probably fine is exhausting to live in.

The cost nobody says out loud

The no-show itself is not the worst part. It stings for an hour, and then the next client arrives.

The worst part is the vagueness. You can't be firm about a cancellation policy when you don't know what cancellations cost you. You can't raise a price calmly when you're not sure whether the service you sell most is the one that pays. You can't tell your partner, or yourself, whether this was a good month, because the honest answer is "I think so?"

So you say "busy" at the bakery. You say "fine" at family dinner. And at the end of the month, when the rent for the room comes due, you hold your breath a little before you open the banking app.

It's a small, private kind of fear. It doesn't make you bad at your craft. It just makes you feel like someone who is winging a business that deserves better.

The turn

The client turns up at 11:50, flustered and apologetic. A bus, a sick child, a phone on silent. Ines is kind about it, because she is kind, and the appointment is shortened to fit what's left of the slot.

While she works, she thinks about something her neighbour, a barber, said weeks ago over the shared bins. He had stopped trying to feel whether the month went well. He just wrote things down in one place, a few minutes at a time, and let the page tell him.

She had nodded politely and forgotten it.

Now, with the client's eyes closed and the studio quiet, the thought comes back, plain and unglamorous: It doesn't have to be a feeling. It can just be a page.

Not an app with fifty settings. Not a course on bookkeeping. One place where the cash and the cards line up, where a missed appointment shows up as a missed appointment with a price on it, and where each service has to answer one simple question: do you actually earn?

A different Monday

Picture a Monday morning a few weeks on.

Ines unlocks the studio, switches on the lamp, puts the kettle on. Before the first client, she opens one sheet and enters the weekend: a few card payments, the cash from Saturday, one no-show. It takes minutes. The overview fills in on its own, and it looks like a calm page, not an accusation.

She sees that the no-shows cost her something real last month, a number she can look at without flinching. She sees that one service she has been quietly discounting is not the one carrying the room. She sees that the cash and the card add up to what she thought they did, or they don't, and either way she knows.

At the bakery, Marta asks, "Busy?"

"Steady," Ines says. "Good month, actually."

And for the first time in a long while, it is a sentence she can stand behind.
