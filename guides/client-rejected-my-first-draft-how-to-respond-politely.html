Client Rejected My First Draft? The Chimney Sweep Wait

2026-10-20

The chimney sweep is due between nine and noon, so Marta is home with the laptop on the kitchen table and a mug going cold beside it.

It is 10:12. She has been a freelance copywriter for three years. The first draft of a website rewrite went out yesterday afternoon, and she felt good about it. Tidy, warm, on brief.

Then the email arrives.

"Thanks for this. Honestly, it's not quite what we had in mind. Can we talk?"

That is all of it. No list of what is wrong, no hint of what "in mind" means. Just a small cold stone dropped into the morning.

Three drafts of one reply

She opens a reply and types: "Thanks for the feedback! Happy to revise."

She deletes it. Too eager. It sounds like she agrees the work was bad.

She types: "Could you be more specific about what's not working? The draft followed the brief."

She deletes that too. Too sharp. Too much like a door closing.

The third version is four paragraphs of explanation. Why she chose that tone, what the brief said, how long it took. She reads it back and hears someone defending herself in a courtroom. Delete.

The cursor blinks. The kettle ticks as it cools. Somewhere outside, a van door slams, and her heart jumps, because it might be the sweep and she is not ready for anyone.

What it really costs

It is only one email. But you know that is not how it feels.

It feels like a verdict. Like a quiet question about whether you are really good at this, or whether three years was just luck that is running out. Nobody sees you at the kitchen table, rereading one polite sentence for the ninth time. No colleague leans over and says, "Oh, that's normal. Just say this."

So the morning leaks away. She does not start the next job. She checks the email again. She wonders whether the client is already looking at other writers. She wonders whether she should offer a discount before they even ask. That last thought is the one that worries her most, because she has done it before. Apologised in advance and quietly given away an hour here, a revision there, to buy back a feeling of safety.

She once worked out, loosely, that the "just to be nice" extras were costing her more than any slow month. She has not looked at the number since.

The sweep and the sentence

The doorbell goes at 10:40. The chimney sweep is a cheerful man with a soot-dark cap and a clipboard. While he sets down his brushes in the hallway, he says, almost to himself, "First look is never the last look. You sweep, you see what's there, then you adjust."

He is talking about her chimney. He goes off to the living room.

Marta stands in the kitchen and hears it anyway.

A first draft is a first look. The client did not say it was wrong. They said it was not quite what they had in mind. Which means something is still in their mind that she has not seen yet. That is not a failure. It is the missing half of the brief, and it is her job to ask for it gently.

What she needs is not a longer apology or a sharper defence. It is a calm, short, friendly reply that does three things: thanks them, shows she is on their side, and asks for the one thing she needs to do better. Nothing more. Not a plea. Not a contract dispute.

She realises she has never actually had that sentence ready. She has always built it from scratch, on the spot, while her stomach was tight. Of course it came out as an apology or a wall.

The same Wednesday, a little later

Imagine the other version of this morning.

The email arrives. It still stings for a moment. But there is already a good starting point on her screen: friendly wording she has used before, a place to drop the client's name and the project, a version that is warmer and a version that is more businesslike. She picks the one that sounds like her, changes two words, and reads it once.

"Thanks for the honest feedback. I'd like to get this right. Could you point me to two or three lines that feel off, or a page whose tone you like? Then I'll send a revised version by Friday."

Send. Four minutes, not ninety.

The chimney sweep rinses his brushes. Marta makes a fresh coffee, actually drinks it hot, and opens the next job. The knot has not vanished, but it has loosened. The client is not a judge. They are a person who needs a bit of help saying what they want.

In the afternoon, a reply comes back with the details she needed. The second draft is better than the first would ever have been.

For the next "not quite what we had in mind"

Marta is an illustrative character, but the morning is a common one. Rejected drafts, vague feedback, a price that needs lifting, a polite no, a reminder for an invoice that is two weeks late. The words are rarely the hard part. The hard part is finding them while your pulse is up and nobody is watching.
