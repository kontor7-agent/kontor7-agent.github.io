Payment 'On the Way' for a Month? The Sewing Repair Shop

2026-10-29

The repair shop smells of machine oil and warm dust. It's Tuesday, 10:35, and the woman behind the counter has disappeared into the back with your mother's old sewing machine. You said you'd wait. You have nothing to do but look at your phone.

So you open the email thread you've been avoiding.

Jonas, the copywriter in this story, is not a real person. But you may know him. Four weeks ago his client wrote: "Thanks! Payment is on the way." Nine words, friendly, with an exclamation mark. He felt relief for about an hour.

Now he's standing between a rack of bobbins and a shelf of tired pedal machines, scrolling past that line again. Four weeks. Nothing has arrived. He has checked the banking app so many times that his thumb opens it on its own.

The sentence you can't argue with

The trouble with "on the way" is that it can't be wrong. It isn't a lie, and it isn't a date. It's a small cloud floating over your month.

You can't say "you said it was coming" without sounding like you're keeping score. You can't say "is everything okay?" because you know everything is fine for them. You're the one doing the quiet maths: rent on the first, the pension payment, the groceries you put on the card last week and tried not to think about.

Jonas starts a reply on his phone. Hi Katrin, just checking in on… He deletes it. Hope you're well! Wanted to follow up… Deleted. Sorry to bother you again…

He stops at "sorry". He hasn't done anything wrong. He did the work, sent the invoice and waited politely. Somewhere along the way, asking to be paid started to feel like asking for a favour.

What the waiting actually costs

It isn't only the money, though the money is real.

It's the way the unpaid invoice follows you around. It sits at the back of your mind during a good call with another client. It's there in the supermarket queue when you do the arithmetic before you tap your card. It's there on Sunday afternoon, when you could be walking and are instead drafting emails in your head.

It costs you the thing you went freelance for, which is feeling like the person in charge of your own working life.

And there's a smaller cost nobody mentions. You start to wonder whether you're being unreasonable. Whether a "real" business would just let this go. Whether other people simply don't have this problem.

They do. Almost everyone who works for themselves has stood in a doorway somewhere with this exact email half-written.

The counter, the bobbins, the small clarity

The repair woman comes back, wiping her hands on a cloth. "The belt was perished," she says. "Easy fix. I'll ring you when it's ready. Thursday."

Thursday. Not "soon". Not "on the way". A day.

Jonas almost laughs. He pays the small deposit, thanks her, and walks out into the thin autumn light. It's the first time in a month that a promise has had an edge to it, something he can plan around, lean on or hold her to without it being rude.

Sitting on the bench outside, it occurs to him that the shop isn't doing anything special. It just names a day. Nobody feels awkward about it, because a day is how adults say what they mean.

The awkwardness in his own email was never about the money. It was about having no clear next step. He didn't know whether to wait another week, write again, or call. He didn't know how firm was too firm. So he did nothing, and the doing nothing was the heaviest part.

A different Tuesday

Imagine the same morning, but with the question already answered.

You open one calm page. It shows you the invoice, the date it was due, how many days have passed, and what the next step is. Not a feeling, a step. A friendly note first, then a firmer one, then something clearer if it comes to that. The words are already there, kind and professional, and you only change the name.

You read the next message once. It sounds like you on a good day: warm, steady, unbothered. It names the invoice and names a date. It doesn't apologise for existing.

You press send, put the phone in your pocket, and go and look at the sewing machines.

The knot in your stomach doesn't vanish. But it loosens, because the matter is no longer floating. It's on a timeline, and someone, you, has it in hand.

Thursday

In this story, Jonas sends a short, courteous message that afternoon. It asks for a payment date, no more. Katrin answers within the hour with an apology and a day. It isn't the drama he'd been bracing for. It's just a person who needed a clear question.

On Thursday he picks up the machine. The belt runs smooth and quiet. He carries it to the car, and for the first time in weeks he isn't checking the banking app.

That's the whole change. The client didn't turn into someone else. He stopped waiting for permission to ask.
