Quoted a Job, Forgot Your Own Wages? The Gala Bleachers

2026-10-24

The pool smells of chlorine and wet towels. It is Saturday, 10:20, and you are on the third row of the bleachers with a lukewarm coffee. Your daughter is somewhere behind a door, pulling on a swim cap.

You are not watching the pool. You are looking at your phone.

It's the quote you sent on Tuesday for the Hendersons' garden fence and gate. They said yes on Thursday. You bought the timber on Friday. Now, on a bleacher bench, you open the message again just to feel good about it.

Posts. Panels. Concrete. Hinges. Screws. Delivery. A little for the skip. Every line is there, and every line is honest.

Then you read it a second time, slowly.

There is no line for you.

The sum that doesn't have you in it

You do the arithmetic in your head while a whistle blows somewhere below. The total you quoted is the materials, plus a little "for the work". You can see now that the little was a guess. You typed it on site, standing in their garden, with the homeowner watching your face. You wanted to sound fair. You wanted to get the job.

So you try the sum honestly. Four days, if the weather holds. Five if the ground is stony, and in that corner of the garden it will be. Take away the timber, the fuel and the hire of the post driver. Divide what's left by the days.

The number is not a wage. It's what you'd pay a teenager to hold the other end of a plank.

Your stomach does a slow, quiet drop. Nobody is shouting. Nobody has done anything wrong. You just agreed, in writing, to work for nothing, and the timber is already stacked in your van.

What it costs besides money

The money is only the first thing.

The second thing is the Monday you'll have. You'll turn up at 7:30 and be friendly, because you are friendly. You'll dig and measure and smile. And somewhere around the third post hole, a small voice will start up. You're good at this. Why are you always the one who ends up underpaid?

The third thing is the conversation you won't have. You could message the Hendersons now and say the price was wrong. But that feels like begging in a different outfit. You said a number. They trusted it. Going back feels like admitting you aren't quite a proper business.

So you do what most people do. You put the phone face down on your knee and tell yourself you'll make it up on the next job. And the next job gets quoted the same way, on a doorstep, from your head, with everyone being nice.

Your daughter's race is called. You cheer a half-second late.

The thing you notice in the car park

Afterwards, in the car park, she is wrapped in a towel the size of a tent, eating a banana, telling you about her turn. You nod in the right places. You are still half back on the bleachers.

"Dad, you're doing the face," she says.

"What face?"

"The sums face."

You laugh, because it's true. And in the laugh something loosens. You realise the problem was never that you can't do the work. You can build a fence that outlasts the people who ordered it. The problem is that every quote starts from nothing, from your gut, in someone else's garden, and your own time is the one thing that never gets its own line.

Nobody ever told you that was fixable. You assumed that's just what working for yourself felt like.

It isn't. It's what working for yourself feels like without a place to put the numbers.

A different Monday

Picture the next one.

You're standing in a customer's yard with a tape measure. They ask, "So, roughly what are we looking at?" And you don't have to say "I'll get back to you" through a nervous smile. You know that when you go back to the van, the job will have its shape: materials, days, your wages, a margin that means you're still standing at the end. You can hand them a price that you actually believe in.

A week later the receipts for the timber and the hinges are somewhere you can find them. They aren't crumpled in the door pocket. At the end of the job you can see what the fence really earned, not what you hoped. And when someone is slow to pay, you know exactly who, and how much, without lying awake doing it in your head.

None of that is dramatic. It's quiet. It's a Friday where you close the laptop at six, knowing the work paid you, and go and stand in the kitchen while something cooks.

About the Hendersons

You'll probably still build the fence. You gave your word, and your word matters. Maybe you'll find a way to have an honest, calm conversation about the gate. Maybe you'll absorb this one and call it a very expensive lesson. Either choice is yours, and neither makes you less professional.
