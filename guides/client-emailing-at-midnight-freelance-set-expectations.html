Client Emails at Midnight? The Canal Towpath at 7:05

2026-10-18

You are walking the towpath with a coffee going cold in your hand. It is 7:05 on a Tuesday. The water is flat and a heron is standing very still on the far bank.

You are not looking at the heron. You are looking at your phone.

There are three emails from the same client. The first came in at 23:52. The second at 00:14. The third at 00:31, with the subject line "Quick one!!"

None of them is a disaster. That is the strange part. One asks if the logo could be "a bit warmer." One has a forwarded thread you have to decode. The last says, "Let me know your thoughts when you get a sec."

But you read all three before you had brushed your teeth. Now your shoulders are up around your ears, and the day has not even started.

The reply you almost sent at 00:40

Let's call you Jonas, a freelance designer, three years in. Not a real person, just someone you might recognise.

Last night you saw the first notification while you were putting the dishes away. You told yourself you would only look. Then you were answering. Then you were answering the second one too, because the first answer seemed to start a conversation.

You sent "Sure, I'll take a look first thing!" at 00:40, which is a sentence you did not mean as a promise.

The client read it as one.

What it quietly costs

Nobody warns you about this part. The midnight emails are not really the problem. The problem is what you learn from them.

You learn that you are expected to be awake. You learn that silence is a risk. You start checking your inbox at the supermarket checkout, in the cinema queue, and in the five minutes before sleep, just in case.

Your evenings get thinner. Your Sundays get a low hum under them.

And you resent a client you actually like. That is the part that feels worst. They are not a villain. They are a person with a busy head who emails when the ideas arrive. Nobody ever told them that this is not how you work, because nobody ever told them how you work at all.

You never said it. You started the project the way many of us do: a friendly call, a "sounds great," a first invoice, and an unspoken hope that things would sort themselves out.

They did not. They sorted themselves out in the client's favour, one midnight at a time.

The call that turned it

At 10:30 that Tuesday you have a call with this very client. You rehearse a speech on the towpath and delete it in your head three times. It sounds either stiff or apologetic.

Then, halfway through the call, something small happens. They mention they are also working with a copywriter on the same project. You ask how the copywriter handles things. The client laughs and says, "Oh, she sent me this one-pager at the start. When she answers, when she doesn't, who gets what and when. It's honestly the reason I never email her at night. I know she'll see it in the morning."

You sit there with your pen in your hand.

The client did not need to be corrected. The client needed to be told. And the copywriter had simply told them, calmly and early, in writing, before there was anything to be annoyed about.

No confrontation. No awkward "could you please not." Just a normal part of how the work begins.

What you decide that afternoon

You do not rewrite your whole business. You do something much smaller.

You decide that the next time someone says yes to working with you, there will be a first week. A proper one. A welcome that explains how you work: when you reply, how feedback comes in, what counts as urgent, what the first call is for. Not as rules. As a friendly handshake in text.

You realise you have been trying to set boundaries in the middle of a project, when everyone is tired and attached to their own version of events. It was never going to feel good there. It would have been so much easier on day one, when everyone is still smiling.

A different Tuesday

Picture a few months on. Same towpath, same heron, probably.

Your phone is in your bag. Not because you are being strict. Because you know what is waiting there, and that it will keep. A client's late idea sits in your inbox like a note left on a desk. You will read it at nine, with a proper coffee, and answer it like someone who has slept.

The new client on Thursday already knows your reply window. They know where to put their feedback. They know what you need from them before you can begin. When they send something at 23:40, it comes with a line like "no rush, whenever you're in tomorrow," because they were told it was fine to say so.

You are not colder. If anything you are warmer, because you are not secretly annoyed.

And when someone is a bit slower to adapt, it is a short, kind conversation, because the first page already exists. You are only pointing back at it.

The heron lifts off. You do not check your phone.

If this sounded like your towpath

If you have been answering at midnight because nobody ever said you didn't have to, you are not bad at boundaries. You just started without a beginning.
