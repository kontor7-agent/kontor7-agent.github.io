Negative Google Review? The Shutter Half Up on Monday

2026-11-01

It is 7:45 on Monday morning. The shutter of the bike workshop is half up, the kettle is not even on, and your phone buzzes once on the workbench.

New review for your business.

One star. Four lines. Your name isn't in it, but everything else is: the wait, the "attitude", the words "would not recommend".

You read it standing up. Then you read it again. Your face is warm, and you start answering in your head. I told them it would take a week. I wrote it on the ticket. I stayed late on Thursday for them.

The coffee machine is still cold. Your morning is already gone.

The reply you write in your head

This is the part nobody warns you about when you start working for yourself. A bad review isn't just a bad review. It feels like a verdict on you, with your name on the door, your Saturdays in the work, and your savings behind it.

So you draft a reply in the notes app, and it comes out hot.

Dear customer, as stated on the ticket...

You delete "as stated". It sounds like a lawyer. You write something softer, and it sounds like begging. You write something in between, and it sounds like a robot with a grudge.

You know the reply isn't really for the person who wrote the review. They have probably moved on. It is for the stranger who will read it next month, standing in the street, deciding whether to book you. That makes it feel like the most important paragraph you will write all week.

Which is exactly why you can't write it now.

The hour that disappears

The first customer arrives at nine to collect a wheel. You smile, you hand it over, you take the card payment. All the while, the review sits at the back of your head like a stone in your shoe.

At ten you try the reply again. At eleven, between two phone calls, you try once more. Each version is a little more polite and a little less like you.

By lunchtime you have spent nearly an hour on four sentences. You haven't answered the supplier, you haven't looked at this week's bookings, and you haven't eaten. You still haven't posted anything, because the review is the first thing anyone will see.

And under the irritation is the quieter worry: am I actually bad at this?

You are not. But a one-star review at 7:45 has a way of making you doubt it.

The small turn

Here is what you realise on the walk to get a sandwich.

You don't struggle with the words because you're a bad writer. You struggle because you're writing alone, while your pulse is up, with no starting point. Every blank box feels like a test.

A calm reply doesn't need to be clever. It needs to say a few plain things in a warm voice: thank you for telling us, here is what we understand happened, here is what we'd like to do, here is how to reach us. That's all. Whoever reads it later isn't looking for perfect prose. They're looking for a person who stays steady when someone is unhappy.

That is something you can do. You just can't do it from a blank page, with your heart in your throat.

A different Monday

Picture the same morning, but with a few drafts already waiting for you.

The review comes in at 7:45. It still stings, and it probably always will a little. But you open a ready-made starting point, fill in the brackets with the real details (the date, what was agreed, what you can offer), and read it through once.

Then you change one line so it sounds like you, and that's it.

You don't argue and you don't grovel. You thank them, you say what you can do, and you leave the door open. You post it before the first customer walks in, and you actually drink your coffee while it is still hot.

The rest of the morning belongs to the work you came in for. The wheel, the brakes, the phone call you were going to return. By evening the review is a small thing again, one line in a long list of good days.

The review didn't change. The only difference is that you didn't have to face it with nothing in your hands.

The quiet things a ready draft takes off your plate

When the wording is already half there, you stop rehearsing arguments on the way home. You stop checking whether the customer has answered. You stop dreading the next notification.

You also stop treating every unhappy customer as an emergency. Some reviews are fair, and some are not. Either way, you have a calm place to start from, and what you write afterwards is still your decision.

That is a small kind of freedom, but it is the kind that gets you through a busy week.
