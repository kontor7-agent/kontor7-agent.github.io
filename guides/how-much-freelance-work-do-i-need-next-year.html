No Idea How Much Freelance Work You Need? The Sat-Nav

2026-11-06

Saturday, 9:20 in the morning. You are in the passenger seat of a hired van, and the back is full of your friend's old sofa.

The sat-nav on the dashboard says: Arrival 10:07. It says it calmly, the way it always does. Two turns, one roundabout, done.

Your friend Jonas has the wheel. He is a joiner, so he is good at plans. He glances at the screen, then at you.

"So what's the goal for next year? Work-wise."

You say, "More. Obviously. Just a bit more than this year."

He nods. You look out of the window at the grey ring road. You know that answer is not an answer.

A destination with no arrival time

This is what you don't say out loud.

You do have a number in your head. It showed up in October, somewhere between a client call and the supermarket queue. A figure you'd like to earn. A figure that would let you stop flinching at the electricity bill and book an actual holiday without doing sums on a napkin.

But you have no idea what it takes to get there.

How many projects is that? How many months of full diary? Do you need two big clients or ten small ones? And how many weeks are you really going to work, once you take out the days you're ill, the week at your mother's, the Christmas dead zone, and the afternoons that vanish into invoices?

The sat-nav knows its distance. It knows its speed. You know your destination, and nothing else.

So each January you do the same thing. You say yes to nearly everything, because every no feels like a hole in the number you can't see. You work hard. In December you wonder why it feels like you ran all year and still can't tell whether you arrived.

The roundabout

Jonas takes the roundabout. The sofa shifts in the back with a soft thud.

"I used to do that," he says. "Quote everything, take everything. Then one spring I had four jobs overlapping and a wedding to go to, and I realised I'd never once counted how many jobs a year is actually possible. I just kept going until something snapped."

He doesn't say this to teach you anything. He says it the way people talk in vans, eyes on the road.

You realise something small and slightly embarrassing. You have never planned your year the way you'd plan this drive. Start, route, estimated arrival, and a sensible guess about the traffic.

You plan every client project. You'd never take on a brief without knowing the deadline. But your own year is a drive with no screen on the dashboard.

What a calmer Monday looks like

Now imagine the same Saturday, a few weeks on.

It is the first Monday of the year. Not a panicked one. You make a coffee, and it is still hot when you sit down.

On the screen is one page that shows your year the way the sat-nav shows a route. Your income goal on one side. The weeks you actually have on the other, with the holidays already carved out, not hoped for. A rough idea of how many projects fit in the space between. A few conversations you're already having with clients, sitting honestly in a list instead of floating around as maybes.

It doesn't make the year certain. Nothing can. But the number stops being a fog. You can look at it and say, quietly, this is roughly what it takes.

And something else changes. When a client emails on a Tuesday with a small, poorly paid job, you don't feel the old panic. You can see whether the year has room for it. Sometimes the answer is yes. Sometimes it is a kind, clear no, and your shoulders don't tighten when you write it.

In March, you glance at the page again, see where you are against where you hoped to be, and adjust a little. Nothing dramatic. Just a small correction, like the sat-nav rerouting around a closed road. No one has to know. It's just your business, quietly being run by someone who can see it.

And the week off in August is already in there. Not as a guilty gap. As part of the plan.

Arrival 10:07

The van pulls up outside a narrow terraced house. Jonas puts on the handbrake. The sat-nav says, in the same even voice: You have arrived.

You get out. You and Jonas lift the sofa, shuffle it through the door and negotiate a corner with a lot of swearing and laughter. It fits, just barely.

Afterwards, with tea in someone else's mugs, you hear yourself say:

"I think I want to actually work out my year. Properly. Before January grabs me."

"Good," Jonas says. "Then you'll know when to say no."

That's the whole thing, really. Not a bigger ambition. Just a screen on the dashboard.
