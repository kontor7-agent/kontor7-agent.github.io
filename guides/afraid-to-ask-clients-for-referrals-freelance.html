Afraid to Ask Clients for Referrals? The Last Minute

2026-11-06

The minute before the call ends

It is 11:52 on a Thursday. The wrap-up call for the website project is almost over.

Your client, Jonas, is smiling at the camera. He has just said the launch page is "exactly what we wanted." Behind him you can see a shelf of mugs and a half-dead plant. You can hear a colleague laughing somewhere in the office.

This is the moment. You know it is.

The thought arrives fully formed: Ask him if he knows anyone else who needs this.

Your mouth is already saying something different.

"Great. Well, thanks so much. Let me know if anything comes up."

"Will do!" says Jonas. "Take care."

The screen goes back to your own face and a grey rectangle. You close the laptop lid a little harder than you meant to.

The sentence you rehearse in the shower

You are not shy. You negotiate. You explain things to people for a living.

But this one sentence sticks in your throat. Do you know anyone else I could help? It sounds fine in your head. Out loud, it sounds like asking for a favour you haven't earned yet.

So the doubts line up, the way they always do:

He is busy. He has already paid. It will sound needy. What if he says no? What if he says yes and sends me someone awful? What if it makes him think I'm short of work?

That last one is the real one, isn't it?

Because you are a little short of work. The calendar for next month has a gap you keep not looking at. And the thing that would help most is sitting right there in Jonas's contacts, waiting for a sentence you can't say.

What the silence costs

Nothing dramatic happens. That is why it is so easy to keep doing.

You finish a good project. You send the final invoice. You get a warm thank-you. And then the door closes softly, and you go back to refreshing your inbox and hoping someone finds you.

Meanwhile, the people who know your work best are the ones who never get asked. Not because they would mind. Most people like being asked for a recommendation, because it means you thought they had good taste. But you decided for them, in advance, that it would be a burden.

So your work depends on strangers and luck. And the quiet worry hums under the week: what if the next one doesn't come?

The supermarket queue

On Saturday you are standing in the supermarket queue, holding oat milk and a lemon. The woman ahead of you is telling the cashier about her nephew, who just started as an electrician.

"I told my whole street," she says. "He's brilliant. Of course I did."

Of course she did.

It lands strangely. She did not ask permission. She did not worry about sounding needy. People talk about the good things they have found. They only need a small, easy opening, and someone to make it feel normal.

You realise the problem was never Jonas. It was the blank space at the end of the call, where you had no words ready, so you grabbed the safest ones.

A different Thursday

Picture the same call, a few weeks from now.

The project is wrapping up. Jonas says it looks great. You feel the old flutter, but this time you are not improvising. You have already decided how it goes: a warm, short ask, in your own voice, sent that afternoon in a follow-up email where he has time to think and nobody is watching his face.

Three sentences. A thank-you, a clear picture of who you work best with, and an easy way out if he can't think of anyone.

No pressure. No speech. You press send and close the laptop at six, and nothing feels like begging.

Some of those emails get a reply. Some get a friendly "nobody comes to mind right now." Both are fine. The ask has stopped being a cliff and become a small habit, like saying thank you.

Before the next call ends

You don't have to become a different person. You just have to stop meeting that last minute empty-handed.
