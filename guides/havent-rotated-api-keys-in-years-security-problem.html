Haven't Rotated API Keys in Years? The Question on Mute

2026-11-07

It is 11:20 on a Tuesday, and you are halfway through a call with a new client.

The call has gone well. You shared your screen, walked through the plan, and nodded in the right places. Then their IT lead, a friendly man named Jonas, unmutes himself and says it lightly, almost in passing.

"By the way, how often do you rotate your API keys?"

You smile. Your face does the professional thing. Behind it, your mind goes to the .env file on your laptop and the key you generated for that first project. You think it was three years ago. Maybe four. It has been working so well that you stopped seeing it.

"We have a process for that," you hear yourself say.

The call moves on. Your stomach doesn't.

The shrug you've been carrying

Here is the part nobody says out loud. Most people who work alone have a drawer like this. Not a physical one, but a pile of small things that work, so you leave them alone. A key here. A token there. A password that a former contractor once knew.

You are not careless. You are busy. You build things, send invoices, answer emails, and fix the thing a client broke on a Friday. Nothing has ever gone wrong, and "nothing has ever gone wrong" is a comfortable place to live.

But it is also a quiet place to worry. You wonder whether this is a real problem or just an old habit that looks bad. You wonder whether everyone else has a tidy routine you somehow missed. You wonder whether you are a real professional or someone who has been getting away with it.

The honest answer is usually somewhere in the middle. Old keys are not a catastrophe. They are not nothing either. They are one of those questions that deserves a calm answer instead of a shrug.

The cost of not having that answer is rarely dramatic. It is smaller and more constant. It is the little flinch when a client says "security." It is the way you steer a conversation away from the topic. It is the Sunday afternoon when the thought drifts in and you push it back out.

What you do after the call

You close the laptop lid halfway. You make tea. You stand at the window, where the neighbour is hanging washing on a line, and you try to think clearly.

The trouble is that you don't know where to begin. Which keys count? Which routes matter? Is the old staging server still up? Did you ever check what the API returns when someone sends it something strange? Every question opens three more.

So you do what most of us do. You tell yourself you will look at it properly next week, when things are quieter. Things are never quieter. That is how a key ends up four years old.

The turning point is smaller than you think

What changes it is not a heroic weekend or a sudden transformation into a security expert. It is the moment you realise the worry is mostly about not knowing. It is not about one big flaw. It is about the missing list.

A key rotated or not rotated is one line. A route that checks who is asking is another line. What the API says when something goes wrong is another. None of them is frightening on its own. They are frightening together, as a fog.

When the fog becomes a list, with a place to tick, a place to write "not applicable," and a place to note "fix this on Thursday," something in your shoulders drops. You are no longer guarding a secret. You are keeping a record.

That is all a simple system does here. It turns "I hope it's fine" into "I looked, and here is what I found."

A Tuesday, later

Imagine the same call a few weeks on.

Jonas unmutes himself and asks, in the same light voice, how you handle key rotation and access checks.

You don't smile your professional smile. You just answer. "I review the API before each launch. I keep a written checklist, and I can send you the latest one."

There is a short pause, the good kind. Then he says, "Great, that's all we needed."

The call ends at 11:50. You make tea because you want tea, not because your stomach needs settling. The neighbour's washing is on the line again, and you notice how nice the light is.

Nothing about your work has changed except one thing: you know where you stand. That is what lets you take on the client with the bigger budget, answer the questionnaire without dread, and say "yes, it's covered" and mean it.

If you recognised yourself

If you read this with a small knot in your stomach, there is nothing wrong with you. You just have a list that hasn't been written yet.
