So Many Spreadsheets, Which One Is Right? The Photographer's Wednesday

2026-10-13

Wednesday, 10:15. Marta has a coffee going cold next to her laptop and a client on hold in her head.

The client has just asked a simple question by email. Did the second payment for the wedding album arrive?

Marta is a photographer. She is good at light, at faces, at the moment just before someone laughs. She is not good at this. And she knows the answer is somewhere.

She opens the folder called Business. Inside: Invoices_2025.xlsx. Invoices_NEW.xlsx. Money tracker (copy).xlsx. Hours – use this one.xlsx. Hours – real.xlsx. And one called Budget idea, which she made in March with great energy and has not opened since.

The thought arrives, quiet and heavy: Which one is the right one?

The tab you were sure you'd updated

She tries Invoices_NEW. The wedding is not there. She tries the copy. The wedding is there, but the payment column is empty, and she cannot tell whether that means unpaid or I never filled it in.

This is the part nobody talks about. It isn't that you lack spreadsheets. You have plenty. Every one was a small act of hope: this time I'll keep on top of it. Each one worked for a few weeks, then life got busy, and a new file seemed easier than fixing the old one.

Now you have a little archive of good intentions. And a client waiting for a simple yes or no.

Marta types a reply, deletes it, and types another. Let me double-check and get back to you this afternoon.

It is a perfectly professional sentence. It also feels like a small lie, because she isn't sure she can check.

What it quietly costs

Not a catastrophe. That's why it's so easy to live with.

It costs a Wednesday morning that was meant for editing. It costs the low hum of doubt whenever a client writes. It costs the small flinch before you open any file, because you don't know what it will tell you, or whether it will tell you anything.

And it costs a certain feeling about yourself. The one that whispers, a real business would know this.

But a real business is often just one person with a laptop and too many tabs. The difference isn't talent. It's whether the numbers live in one calm place or in eleven hopeful ones.

The question underneath the question

Around noon, Marta stops searching. She leans back and asks something different.

Not which spreadsheet is right? but why do I need so many?

She looks at the list. One file for who owes her money. One for what she charges. One for where her hours go. One for receipts. One for what she wants to post this week. Different worries, each pulled into its own corner, built at different moments, never meant to talk to each other.

None of them is wrong. They were just never designed as a set.

That's the turning point, and it's small. It doesn't arrive with fireworks. It arrives as a slow exhale: I don't need a cleverer file. I need fewer, simpler ones that each do one job and look like they belong together.

A different kind of Wednesday

Imagine the same morning, a few weeks on.

The client's email comes in at 10:15. Marta opens one sheet, the one she always opens. She finds the name. She sees the second payment: received, the 4th. It takes less time than lifting her cup.

She writes back: Yes, it arrived on the 4th. Thank you! Your album is on schedule.

Then she goes back to editing. The coffee is still warm.

Nothing about her business has changed. Same clients, same cameras, same prices. What has changed is the knot. The little tightening that used to come with every payment question, every quote, every Sunday evening when she meant to look at the numbers and didn't.

In this version of the week, she spends about ten minutes on a Friday glancing over who owes what, what she billed, where her hours went. Then she closes the laptop. The weekend belongs to the weekend.
