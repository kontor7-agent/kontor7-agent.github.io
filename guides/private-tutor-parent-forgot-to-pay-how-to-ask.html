Parent Forgot to Pay for Lessons Again? The Library Van

2026-10-25

The mobile library parks outside the community centre every Tuesday at 11:30. You are third in the queue, a returned novel under your arm, and your phone is in your other hand.

You are not looking at the books. You are looking at a message you have not sent.

Hi, just a quick reminder that the last four lessons are still

You delete "just". You delete "quick". You add a smiley, remove it, add it back. The queue moves one step. The librarian laughs at something somebody said.

This is the third time with the same parent. Nobody is being cruel, and that is what makes it harder. They are warm. They say "so sorry, it's been a crazy month!" and then it takes another two weeks.

Let's call you Maren. You tutor adults and teenagers in English and maths, mostly in the afternoons. You have never once thought of yourself as someone who chases people for money.

The question underneath the message

Here is what is actually going through your head in that queue.

It is not "how do I word this?" It is "am I even sure how many lessons they owe?"

Because you are not sure. You think it was four. There was one that moved to a Friday, and one that was cancelled the same morning. You mentioned that you wouldn't charge for it, or maybe you said you would. The details are somewhere in your calendar, your bank app and a notes file called tutoring stuff NEW.

So you cannot send the message with a steady voice. If you write "four lessons" and it was three, you will look careless. If you write "three" and it was four, you are quietly giving away an hour of your afternoon again.

That is the hidden cost of asking. It is not the awkwardness of the words. It is the feeling that you cannot back them up.

What it costs when nobody says it out loud

You have noticed some small things lately.

You dread the moment a lesson ends and the parent is standing at the door. You find yourself talking faster, as if speed would stop the subject coming up.

You have started to feel a flicker of resentment before certain lessons. Not toward the student, who is lovely. Toward the situation. And then guilt for feeling it.

You have told yourself that a slow payer is "basically fine, they always get there." It is true. They do get there. But "eventually" is not a plan when your own rent is due on the first.

And somewhere in the back of your mind sits a question you have not asked yourself properly: what do the late cancellations actually cost me each month? You suspect the number would sting. So you look away from it.

The librarian's slip

When you reach the front, the librarian scans your book, then pulls out a small paper slip and writes a date on it. "Due back the fifteenth," she says, as if it were nothing.

You take the slip and look at it for a moment longer than makes sense.

It is just a date. But it is clear and neutral, and nobody is embarrassed by it. The librarian does not apologise for the date. You do not feel judged for it. It simply says what was borrowed and when it comes back.

In the van, between the shelves of crime paperbacks, the thought arrives quite gently: what if my lessons had that?

Not a stern invoice. Not a speech. Just a plain, visible record of what was taught, what was paid and what is still open. Something so ordinary that asking becomes a sentence like "here's where we are" instead of "sorry to bother you."

A different Tuesday

Imagine the same week with that in place.

Lessons are logged as you finish them. It takes seconds, standing in your own hallway with the kettle on. Payments are ticked off when they arrive. Once a week, ten minutes, you look at one overview and see it: two families fully paid, one a little behind, one lesson that was cancelled late and what it cost you.

When you write to the parent who is behind, you do not hunt for the facts. They are already there. You write three calm lines. The number is right. The dates are right. You press send and close the laptop.

Nothing dramatic happens. That is the point. The parent replies, a little embarrassed, and pays on Thursday. The knot you had been carrying since the weekend simply is not there.

And on a quiet evening, you glance at the overview again and notice that the hours you actually teach are fewer than you thought, yet they earn more than you thought. You realise you could raise your rate in September without it feeling like a leap in the dark. You can see the reasons.

What changes is how you stand

Most of this was never about the parents. It was about standing on solid ground when you speak.

When you know exactly what is owed, asking is no longer begging. It is just being a professional who keeps tidy books. You are allowed to be that. You already are that, in every lesson you prepare.
