Forgot to Invoice Hours Last Month? The Donation Chair

2026-10-22

The nurse tells you to squeeze the foam ball every few seconds and then leaves you alone with the ceiling.

It's Tuesday, 10:20 in the morning. You're lying in a blood donation chair in a community hall that smells of floor polish and weak coffee. Your left arm is busy. Your right hand has nothing to do, so it picks up your phone.

You open the calendar, just to look at something. You scroll back to last month.

And there it is, in small coloured blocks: the workshop days for the Hartmann account. Three long ones, then two half days. You remember them clearly. The train, the flip charts, the client who kept saying "quick question" at 5 pm.

You try to remember the invoice for that.

You can't.

The squeeze, the pause, the squeeze

You squeeze the foam ball. Your mind starts running through it in a very orderly, very quiet way, like someone checking the doors of a house.

Did you send it on the 30th? You sent something on the 30th. Was that Hartmann, or the other one? You remember the invoice number being a bit odd. You remember thinking, "I'll double-check the hours on Monday."

Then Monday became a call. Then the call became a week.

No one in the hall can see any of this. The woman in the next chair is flipping through a magazine. A man by the biscuit table is telling a volunteer about his knee. Meanwhile you're lying very still, doing sums in your head, and the sums keep coming out the same.

Somewhere between two and four days of work might not have been billed.

Not a catastrophe. Just a small, cold drop in the stomach. The kind you used to feel before exams.

What it costs, quietly

The money is the obvious part. You know roughly what those days are worth. You know what it would mean to the month if they're simply gone.

But it's not only the money.

It's the thought that comes right after: a real business wouldn't lose track of this. It's the old suspicion that everyone else has a system and you have a good memory and some luck.

It's the awkwardness already forming. If you did forget, you'll have to write to the client. "Hi, small thing, I think I missed some hours." You can already feel yourself softening the sentence, apologising in advance for being paid.

And it's the evenings. You know how the next few go. You'll open the sent folder, the calendar, three half-finished spreadsheets and a notes app, and try to rebuild a month out of crumbs. You'll find a time here, a hunch there. At the end you'll still not be quite sure.

The nurse comes back, peeks at the bag filling up, and smiles. "You're doing great."

You smile back. You are not, in fact, doing great. You're doing a very good impression of someone lying calmly.

The juice and the quiet turn

Afterwards they sit you down at a small plastic table with a paper cup of orange juice and two biscuits. Fifteen minutes, they say. No rushing off.

So you can't rush off. For once, you can't do anything about it at all.

And that's when something shifts. Not a plan, just a thought that arrives without being asked:

I shouldn't have to remember this.

You've been treating it as a personal failing. A memory problem, a discipline problem. But when you look at it from the plastic chair, with juice on your tongue, it's actually a much plainer problem. The hours live in one place. The invoices live in another. And you, tired, with a client on the phone and a deadline on the screen, are the only bridge between them.

A bridge made of memory is going to fail now and then. That isn't character. That's engineering.

What if the two places simply looked at each other? What if each piece of work had a line, and each line knew whether it had been billed yet? What if you could glance at one page and see the whole month, without reconstructing anything?

You finish the second biscuit. You don't solve it. But the knot in your stomach loosens by a notch, because the problem has changed shape. It stopped being "what's wrong with me" and became "what's missing from my setup."

A different kind of Monday

Picture it a few weeks from now. Same you, same clients, same trains.

It's Monday morning, 9:10. The kettle is on. You open one file, and the week is laid out in front of you. Work done. Work billed. Work waiting. A gentle flag next to anything that has been sitting too long.

You see the Hartmann days, already attached to an invoice, with a due date next to them. You don't search. You don't wonder. You just look, nod and move on to the real work.

Ten minutes, and you close it. Not an evening. Not a weekend. Ten minutes, and the little cold drop in your stomach has nothing to attach to.

When a client says "quick question, did you bill the extra day?", you can answer in one calm sentence, because the answer is simply there.

That's all. No grand reinvention. Just not carrying the whole month in your head any more.

Before you go back out

A volunteer takes your empty cup and tells you to eat something proper. You promise you will.

On the way out, you do open the calendar again. This time you copy the dates of those five days onto a note, so you can check them properly when you're back at your desk. Whatever you find, you'll deal with it. Hours that were missed can be billed, politely, in plain words. It's usually less dramatic than it feels in the chair.
