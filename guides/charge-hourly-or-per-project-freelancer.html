Hourly or Per Project as a Freelancer? The Taxi Meter

2026-11-07

The taxi smells of pine air freshener and wet umbrellas. It is 8:40 on a grey Tuesday, and you are on your way to meet a new client in a part of town you don't know.

Your phone is open on your knee. The email from her sits there, polite and short: Could you send a quote for the new website? Hourly or fixed, whatever you prefer.

Whatever you prefer. That is the sentence you have read eleven times since last night.

The driver glances in the mirror. 'Meter or flat fare?' he asks, because you gave him an address at the edge of his zone. 'Flat is easier. But if there's traffic, the meter is better for me. If it's clear, flat is better for you. Nobody knows until we've gone.'

You laugh, a little too loudly. Because that is exactly it.

The two doors you keep standing in front of

You know the arguments. You have read them at midnight, in three browser tabs.

If you charge by the hour, the client watches every minute. Every email, every small question feels like it costs them. And you wonder whether you're paid for being good or only for being slow.

If you charge per project, you carry the risk. The client who 'just wants one tiny change' becomes the client of fourteen tiny changes. You remember the logo job that was supposed to take a weekend and swallowed three weeks. You smiled through it and sent the invoice anyway.

So you stand between two doors. Behind each one, you can picture yourself quietly losing money and saying nothing.

What nobody tells you is that the fear is rarely about the method. It's about not knowing. You don't know how long things really take you. You don't know what an hour of your life has to earn so that the month works. So whichever number you write feels like a guess, and a guess feels like a bet.

What the meter was really telling you

The taxi turns onto a long avenue. Red light. Red light. The meter ticks upward, steady and honest.

'See?' the driver says cheerfully. 'Today I win.'

You watch the numbers and something small shifts in your head.

The driver isn't anxious. He isn't sweating over whether he has chosen the right system. He knows roughly what a kilometre costs him, what an hour of his day has to bring in, and which streets eat time. Flat fare, meter: both are fine to him, because both sit on top of something he already knows.

You realise you have been asking the wrong question for years. Hourly or per project? sounds like a question about the client. It is really a question about you. What does your time have to earn, and how long do your jobs honestly take?

Once you can answer that, the choice stops feeling like a coin toss. Hourly can be a calm choice for work that is open-ended. A fixed price can be a calm choice for work you've done many times. Either way, there is a floor under your feet, and you aren't guessing in the dark.

The meeting you walk into

It turns out the client is lovely. Her office has a plant on every windowsill, and she offers you tea before she says a word about money.

When she asks, 'So, how do you like to work?', you don't hear your pulse in your ears. You say that for a project like this you'd prefer a fixed price, and that you'll send it by Thursday with exactly what's included. You say it slowly, the way you say things you actually believe.

She nods. 'That's easier for me, honestly. I just don't want surprises.'

Neither do you.

You realise the surprise was the thing you were both afraid of. Not the number itself, only the not-knowing behind it.

An ordinary Monday, later

Picture a Monday a few weeks on. The kettle clicks off. You open one file and see three things: what your minimum hour and day need to be, what you quoted, and what is still unpaid.

The new quote went out on Thursday, as promised. It wasn't padded and it wasn't apologetic. It was a number you can explain in one calm sentence, with a due date on the invoice that follows it.

When a client asks, 'Could you also...?', you don't flinch. You know what your time is worth, so you can say, 'Happy to. Here's what that adds.' No heat in your face. No evening spent rehearsing.

Some months you'll still pick wrong. A job will run long, or a client will be slower than the meter. That's allowed. The difference is that you will see it, learn from it, and charge a little better next time. It stops being a quiet loss you resent.

Hourly or per project was never going to make you safe. Knowing your number does.
