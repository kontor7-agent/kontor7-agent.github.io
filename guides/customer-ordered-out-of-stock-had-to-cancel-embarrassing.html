Had to Cancel a Customer's Order? The Choir Break Text

2026-10-17

It's the tea break at choir rehearsal. You are standing in the church hall kitchen with a plastic cup of lukewarm tea and your phone in your other hand.

There is a new message from a customer. It's friendly and a little excited. She asks whether the gift set will arrive before her mother's birthday on Saturday.

Your stomach drops.

The gift set needs the smoky black tea. You know, with a cold certainty, that the smoky black tea has been gone since last week. You sold the last tin at the weekend market. Your shop page still says in stock, because it says that until you remember to change it.

Behind you, someone laughs at a joke. The altos are arguing about biscuits. You are standing very still, rereading one sentence: I'm so looking forward to it!

The apology you draft three times

You step into the corridor, next to the coat rack, and start typing.

Hi, I'm so sorry, unfortunately...

Delete.

Hello! Small problem with your order...

Delete. It isn't a small problem to her. It's her mother's birthday.

You try a third version and it sounds like a stranger wrote it. Too stiff, too many apologies, or somehow both at once. Your face is warm. It's not the room.

This is the part nobody talks about. The money from one cancelled order is not what hurts. What hurts is the small voice that says: a real business would have known. A real shop wouldn't sell something it doesn't have. A real owner wouldn't be hiding in a corridor, rehearsing an apology next to somebody's umbrella.

You have a spreadsheet, of course. You think you do. You have a notebook too, and a list on your phone, and a sticky note on the shelf that says order black tea?? in your own handwriting. The question marks are the problem. Each of them is a small bet that you'll remember later.

What it quietly costs

The message gets sent eventually. It's honest and kind, and you offer a refund and a different blend. She replies with a polite no worries, thanks for telling me, and she probably means it.

It doesn't feel fine, though.

The second half of rehearsal passes in a blur. You sing the right notes and think about the shelf. You think about the three other things that might be low right now, and you can't say which. That's the real cost. It's not only the one cancellation. It's the low hum afterwards, the feeling that any order could be the next one.

On the walk home you start doing the quiet maths nobody asked you to do. How many orders this month came in for things that were nearly gone? How many times did you get lucky? How many customers got a slightly slower answer from you because you were checking whether you could still deliver?

You also notice what the hum does to you. You stop wanting to promote anything. A busy week feels like a threat instead of good news, because more orders means more chances to be caught out.

That isn't how you wanted to run a shop that you started because you love tea.

The shelf that stops surprising you

Here's the thing you realise the next morning, standing in the back room with a coffee and the cold light from the window.

The tea didn't run out suddenly. It ran down slowly, week by week, in plain sight. Nobody was watching it. The stock didn't surprise you. You just had no place where the numbers lived together and could tell you early.

It doesn't have to be a big system. It needs one calm page that knows what's on the shelf, what usually goes in a week, and how long your supplier takes. A page that says this one is getting low while there is still time to do something about it.

That's all. The shelf stops being a guess.

Imagine a Monday like that. You open the laptop with your coffee. One glance shows you which three items are close to the line. You place one order, send one short message to your supplier, and close the tab. It takes about as long as the coffee does.

Then the next customer message arrives. Will this arrive by Saturday? You read it, and your stomach stays exactly where it is. You answer yes, because you know it's true.

And on a Thursday evening

Picture the next choir break. The tea is still lukewarm and the altos are still arguing about biscuits. Your phone buzzes in your pocket.

You take it out and read it, and you feel nothing in particular. It's an order, nothing more. You put the phone away and join the circle, and when the director raises her hands you are only thinking about the song.

That's the change. It isn't dramatic. It's the absence of a small, hot dread you had stopped noticing was there.

If you recognise the corridor

If you've ever hidden by a coat rack drafting an apology to someone who was looking forward to your product, you're not careless. You were running a real shop from memory, and memory is a poor warehouse.
