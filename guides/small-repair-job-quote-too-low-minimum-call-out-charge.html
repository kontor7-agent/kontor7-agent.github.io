Minimum Call-Out Charge Too Low? The Dripping Tap Fee

2026-11-08

It is 1:20 on a Tuesday afternoon, and you are on your knees in someone else's kitchen.

The tap is fixed. It took twenty minutes. A new cartridge, a bit of patience, a rag to wipe the base. The customer is delighted. She is already holding her phone, thumb ready over the banking app.

"So what do I owe you?"

And there it is, the number you gave on the phone two days ago. Twenty-five. Probably thirty at most. It sounded fair then. It sounded friendly, even. Small job, small price.

Now you are standing in her hallway with a van that cost you fuel to get here and a cartridge you paid for yourself. You are thinking about the forty minutes you drove this morning. The forty minutes you will drive back. The call you took at breakfast, and the message you answered at lunch.

"Twenty-five is fine," you hear yourself say.

She thanks you twice. You smile. You carry your bag to the van and sit there with the door shut for a moment.

The job was twenty minutes. The cost wasn't.

Nobody tells you this when you start. The job is never just the job.

It is the drive there. The parking, which is never close. The ten minutes of "while you're here, could you just look at..." The part you had to collect on the way, and the receipt for it that is now somewhere in the door pocket. The admin afterwards: the message, the invoice, the checking that she really paid.

Small jobs feel harmless because the work is small. But the day around them is not small. One twenty-minute fix can quietly eat a whole morning.

You know this. You have known it for a while. You just never had a calm moment to look at it straight.

Instead you have a feeling. A faint, familiar sour taste after the nice jobs. The ones where everyone was happy and you still came home tired and a little bit poorer than the day deserved.

The voice that says "it's only a small one"

There is a voice that shows up every time someone asks, "How much would it cost to just have a look?"

It says: Don't scare them off. They'll call someone cheaper. It's only a tap. It's only a socket. It's only a door that sticks.

And under it, a quieter voice: If you say a proper number, you will sound greedy.

So you say the small number. You say it a little too fast, so it can't hurt. And then you spend the evening doing the sums in your head and not liking them.

It isn't greed. It is the opposite. It is a person who is good at the work and not yet comfortable with the idea that the work starts at the front door, not at the first screw.

The Friday that felt different

Picture a handyman, call him Marek. He is made up, a person we've imagined for this story, not a real customer of anything. He has the same Tuesday you do. Cartridge, kitchen, twenty-five.

What changes for him is small and a bit dull. One Friday he sits down with a mug of tea and the week's jobs on one page. Not to punish himself. Just to look.

He sees the three small jobs that took most of a Wednesday. He sees what each one actually left in his pocket. It is not a dramatic moment. There is no gasp. He simply notices that the little jobs weren't little at all, and that for the first time he can see the shape of it.

Then something quieter happens. He realises he does not have to argue with the customer. He does not have to defend a price. He only has to decide, once, calmly, what showing up at someone's door is worth. Then say it the way you would say the time of a train.

No apology on the front of it. No long explanation after.

A different Tuesday

Next time the phone rings, it's a dripping tap again.

"Happy to come out," he says. "A visit starts at this much, and then we'll see what the job needs. I'll tell you before I start."

There is a pause. Most people nod through the pause. Some say fine. A few say they'll think about it, and that is allowed too. The ones who stay are the ones who value the work, and those are the customers who make the week feel like a real business.

He finishes the tap. He writes down the cartridge and keeps the receipt in one place. By 1:45 he is in the van with a sandwich, and the sour taste isn't there.

It is just a Tuesday. Quiet. Fair. His.

That is all anyone is asking for. Not a fortune. Just for the day to add up the way you feel it should.
