Guilty About Raising Your Freelance Rate? The Client Call Pause

2026-10-13

It is 10:55 on a Thursday, and the call starts in five minutes.

You are standing in the hallway with your laptop open on the shoe cabinet, because that is where the wifi is strongest. On the screen: a half-written quote for a returning client. A landing page, three rounds of edits, the usual.

In the box marked rate, you have typed 55. Deleted it. Typed 65. Deleted it again.

Sixty-five feels greedy. Fifty-five feels like what you have always said.

And underneath both numbers sits the same small thought: who do I think I am?

The number you say out loud

Let's call her Mira. She is not a real person, just someone who might look a lot like you. A freelance designer, four years in. Good at her work. Slightly embarrassed by how much she likes being paid for it.

Mira's rate was not decided. It was inherited. She said 40 in her first month because a friend of a friend said 40. Then 50, because 40 felt silly after a year. Each raise was tiny and apologetic, tucked into an email under a paragraph of thank-yous.

Nobody ever pushed back. That is the strange part. The clients said yes quickly, and Mira did not feel relieved. She felt a flicker of doubt: too quickly. Could I have asked for more?

She never knew. So she kept the number, and carried the question around instead.

What the guilt is really made of

It looks like guilt about the client. This client has been loyal. They send work every month. They are kind in emails. Raising the price feels like a small betrayal.

But look closer and the guilt is mostly made of something else: not knowing.

She does not know what she needs to earn to have a good month, not just a survivable one. She does not know how many hours in a week are actually billable, once the emails, the invoicing and the unpaid revisions are subtracted. She does not know whether 55 is fair, low, or just a number she got used to.

When you cannot defend a price to yourself, you cannot say it calmly to someone else. Your voice goes up at the end. You add "or whatever works for you!" You apologise for a sum you chose.

That is the cost nobody puts on an invoice. Not just the money left on the table, though that is real. It is the small tightening in the chest every time the topic comes near. The resentment that shows up in month eight, when you are tired and the project has swollen and the rate has not.

The pause on the call

The call starts. The client is warm, as always. Ten minutes in, they ask: "Can you send over a quote for the next round?"

Mira says, "Of course." And then, because the silence needs filling, she says, "Same as last time is fine."

She hears herself say it. She closes the call, makes tea, and stands at the kitchen window for a while.

The tea goes cold. What bothers her is not that she said it. It is that she did not even decide to say it. The old number just came out, like a reflex.

The part where it gets lighter

Here is what changes things, and it is smaller than you expect.

It is not courage. It is not a pep talk about knowing your worth, which has never once made anyone's hands stop shaking over a send button.

It is having a number that comes from your life. Your costs, your quiet weeks, your time off, the hours you can honestly bill. A figure you worked out on a calm afternoon, not in a hallway with five minutes to spare. Once the number has a reason behind it, it stops feeling like a request for permission. It becomes a fact about how your business works.

You may still choose to raise gently, or only for new clients, or in January. That is yours to decide. But you decide from steady ground, not from the flicker of who do I think I am.

A normal Thursday, after

Picture the same call, a few weeks on.

The client asks for a quote. You say, "I'll send it this afternoon." No filler. No apology tucked into the end of the sentence.

You open the quote, and the rate is already there, a number you know the reasons for. You write one clear line about the new rates starting next project. You press send before the tea is cold.

Maybe the client says fine. Maybe they ask a question, and you answer it kindly. Maybe one day someone says it is too much, and you can wish them well without feeling the floor tilt.

The rest of the day is just a day. You work, you close the laptop at six, and the knot you had stopped noticing is simply not there.
