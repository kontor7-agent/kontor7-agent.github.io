Which Resume Version Did You Send? The Olive Stall Call

2026-11-05

The phone buzzes just as the man behind the olive stall hands you the little paper tub.

Unknown number. Lena, our character for this story, almost lets it ring out. It's Saturday lunchtime and the market is loud. But she's been waiting for a call for nine days, so she steps sideways between a cheese van and a table of lemons and answers.

"Hi Lena, it's Priya from Hollin & Reeve. I just wanted to say I really liked the version of your resume you sent. The product-writing angle was exactly what the team is after."

Lena says thank you. She says it warmly, like someone who knows exactly which version that was.

She doesn't.

The sentence she can't finish

There are four of them on her laptop. Four files, all named some variation of Resume_final. One leads with UX writing. One leads with content strategy. One is a single page. One has the freelance projects folded into a tidy block that she wrote at 7 am on a bad day and wasn't sure about.

"Great," says Priya. "Could you walk me through the project you mention at the top? I'd love to hear more about it on Monday."

The project at the top. Lena stands there with a tub of olives, trying to see the page in her head. Was it the app onboarding rewrite? Or the help-centre one? She could say either. One of them would be right, and the other would sound like someone who hasn't read her own resume.

"Of course," she hears herself say. "I'll go through it again beforehand."

She hangs up. The stall owner asks if she wants anything else. She shakes her head, and her appetite has quietly left the market.

What nobody says out loud

It isn't the number of applications that wears you down. It's the small, repeated moment of not knowing.

Not knowing which cover note went with which company. Whether you wrote "five years" in one place and "six" in another. Whether the company that wrote back on Wednesday is the one you applied to in a hurry, or the one you spent a whole weekend on.

You keep a rough map in your head, and it works until it doesn't. Then a stranger says something kind and specific, and you realise you're bluffing your way through your own job search.

That's a strange kind of embarrassment. Nobody saw it. Priya didn't notice a thing. But Lena felt it land somewhere under the ribs, the way you do when you're pretending to be more organised than you are.

The walk home

She doesn't go back to the stalls. She walks home with the olives untouched, and somewhere around the second crossing she stops being annoyed at herself and gets curious instead.

Why was it so hard to remember? She wasn't careless. She'd sent every application with care. The trouble was that the care went into the sending and nowhere else. Each application left her hands and disappeared into an inbox, a portal, a tab she'd closed.

She had been doing the work of ten applications and keeping the memory of two.

It didn't have to live in her head. That's what she thought, there at the red light, with the market noise fading behind her. It could simply live somewhere she could look at. One place. Company, date, which version, what she said, what happens next.

Not a grand system. Just a page that remembers, so she doesn't have to.

Monday, a different kind

Picture the same call, a few weeks later, with the same warm voice on the other end.

"I really liked the version you sent."

This time Lena is at her kitchen table with a cup of tea, and she doesn't need to panic. She glances at one line. Hollin & Reeve. Sent on the 24th. Product-writing version. Followed up on the 3rd. And beside it, a short note about the project she led with and the story she wants to tell about it.

"Thank you," she says. "That's the one that opens with the onboarding rewrite. I'm glad it landed. I'd love to talk about what we changed and why."

There's no pause. No bluffing. Her voice sounds like a person who knows her own work, because she does, and now she has somewhere to keep that knowing.

After the call she doesn't spiral. She doesn't scroll through four files, hoping for a clue. She just gets on with her day. The interview prep is waiting where she left it, with the stories she wants to tell already roughed out.

That is the quiet gift of it. It isn't more productivity. It's the loosening of a small, constant knot: what did I send, and to whom, and what happens now?
