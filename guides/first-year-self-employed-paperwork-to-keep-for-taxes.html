First Year Self-Employed, What Paperwork Do I Keep?

2026-11-01

It's Saturday, 9:10, and you're standing in the hallway with a carrier bag in each hand.

One holds paper. Till slips gone pale, a train ticket, a printed invoice with a coffee ring on it, an envelope from the tax office you haven't opened. The other holds your laptop and a half-eaten banana. You're about to leave for your sister's birthday brunch, and you've just realised you have no idea what any of it is for.

Eight months ago you quit your job to freelance as a translator. Nobody told you what comes with that. You have clients, you have invoices, and you have a growing feeling that there is a second job hiding behind the first one: the paperwork.

The question nobody answers

At brunch, your cousin Jana asks how the new life is going. You say "great, busy," which is true.

Then she asks, casually, "Do you keep all your receipts?"

You laugh a little too fast. "Sure. Yes. Mostly."

What you mean is: I don't know which ones count. I don't know how long to keep them. I don't know whether the screenshot of that software subscription is enough. I don't know if I'm supposed to be doing something every month that I'm not doing.

You nod and cut your croissant in half. Under the table, your thumb is scrolling through your banking app, looking for a payment you can't place.

What the first year quietly costs

Nobody warns you that the first year isn't hard because of the work. It's hard because every small decision is brand new.

Is this a business thing or a personal thing? Do I keep the paper one or the email one? Did I already log that train ticket, or did I only mean to?

So you do what most of us do. You keep everything, just in case. The bag in the hallway. A folder on the desktop called "Stuff". A photo album on your phone with 140 blurry pictures of receipts, each one a tiny promise to your future self.

And a low hum sits behind your days. It's not panic. It's lower than that. It's the feeling of not being quite a real business yet, of winging it with a straight face.

It shows up at odd moments. When a client asks for your tax details and you hesitate. When you see an envelope from an official office. When a friend with a steady job says "just hand it all to the accountant," and you realise you'd have to hand over a carrier bag and an apology.

The moment it shifts

On Sunday afternoon, you finally tip the bag out on the kitchen table.

It isn't as bad as you thought. That's the strange part. It's a lot, but it's finite. Every slip is just a small fact: a date, a place, an amount, a reason.

You realise what's been wearing you down isn't the paper. It's that none of it has a home. Each receipt is an open question, and open questions are heavy when there are a hundred of them.

You also realise something kinder. You don't have to know the rules by heart. You only need your own records to be in one calm place, so that when you do ask a tax professional what applies to you, you can say "here's everything, in order" instead of "um, I think I have some of it."

That's all. One place. One list of what's still to do. A sense of how far along you are.

You don't need to become an expert in a weekend. You need the pile to turn into a list.

An ordinary Monday, a few months on

Picture it.

It's Monday, 8:30. The kettle is on. A client has just paid, and before you open your first document you spend ten minutes with your coffee. You add last week's few receipts, tick the two small admin jobs that were due, and glance at a simple bar that shows how far along the year-end list is.

Then you close it.

The carrier bag is gone. The photo album of blurry slips is empty. The envelope from the tax office is open, and the date inside is on a short deadline list that you can see at a glance, so nothing creeps up on you.

When Jana asks again, at the next family lunch, "Do you keep all your receipts?", you smile. "I do, actually. They have a home."

There's no speech in it. It just feels like being someone who runs a real business, quietly, without drama.

And when December comes, there's no January scramble waiting behind it. Nobody at the kitchen table at midnight, sorting slips into piles and doing sums in the margin.

Just a year, already half-sorted, and a weekend that belongs to you.
