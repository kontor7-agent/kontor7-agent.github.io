Client Agreed to Pay but Keeps Making Excuses? Freelancer

2026-10-16

It is 14:50 on a Thursday, and you are parked outside the primary school with the engine off. Twelve minutes until the bell.

Your phone is propped against the steering wheel. The email from your client is open. You have read it three times.

"Hi Mara, so sorry, our finance person is out this week. Will definitely sort it next Tuesday! Thanks for your patience."

This is the third "definitely". The first one was a call, three weeks ago. Yes, of course, it's approved, it just needs to go through. The second was a short message about a system update. Now, a person who is out.

Nobody has said no. That is the strange part. Nobody has refused. Everyone has been friendly.

And still the money is not there.

The friendly kind of stuck

Mara designs brochures and websites for small companies. She is the illustrative one in this story, but you may know her well. She is good at her work. The client even said the project looked wonderful.

What she is not good at, if she is honest, is the part after the work.

Because a flat refusal is easy to answer. A polite excuse is much harder. You cannot argue with "finance is out this week". You cannot be annoyed at someone who apologised so nicely. So you type back, No problem at all, thank you! and feel something small curdle in your stomach.

That is the quiet cost. It is not only the amount, though the amount matters. It is the rent date you keep doing sums around. It is the thought at the supermarket checkout, wondering whether the card will be fine this week. It is the way you start to feel like a nuisance for asking for what you already earned.

What the waiting actually costs

Mara has done the maths in her head more than once. If Tuesday comes and goes, she will have to write again. If she writes again, she has to decide on a tone. Too soft and she is ignored. Too firm and she might lose a client who sends her work twice a year.

So she doesn't write. She waits one more day. Then another.

Meanwhile the invoice sits in a folder, and a small mental file labelled Is it awkward yet? sits open all day, every day. It takes up space during calls with other clients. It shows up at dinner. It hovers over a Sunday walk.

The bell rings. Children pour out in bright jackets. Mara puts her phone face down and waves at her daughter.

But the knot comes along on the drive home.

The moment it clicked

That evening, after the dishes, she did something small. She did not write the email. She just looked at the situation as if it belonged to a stranger.

The invoice was due 24 days ago. There had been three promises. Each one had been accepted without any reply that moved things forward.

And she noticed something. She hadn't been struggling with the client. She had been struggling with not knowing the next step. What do you say after the first excuse? After the second? When is it fine to be firmer? Every time, she was inventing the answer from scratch, at the worst possible moment, with her stomach already tight.

People who send invoices for a living in larger companies don't agonise like this. They have a rhythm. Day one after the due date, a friendly note. Later, a clearer one. Later still, a firm one. Nobody takes it personally, because it is simply how it goes.

She didn't need to become tougher. She needed the decision to be already made.

A different Thursday

Picture the same car park, a few weeks on, a different invoice.

It is 14:50. Engine off. Twelve minutes.

The client has said "definitely next week". Mara reads it, nods, and opens one overview. It shows her what has been sent, what date it was, and what the calm next message is. She is not composing. She is choosing from words that already sound like her on a good day: warm, clear, and unembarrassed.

She sends it. It takes under two minutes. She closes the laptop lid, and notices she is humming.

Nothing dramatic has changed. The client may still be slow. But the dread has gone out of it. She is no longer a person begging for a favour. She is a professional following a routine, politely, on schedule.

The bell rings. She waves.

And the drive home is just a drive home.
