Sold Out After They Ordered? The Long Weekend You Spent Apologising

2026-10-14

Imagine Maren. She blends and sells loose-leaf tea from a spare room, a shelf of tins and a small online shop. Her story is made up, but you may know the feeling.

It is Friday, 2:10 pm. She is standing on a gravel path above a lake, in the first hour of a long weekend she booked three months ago. The air smells of pine and warm stone. Her boots are new. She has promised herself that the phone stays in the bag.

The phone buzzes in the bag.

She tells herself it is the weather app. She takes two more steps. It buzzes again.

The order for something you don't have

The notification is from the shop: New order: Smoked Earl Grey, 3 tins.

A small, cold drop in the stomach. She cannot remember the last time she saw that blend on the shelf. Was there one tin left? Two? She packed a big order on Wednesday and thought: I should check. Then the phone rang, and the thought went wherever those thoughts go.

She stands very still on the path while her friends walk ahead. She could check the shelf, but the shelf is two hours away. She could check the spreadsheet, so she opens it on a screen too small for it. Columns are cut off. One cell says ~4? in her own handwriting-by-keyboard. A question mark. She typed that herself, on a day she was in a hurry.

She does not know. That is the whole problem. Not that the tea is gone, because tea runs out. It is that she does not know, and the customer already has a confirmation email.

What it quietly costs

By 3 pm she has opened and closed the shop dashboard four times. By 4 pm she is on a bench by the water, drafting an email that begins "Unfortunately." She rewrites it. The version that sounds casual feels careless. The version that sounds sorry feels like a confession.

She thinks of the customer, who is probably looking forward to it. Maybe it is a present. Maybe it is the one nice thing they ordered this week.

The lake is beautiful. She sees about a third of it.

That is the cost nobody puts in a spreadsheet. It is not only the refund or the apology. It is a weekend half spent somewhere else, and the small, nagging feeling of being "not a real shop". Real shops know what is on their shelves. Real shops do not find out from a customer's order.

She has done this before. A refund here, a "so sorry, a delay of three weeks" there. Each time she promises herself she will sort it out properly. Each time the next week is busy, and sorting it out is the thing that gets pushed to Sunday, then to next Sunday.

The sentence she says out loud

On the walk back to the cabin, her friend Jonas asks what is wrong. She tells him. Not the whole thing, just: "I sold something I don't have, and I don't even know how many I don't have."

He laughs, not unkindly. "So the problem isn't the tea. It's that you carry the shelf in your head."

She doesn't answer. But the sentence stays with her through dinner, through the dishes, through a sleep that is lighter than it should be.

It is true. She has been the stock system. Every count, every "I think we're low on that," every reorder that happens only when a tin looks lonely. All of it lives in one tired head, and that head was supposed to be on holiday.

The thought arrives gently, the way a good one does: it doesn't have to be like this.

Not a bigger warehouse or a fancier shop. Just a place where the numbers live, so she doesn't have to. A list that says what is on the shelf, what is on its way, and which blends are getting low before they are gone. Something she glances at over coffee, not something she reconstructs on a bench.

A different Monday

Picture the same shop a few weeks later. It is Monday, 8:30, and the kettle is on.

Maren opens one file. It takes under a minute. Two blends are marked as running low, with room to spare, so the supplier order goes out before lunch. Nothing is a surprise. The Smoked Earl Grey is on its way, and she knows roughly when.

She updates three numbers from the weekend's orders. The page settles. She feels what you feel when a drawer finally closes properly.

Then she does the thing she could not do on that gravel path: she stops thinking about stock. The rest of the morning is for the things she actually likes, such as a new blend she has been testing and a photo of the tins in the window light.

The next time she goes away, the phone stays in the bag. If it buzzes, it is only an order, and the order is for something she has.

If you recognise the bench

Maybe your shelf is tea, candles, prints, or soap. Maybe your "system" is a question mark in a cell and a good memory that has been working too hard. If you have ever apologised to a customer for a thing you could have seen coming, you already know the weight of it.
