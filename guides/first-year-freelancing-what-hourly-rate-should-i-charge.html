Guessing Your Freelance Hourly Rate? The Required Field

2026-10-24

It is 9:15 on a Wednesday, and the cursor is blinking in a box marked Hourly rate*.

The asterisk means you can't skip it. You are filling in a supplier form for a client who said yes to your proposal yesterday. The yes still feels warm in your chest. Now this: a white box, a currency symbol, and a number you are expected to simply know.

You don't know it. You have never known it.

Let's call you Mira. You write product copy for small software companies. Your first year is almost over. You have a cup of tea going cold next to the laptop and a thought looping through your head: everyone else seems to have a number. Where did they get it?

The number you borrowed

So you do what you have always done. You look sideways.

A forum thread from three years ago. A colleague's remark at a meetup that you half-remember. The rate you put on your first invoice, which you chose because it sounded "not scary." It was a number picked to be agreeable, not a number built on anything.

You type 45. You delete it. You type 60. Delete. You type 45 again, because 60 makes your stomach tighten, and you can't say why.

That tightness is the interesting part. It isn't greed or shyness. It's the feeling of standing on a floor you never checked. If the client says "that's a lot," you have nothing to say back. Not an argument, not a reason. Only a flinch.

What guessing quietly costs

Nobody tells you that a guessed rate is expensive in small, invisible ways.

It costs you on the call where you say your price and then immediately add "but we can talk about it," before anyone has even objected.

It costs you in the quiet month, when you wonder whether the problem is the market or the number. You can't tell, because you never knew what the number was supposed to cover.

It costs you on Sunday evenings, when you add up what came in and feel a low hum of is this okay? Is this enough? There's no answer, because you never defined "enough."

And it costs you a little dignity. You call yourself a freelancer, but some mornings it feels more like improvising a business and hoping nobody asks to see the plan.

The box is still blinking.

The question underneath

Mira leans back and, for the first time all morning, stops typing.

The real question isn't what do other people charge? It's smaller and more personal: what does one working hour of mine actually have to carry?

Because an hour you bill is never only an hour. It's also the hours you don't bill: the emails, the proposals that go nowhere, the afternoon you spend on your own invoices. It's the weeks off you'd like to take without panic. It's the slow month that will come, because slow months do. It's the part of your income that isn't really yours to spend.

None of that is mysterious. It's just never been put in one place, where you can look at it calmly. You've been carrying it all in your head, and a head makes a terrible spreadsheet.

So you close the supplier form. It can wait an hour. The client won't mind; they've said nothing about a deadline.

A different kind of number

Here is what changes, and it's not dramatic.

There's a number on your screen that you didn't borrow. It has a reason behind it. You could point at it and say: this is the least an hour has to be, so that my year works. Not a dream rate, not a bargaining position. A floor.

Standing on a floor you've checked feels different from standing on one you've guessed at. Your shoulders drop a centimetre. The tightness doesn't vanish, but it has a shape now, and a shape is easier to live with than a fog.

You go back to the supplier form. You type a number that is higher than the one you almost chose, and also, oddly, easier to type. No flinch. The box accepts it without comment.

That is the whole drama. A form field, filled in by someone who knows why.

An ordinary Monday, afterwards

A few weeks later, a prospective client asks on a call, "And what's your rate?"

The old you would have heard a small alarm. The new you says the number the way you'd give your postcode. A beat of silence follows, as there always is. But you're not filling that silence with apologies. You're just waiting, tea in hand, the window open behind you.

Afterwards you send the first invoice of the week. It's clean, it has the due date on it, and it looks like it came from a business. You close the laptop at six, and the evening is simply an evening.

Nothing about your talent changed. You only stopped guessing.
