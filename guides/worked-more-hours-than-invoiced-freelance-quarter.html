Worked More Hours Than You Invoiced? The Arrivals Hall

2026-10-18

The board says delayed, 55 minutes. Mara sighs, finds a seat by the pillar with the phone chargers, and opens her laptop on her knees.

She is a freelance UX researcher. Today she is also a sister, waiting for someone who will land at some point. Fifty-five minutes is a gift, she decides. She will use it to close out the quarter.

The quarter ended on Tuesday. She has not looked at it yet.

She opens the invoices folder. Seven invoices, neatly named. She adds up the amounts on the back of a boarding-pass envelope someone left on the seat. Then she opens her calendar and scrolls back through July, August, September.

Something is off. She can't say what yet. It is just a feeling, low in the stomach, like walking into a room and knowing something has been moved.

The feeling before the number

She remembers the August workshop that ran into a second day. The follow-up calls that were "quick" and weren't. The report she kept polishing on a Sunday because the client had been so kind, and she didn't want to ruin that by sending a bill that looked bigger than they expected.

Were those on an invoice? Some of them, probably. Maybe.

She tries her notes app. There are half-lines like Thurs – interviews + synthesis? and call w/ Jonas, 1h-ish. She tries her email sent folder, scrolling for proof. She tries her bank app, as if the answer might be hiding in the incoming payments.

A family walks past with a balloon on a stick and a very tired toddler. A voice announces a gate change for a flight to somewhere warm.

Mara stares at the screen. She thinks: I know I worked more hours than I invoiced. I just can't prove it, so I can't do anything about it.

That is the part nobody tells you. The loss itself isn't always the worst bit. It is the fog around it. If you can't see exactly what went missing, you can't be angry in a useful way. You can only feel vaguely tired and slightly foolish, and tell yourself that next quarter you'll be more careful.

You told yourself that last quarter too.

What the fog costs

It costs money, of course. Maybe a few hours, maybe a couple of days of work that nobody was ever asked to pay for.

But it also costs something quieter. Mara notices that when she quotes a new project, she pads it, then feels guilty about the padding. When a client says "could you just…", she says yes a little too fast, because she has no picture of how many justs she has already given away. When people ask how business is, she says "busy, good, busy", and the words have no number behind them.

Busy is not the same as paid. She knows that. Somewhere in the pit of her stomach, she has known it for weeks.

The arrivals doors slide open and shut. Not her sister. A man with a cardboard sign for someone called Dr. Okafor.

The sentence that changed the next hour

She closes the notes app. And for the first time in a while, she asks a different question.

Not what did I do wrong? but what would it look like if I could just see this?

Not a grand system. No new software to learn on a Friday afternoon. Just one calm place where a client, a project, the hours she worked and the invoice that covered them sit next to each other. Where a gap between "worked" and "billed" would simply show up, in plain sight, before the quarter was over rather than after.

She thinks about how that would feel. Not dramatic. Just quiet. Like the moment when a loud appliance in the next room switches off, and you realise how long it had been humming.

She would open it on a Monday morning. She would see the week. She would notice that the Jonas project had forty hours behind it and only thirty on paper, and she would send a short, friendly note while it was still fresh, instead of finding out in October that it was never going to happen.

The awkwardness she fears most, asking for money long after the work, would mostly never come up. The conversation would happen early and be small.

A normal Monday, later

It is easy to picture. Coffee. The laptop open on the kitchen table. Ten minutes, maybe, enough to log last week's hours, glance at what is open and what is overdue, and close the lid.

No scrolling through sent mail like a detective. No rounding in the dark. No Sunday dread that belongs to an invoice you may or may not have written.

Just a clear picture, and then the day. A walk before lunch. A client call where she can say her number without clearing her throat, because the number is real.

The board flickers. Landed.

Mara shuts the laptop. The quarter is still a little blurry, and she will have to reconstruct what she can from the calendar. But she decides this is the last quarter she does it this way.

The doors open, and there is her sister, dragging a suitcase with one broken wheel and waving with her whole arm.
