Client Feedback After Project Closed? The Cafeteria Table

2026-11-05

Thursday, 11:15. You are in a hospital cafeteria, holding a paper cup of tea that is too hot to drink.

Your dad is down the corridor for a check-up. The nurse said forty minutes, maybe an hour. You brought your laptop "just in case", which is the kind of sentence that has ended many quiet mornings.

Then your phone buzzes.

Hi Elena, one more thought on the logo. Could it feel a little warmer? Not a big change!

You read it twice.

The project closed three weeks ago. The files were delivered. The final invoice was paid, and you remember the small, private exhale when it landed. You filed the folder under "done". You even deleted the project from your to-do list, which felt like closing a door gently.

Now the door is open again, and a cold draught is coming through it.

The reply that won't write itself

You start typing.

Hi Tom, thanks for your message! Happy to take a look at...

You delete it. That sounds like a yes, and you are not sure it is a yes.

Hi Tom, as the project is now closed...

Delete. That sounds like a locked gate, and Tom has been kind. He paid on time. He said thank you. He probably has no idea he is asking for anything.

That is the part nobody tells you. The difficult messages are rarely from difficult people. They come from nice clients who simply don't see where the edges of the work are, because you never had a moment to show them.

So you sit there, a grown professional in a plastic chair, bargaining with yourself.

It's only a colour tweak. Ten minutes, tops. Maybe he'll hire you again if you just do it. But last time it was ten minutes, it turned into two evenings, and nobody counted them.

A man at the next table is eating soup very slowly. A trolley rattles past. You still have no reply.

What it quietly costs

It isn't the ten minutes. It's the low hum that comes with them.

It's checking your inbox in the supermarket queue to see whether Tom has written again. It's the faint dread when a finished client's name appears in bold. It's never quite feeling finished, even with the money in your account.

And under that hum sits a smaller, sharper thought: a real business would know what to say here.

You do know what you want to say. You want to be warm, and you want to be clear. You want to say: "I'm glad you're enjoying it. This part is complete. If you'd like a fresh round of changes, I'd be happy to quote it." You just can't find the words that sound like you on a day when your mind is half down the corridor with your dad.

So the draft sits there. A blinking line. A small weight.

The moment it turned

Here is what happens next, and it's not dramatic.

You remember that you once saved a page of ready-made email wording for exactly this kind of moment. Not a template that sounds like a robot wrote it, but a starting point with room for your own details: the client's name, what was delivered, what you'd like to say, and how warm or how brisk you want to be.

You open it. You fill in three gaps: Tom, the logo, the handover date. You pick the gentle tone, the one that says thank you before it says no.

A draft appears. You read it the way you'd read a note from a friend who happens to be good with words. You change one sentence, because Tom likes things short. You add his favourite phrase from the kickoff call.

Then you press send. It takes about four minutes. You drink your tea. It is finally the right temperature.

Nothing in the message is harsh. It thanks him. It says the project is wrapped up, and that small changes after delivery sit outside what was agreed. It also says you'd be glad to look at a new round of work if he wants one, and it names the next step plainly. Nobody is told off. Nobody is left guessing.

A Thursday that stays yours

The after is not a grand transformation. It's smaller than that, and better.

Your dad comes back down the corridor, a little tired, a little cheerful, holding a leaflet about autumn walking groups. You close the laptop because there is nothing left to do on it. You buy him a slice of lemon cake. You talk about the leaflet.

When Tom answers that afternoon, it's a thumbs-up and a note that he'd love a quote for the new packaging. You read it at a red light and smile at nothing.

The next time a closed project knocks again, you won't feel the old drop in your stomach. You'll know there is a calm, kind sentence ready, and that it will sound like you on your best day, even if you are having a rather ordinary one.

That is what we mean by a professional. Not someone who never gets the awkward message, but someone who doesn't have to carry it around for three days.
