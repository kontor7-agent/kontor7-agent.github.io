Selling Handmade Weekly, No Money Left? The Dryer Timer

2026-10-24

The dryer has eleven minutes left. You are sitting on the orange plastic chair in the launderette with your phone in one hand and a paper cup of coffee in the other. It is Thursday, just after ten.

On the screen: three notifications. A new order for an embroidered tote bag. A payout from the marketplace. A message from a customer asking if the linen print comes in green.

You should feel good. You sold something every day this week. You packed parcels until your fingers were sore.

But you also opened your banking app this morning, and the number made your stomach go quiet.

Where did it go?

The strange maths of a busy week

Let's call you Ines. You are an illustrative character, but you might feel familiar. You embroider tote bags and small prints at your kitchen table. You sell online during the week and at a market stall twice a month.

You are not lazy. You are not bad with money. You are the person who remembers every birthday in the family.

But every time you try to work out what a tote bag really earns, the sum falls apart halfway through. There is the thread you bought in a sale. There is the blank bag from the supplier. There is the listing fee, the payment fee, the postage you charged and the postage you paid. There is the box, the tissue paper and the sticker you ordered because the parcel looked plain without it.

And then there are the hours. You never count the hours. That feels almost rude, as if counting them would make your hobby into something cold.

So you do what many makers do. You look at the sale price, think "that's decent", and move on.

The payout arrives. It is smaller than the sales. You tell yourself it is the fees and carry on.

The sentence you don't say at the stall

On market days it is worse in a quieter way.

A woman picks up a tote bag, turns it over, admires the stitching. She asks, "Is that your best price?"

You smile and say, "Of course, take it for less." You say it fast, because the silence feels expensive.

Driving home, the cash box is lighter than you hoped, and you cannot say exactly why. Was it the discount? The stall fee? The tote bags you gave away to a friend of a friend? The notebook with the market takings is in one bag. The online orders are in another place entirely.

Nobody warns you about this part of making things. You can be good at the craft and still feel like you are guessing at the business. Under that sits a small, stubborn worry: what if I am working this hard to earn almost nothing?

You don't say it to anyone. It sounds ungrateful. People buy your work. They tell you it is beautiful.

Eleven minutes, one honest question

The dryer thumps. A man in the corner folds a duvet cover with great patience.

You open the marketplace dashboard, and for once you don't look at the sales total. You look at one single order. The tote bag. Sale price at the top, then the deductions listed underneath, line by line.

You take a breath and try to add your own costs. Thread. Bag. Packaging. Your head starts to blur.

And then a thought arrives, very calm and very plain: the problem is not that I am bad at this. The problem is that I have never seen it laid out in one place.

You have been carrying the whole picture in your head. Of course it feels heavy. Nobody can hold fees, materials, postage, market stall costs and their own time in memory and still feel relaxed about it.

It doesn't have to live in your head. A simple page that holds the numbers for each product could do that job for you. Then you could look at it, the way you look at a finished piece, and know.

What a different Thursday feels like

Imagine the same launderette, the same orange chair.

The notification comes in: new order, tote bag. This time you already know what that bag leaves you with after the fees and the materials. Not roughly. Not "probably fine". You know.

You also know which of your products quietly earns well, and which one you love making but sells for too little. The linen print, perhaps, is the real star. The little coasters that took so long may need a higher price, or a gentle retirement.

At the next market, when someone asks about the best price, you don't hurry to fill the silence. You have a number you trust. You can say it kindly and let it stand.

If you want to offer a market-day deal, you do it on purpose, knowing what it costs. That is a very different feeling from giving money away by accident.

The cash box and the online orders end up in the same picture. When you open your banking app on a Thursday, the number is just a number. It no longer feels like a verdict on you.

You fold the warm laundry, pick up the coffee cup and still have the afternoon for your embroidery hoop.
