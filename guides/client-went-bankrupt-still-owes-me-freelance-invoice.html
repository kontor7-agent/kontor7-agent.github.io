Client Went Bankrupt, Still Owes You? The Grey Envelope

2026-11-09

The envelope is grey, and it is thicker than a bill.

It is 7:50 on a Thursday. You are standing in the hallway in socks, one hand on the door, the other holding a coffee that is going cold. The sender's name is a law firm you have never heard of. Underneath, in small print: Insolvenzverwaltung. Insolvency administration.

You know before you open it. You open it anyway.

Your biggest client of the year has filed for insolvency. The letter is polite and formal. It mentions a procedure, a deadline, a form. It does not mention the invoice for the brand guidelines, the one for the website, or the one you sent in September and stopped looking at in October.

The sum you already spent

You do the arithmetic standing in the hallway, because you can't help it. It is not a small number. It is the rent for a few months. It is the holiday you did not book. It is the money you mentally spent the day you sent the invoice.

The strange part is the shame. Nobody has accused you of anything, and still you feel it. Should I have seen this coming? Should I have said something sooner?

You think back to August. The payment was a week late, then two. You wrote one short email, got a friendly "on it!" in reply, and let it go. Chasing felt like begging. Their logo was on your portfolio. They seemed busy, not broke.

Busy and broke can look exactly the same from the outside.

What the day does to you

You shower. You make a second coffee. You open the laptop and look at the project folder, and it feels like looking at a house you used to live in.

At eleven you have a call with a different client, a small one, a friendly one. You hear yourself being cheerful. Halfway through, they ask, "Everything okay on your side?"

"Yes, all good," you say. Which is what you always say.

After the call you notice that you have no idea whether they have paid you. You think they did. You are fairly sure. You scroll the bank app for a minute, then another, and the hot little knot in your stomach tightens a notch, because the bankruptcy is not the only open question. It is just the loudest.

That is the worry nobody talks about. It isn't only the one lost invoice. It's the sudden realisation that you've been running your money on a feeling of probably fine.

What you can't change, and what you can

Here is the honest part. A letter from an insolvency administrator is a matter for people who do this for a living: a lawyer, a professional adviser, the administrator's own instructions. This story can't tell you what you will get back, or how. Nobody can promise you that. It may be a little, it may be nothing, and it may take a long time.

That part is out of your hands. You file what you are asked to file, you ask a professional the questions that need a professional, and you let the process be slow.

But as you sit there, you notice a smaller question that is yours to answer.

How quickly would you have known?

Not how to prevent a company from failing. You can't. But whether the August silence would have shown up on a page, clearly, with a date next to it and a calm next step, instead of drifting in the back of your mind as a vague unease. Whether the quiet client would have been one line you could see, instead of a feeling you could dodge.

A different kind of Monday

Picture the same week, a few months from now.

It's Monday, 8:40. You open one sheet, and it takes ten minutes. Three invoices are open. One is two days late, shown in a gentle amber. One is nineteen days late, and next to it a short line says what the next calm step is. You copy a friendly, steady email you wrote once and never have to rewrite, change the name, and press send.

No draft number nine. No staring at the cursor. No "is this too rude, is this too weak?"

Then you close the laptop and go and buy bread.

Nothing about this makes every client pay. Some won't, and some situations stay hard. But you are the first to notice instead of the last. The silence has a date on it. You stop negotiating with yourself at 2 a.m. about whether it's "too soon to ask".

And on the day a grey envelope does arrive, if one ever does, you open it knowing exactly what else is outstanding and with whom. That is a different kind of morning. It is a quieter one.

Back in the hallway

Back in the real week, you do something small. You write down every open invoice on one page, every client, every date. It takes twenty minutes and it hurts a little. When it's done, the knot is still there, but it has edges now. You can see where it starts and where it stops.

Then you go for a walk, because it's Thursday and it's allowed.
