Agency Paid Late Again? A Translator's Quiet Tuesday Lunch

2026-10-14

It is 12:40 on a Tuesday. You are standing at the counter of the bakery near your flat, holding a paper bag with a cheese roll in it, and your phone is open to your banking app.

Nothing. Again.

The agency's invoice was for a 14,000-word medical manual. You remember it well. Two weekends, a lot of tea, one terminology query you sent at midnight. The payment terms said 30 days. That was a while ago. You think it might be sixty by now, but you are not sure, and that is the part that bothers you most.

You are not sure.

The email you start and don't send

Back at your desk, you open a new message to the project manager. You type Dear Anna, and stop.

You like Anna. She sends you work. She is polite, quick, a little overworked. Last month she gave you a rush job on a Friday afternoon and you said yes. Now you are about to write something that might sound like an accusation.

So you try: Just a gentle reminder... Too soft. You try: As per our agreed terms... Too stiff. You delete both.

A translator's work is words. You can find the exact right register for a legal contract in three languages. And still you cannot find the right way to say: Please pay me what you owe me.

It is a strange kind of embarrassment. It feels like begging, even though it is just a date on a calendar.

What it quietly costs

The invoice itself is not the whole problem. The fog around it is.

Is this the manual invoice, or the subtitling one from the other agency? Did the first agency say "payment run at month end" or was that someone else? Was there a purchase order number you were supposed to quote? You scroll through your sent folder, then through a folder called Invoices, then through a spreadsheet you started in January and stopped updating in April.

Twenty minutes pass. You have not translated a word.

And under the admin there is a quieter worry, the one you rarely say aloud. If this one is late, and the other one is late, how thin does the month get? You do the sums in your head while you rinse your mug. You are fine. Probably. But "probably" is a tiring place to live.

So you do what many of us do. You wait one more week. Then another. You tell yourself it is more professional not to fuss. Meanwhile you accept the next job from the same agency, because you need the work, and the pile of unpaid invoices grows a little taller.

The moment it shifts

The turn does not come as a dramatic event. It is smaller than that.

Imagine a different version of this same Tuesday. You open one simple page and see three lines: the agency, the amount, the day it was due. One line is marked 14 days late. Beside it sits a short message, already worded, in a tone that is warm and clear and entirely yours to adjust. You change one word, add Anna's name, and press send.

Two minutes. The cheese roll is still warm.

The realisation is not "I need to be tougher." It is gentler: this does not have to be improvised every time. The wording problem has been solved before. The remembering problem can live somewhere other than your head.

Once you see it that way, a lot of the dread goes quiet. Chasing becomes a routine, like backing up your files. Not a confrontation. A tidy, polite, regular thing.

A normal Monday, afterwards

Picture next month. Monday morning, 9:10. Coffee on the left of the keyboard, a glossary open on the right.

Before you start the day's text, you take five minutes with your list of open invoices. Two are on time. One is a few days past due, so a friendly nudge goes out. One agency has gone silent for three weeks, so a slightly firmer note goes out, still courteous, still clear about the date and the amount.

Then you close it and translate.

When the payment from Anna lands on Thursday, you feel something you had almost forgotten: nothing. No sigh, no relief so sharp it hurts. Just money arriving when it should, because someone, namely you, quietly kept the thread.

You can say yes to the next job without the little flinch. You can take a lunch break without refreshing the banking app. And if an agency really is slow, you know it, with dates, and you know what to say next.

That is the difference between hoping to get paid and knowing where you stand. Your work does not change. Your evenings do.
