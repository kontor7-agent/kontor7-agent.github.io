Mail Merge, State Names All Different? The Parcel Counter

2026-11-06

Thursday, 10:20, the parcel counter

The clerk slides the first tray of envelopes back across the counter. She does it gently, the way you hand back a coat someone left behind.

"These three can't be sorted," she says. "No state. Or it says Cali. And this one has the street twice."

Behind you, the queue shifts its weight. Someone sighs. You have 412 holiday letters in two plastic crates, a roll of stamps you already paid for, and a small, hot feeling in your chest.

Imagine you are Marit. She makes hand-printed cards and calendars, works alone from a spare room, and this is her one big mailing of the year. Every customer who ever bought from her gets a letter and a discount code. It took her two evenings to get the labels done. She was proud of that.

Now she is holding an envelope addressed to Jo Hartley, 14 Elm St. Apt 2, new york, and another to J. Hartley, 14 Elm Street, #2, N.Y. The same person. Twice. Printed side by side.

What the spreadsheet had been saying all along

The list was never one list. It was five years of everything stacked on top of each other: the shop export, the market-day sign-up sheet, the names a friend typed in from a notebook, the customers who filled out the form on their phone with their thumbs.

So the same state showed up as CA, Ca., Calif., california and, once, Californa. Streets were St, St., Street and sometimes Str. A postcode had lost its leading zero. A few cells had a space at the end that nobody could see.

When she ran the mail merge the night before, it did exactly what she asked. It put every cell onto a label, faithfully. Nothing looked wrong on screen at 22:00. It only looked wrong under the fluorescent light of a post office, with a queue behind her.

The quiet cost

The printing was not the expensive part. Neither was the postage, although a few of those letters would come back weeks later with a yellow sticker, and she would pay for that too.

The cost was the feeling. The small shame of standing at a counter with a crate of your own work, being told it isn't quite ready. The thought that a real business would have this sorted. The creeping suspicion that some customers wouldn't get the letter at all, and would think she had forgotten them.

She took the crates home. She spent the afternoon fixing labels by hand, one at a time, scrolling a list she no longer trusted. Every fix made her wonder what she hadn't caught. The mailing went out two days late. Nobody complained. That was almost worse, because it meant nobody knew how much it had cost her.

The turning point is smaller than you think

Here is the part Marit doesn't see coming that Thursday. The problem was never her carelessness. Nobody types addresses the same way twice. Not customers, not friends, not her own tired self at the market. A list that grows over years will always look like this.

What was missing was a quiet step between the messy list and the printer. A moment where the list gets read through once, the obvious things get tidied, and anything unsure is set aside for a human to look at. And, importantly, a way to see what changed before it changes anything.

That is all. It isn't a new career or a database course. It's a pause with a tidy page at the end of it.

An ordinary Monday, afterwards

Same spare room, a different week. It is Monday, 9:15, the kettle still warm. The list opens, the same five years of it, and nothing about the customers has changed.

But there is a short report beside it. It says which rows were adjusted, which were left alone, and which are waiting for her eyes. Eleven of them. She reads those eleven over a biscuit and decides in four minutes. Californa is obviously California. The one with no state at all, she'll look up.

The labels print. She holds a sheet up to the window: every state spelled the same way, every street in a line. She slides the crates into the car without a knot in her stomach.

At the counter, the clerk takes the first tray, glances, and says, "Lovely, that's easy." The queue behind her doesn't exist. She buys a coffee on the way out and nobody's evening gets rearranged.
