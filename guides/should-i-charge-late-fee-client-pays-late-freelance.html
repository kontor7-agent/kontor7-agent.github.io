Charge a Late Fee to a Client? The Chairlift Question

2026-10-27

The chairlift lifts you off the ground with a small jolt, and your phone buzzes in your jacket pocket.

Jonas, a freelance web developer, is on his first proper day off in eight months. Skis on his feet, a friend beside him, the valley dropping away below. He pulls the phone out with a gloved thumb. It's not the client. It's his banking app, with the same empty list as yesterday.

Invoice 0412 is twenty-three days overdue. Nothing has arrived.

He puts the phone away. He looks at the view. He thinks about the invoice anyway.

The question under the question

Half-way up the mountain, a thought starts to repeat itself: Should I charge a late fee?

His payment terms say fourteen days. Somewhere in his old contract template there is a sentence about interest on late payments. He wrote it once, years ago, and never looked at it again.

Charging it feels enormous. It feels like sending a bill for being annoyed.

The client has been friendly. They always say "great work!" and "let's do more next quarter." A fee would turn a warm relationship into a transaction, wouldn't it? What if they take offence? What if the next project goes to someone who never asks for anything?

So Jonas does what many people who work for themselves do. He decides the fee isn't worth the risk. He decides to be "flexible." He decides, quietly, to absorb it.

It doesn't feel like a decision. It feels like politeness. But there is a cost.

What the quiet absorbing costs

At the top station, his friend orders hot chocolate. Jonas stands in the sun and does the maths without wanting to.

The rent is due on the first. The hosting bills come on the fifth. And a sum he has already earned, already delivered, already invoiced, is sitting in someone else's account, being useful to someone else.

He isn't angry exactly. It's more like a low hum. A tight spot behind the ribs that comes back whenever he stops moving.

He thinks of the email he'd have to write. He has drafted it in his head six times since Tuesday. Too soft, and it reads like an apology. Too firm, and it reads like a threat. Mentioning a fee feels like crossing a line he can't uncross.

So he does nothing. And the nothing has a price too: the day off isn't really a day off. It's a day of carrying a number around the mountain.

The sentence that doesn't fit

On the next ride up, his friend, who runs a small bike workshop, asks what's wrong.

Jonas explains it badly. A client, a late payment, a fee he doesn't dare to ask for.

His friend shrugs. "Do you tell the client the price before you start work?"

"Yes."

"And the payment date?"

"It's on the invoice."

"So a date is just a date. Why does saying it out loud feel like a fight?"

Jonas has no answer. He looks down at the tracks in the snow, hundreds of small lines, each one going somewhere on purpose.

It isn't really about the fee. A fee is just one possible step. What he's missing is a way of doing this, something decided in advance, in a calm hour, so that he isn't inventing his tone from scratch at 22:40 with a knot in his stomach. A first friendly nudge on day one after the due date. A clear second note. A third that mentions the terms already agreed, whether he ever applies a fee or not. Each one already written. Each one polite and plain.

If the words were ready, the decision about a fee might stop being a verdict on the whole relationship. It would just be one line in a process, applied evenly, to everybody.

That thought is small, but it loosens something.

A different Monday

Picture the same invoice, a few months later.

It's Monday, 9:10, coffee still too hot. Jonas opens one overview. It shows him which invoices are open, which are overdue, and, next to each one, what the next step is. No guessing. No staring at the ceiling.

Invoice 0519 turned overdue on Friday. The tracker says: gentle reminder today. He opens the matching email, changes the project name, and reads it once. It sounds like him on a good day. Warm, clear, not sorry.

He sends it. It takes ninety seconds.

Then he closes the tab and starts the work he actually likes.

When payment lands on Wednesday, it isn't a victory. It's just the normal end of a normal process. Nobody was embarrassed. Nobody was begging. A date was a date.

And if a client one day needs a firmer reminder, the words are ready, and so is the timeline. Whether he mentions a late fee is then a calm, considered choice about his own terms, made when he's rested, not a panic reaction on a chairlift. (What your contract and local rules allow is worth checking with a professional. That part is yours to decide.)
