Asked for an Item You Don't Have? The Empty Tea Tin

2026-10-20

It is Thursday, a little after twelve, and the rain is running down the shop window in long lines.

There is one customer inside. She is holding a gift bag with a ribbon already tied on it. She smiles and says, "It's for my mother's birthday. She only drinks your chamomile and lemon balm blend. Two tins, please."

"Of course," you say. "One moment."

You say it the way you always say it. Light and sure. Inside, a small voice is already asking: Did I reorder that?

The walk to the back

The shop is small, so the walk to the stockroom takes five steps. Today it feels longer.

You open the cupboard. The tin is where it always sits, third shelf, left side. You lift it, and it weighs nothing. A few dry petals rattle around the bottom.

You check the shelf behind it. Nothing. You check the box under the table, just in case. Nothing.

You stand there for a second with an empty tin in your hand, listening to the rain on the skylight.

The worst part isn't the missing tea. It's that you didn't know. You would have sworn you had enough. Somewhere in your head there was a picture of a full shelf. That picture was three weeks old.

What you say at the counter

You walk back out. You keep your face calm, because that is part of the job.

"I'm so sorry," you say. "I'm out of that one. I can have it in a few days, if that works for you."

She is kind about it. That almost makes it worse. "Oh, that's all right," she says. "I'll just look around."

She buys a small jar of honey and a tin of something else. She says thank you, and the bell above the door rings softly behind her.

You know she won't be back for the chamomile. The birthday is on Saturday. By the time the delivery arrives, the moment will have passed.

The small cost nobody counts

It isn't a disaster. You tell yourself that while you wipe down a counter that is already clean.

But you also know what it really is. It is one sale gone, and probably a little trust with it. It is the quiet feeling of being the owner who should have known.

And it isn't the first time. There was the ginger blend in spring. There was the afternoon you promised a café owner a big order and then had to ring her back. You keep a mental list of these moments, and you never say it out loud: I run a shop, and I don't know what's on my own shelves.

So you do what you always do. You write "chamomile!!" on a sticky note and press it to the edge of the till. By tomorrow it will be one of five sticky notes. By Monday it will be curling at the corners.

The part where it stops being a guess

That evening you sit with a cup of tea you did not sell and look at the orders you placed this year. The numbers are in your head, in a notebook, in an old spreadsheet with too many tabs, and in your supplier's emails.

The thought arrives quietly: It doesn't have to be a memory exercise.

You don't need a warehouse system. You don't need software with a monthly fee and a hundred buttons. You need one calm place where every item has a number, and where that number changes when something comes in or goes out. You also need the shop to tell you, before the customer does, when a shelf is getting low.

That is all. A shelf that speaks up first.

An ordinary Thursday, afterwards

Picture the same Thursday, a few months on.

The rain is still on the window. A customer comes in with a gift bag and asks for two tins of the chamomile blend.

"Of course," you say. This time the sentence has no small voice behind it. You already know there are six tins in the back, and you know the next order is due in ten days. You didn't check the shelf this morning because you didn't need to.

You walk to the stockroom anyway, because that's where the tins live. You come back with two, wrap them, and tie the ribbon.

The sticky notes are gone from the till. On Monday morning you open one page, glance at what is running low, and send a single message to your supplier. It takes ten minutes. Then you put the kettle on for yourself.
