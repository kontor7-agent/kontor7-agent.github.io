Hairdresser Forgot to Charge for an Extra? The Toner Pot

2026-11-02

Monday, 9:15. The shutters are still half down and the radiator is ticking itself awake.

You are standing at the back counter with a mug of tea and the paper appointment book open next to the card terminal slip. Saturday's page. You are doing the thing you do every Monday: matching what the book says with what the till says.

Mrs. Albrecht, 14:00. Cut and colour. Rung up as cut and colour.

But in the margin, in your own scrawl, three letters: ton. A toner. Twenty minutes of her hair sitting under the lamp, a pot of the good stuff, a rinse you didn't hurry.

You never charged for it.

You check twice. Not on the slip. Not in the cash envelope. Nowhere.

The small sinking feeling

It isn't a disaster. You know that. It's one toner.

But your stomach does its small drop anyway, because you know how it happened. She was chatting about her daughter's wedding. You said "let me just freshen that up" and reached for the pot without thinking. Then the card machine was out of paper, the next client was already hanging up her coat, and the moment to add it passed quietly by.

Now you're holding a very ordinary question that feels strangely heavy: do you text her?

You type Hi Mrs. Albrecht, quick note about Saturday… and stop. It feels like asking for a favour. It feels like admitting you're not quite a proper business. Delete.

You type Don't worry about it, I'll let it go. Also delete, because you did that last month with the olaplex add-on, and the month before with the beard trim you did as a favour for a regular.

You don't really know how many of these there are. That's the part that sits with you.

What a small leak does

Nobody talks about this one. Not the big stuff, not the client who vanishes owing you four hundred. Just the little extras. The toner. The ten extra minutes on a fringe. The deep-conditioning mask you offered because her hair looked tired.

Each one is generous. Each one is also quietly yours to pay for, in product, in time, in the chair you're renting whether she tips or not.

And because you do the sums from memory on Sunday nights, the numbers never quite agree. Cash here, card there, a tip that may or may not have been in the jar. You tell yourself it evens out. Some weeks you believe it.

What you feel isn't really about the toner. It's the sense that you're working hard and still guessing, and that a real salon, a real business, wouldn't have to guess.

The pause at the counter

The first client of the day is at 10:00. You have forty-five minutes.

You put down the tea and, instead of fixing Saturday in your head, you do something slightly different. You write the toner down as its own line. Date, client, service, what it would have been. Just that. Not a confession, not a complaint, a line.

It takes thirty seconds, and something shifts. It's no longer a vague worry floating around the room. It's a fact on a page, and a fact is something you can decide about.

You notice you can think clearly now. Maybe you'll message her kindly and keep it light. Maybe you'll let this one go as a thank-you for being a regular, but on purpose, not by accident. Either way, you're the one choosing. That's the whole difference.

And you think: what if every Monday looked like this? Not a hunt through the margins, but the week simply laid out in front of you. Cash and card side by side. The no-show on Thursday sitting in its own column with a price next to it. A quick glance at which services actually earn their chair time and which ones you've been giving away.

A different Monday

Picture it. Same counter, same radiator, same tea.

You open one sheet. Saturday is already there, because on Saturday at the end of the day you spent five minutes entering what happened. The toner has its own tick. The card total and the cash total sit next to what you expected, and they agree, or they don't, and if they don't, you see exactly where.

The no-show from Thursday has a number beside it. Not to make you feel bad, just so you know. Just so that when you decide how to handle the next one, you're deciding with your eyes open.

By 9:30 you're done. You sweep the floor. You line up the brushes. When the door opens at ten, you greet your first client with your head clear and your hands free.

That's the whole picture. Not a bigger salon. Not a new brand. Just fewer small leaks, and the quiet feeling of running a business that knows where it stands.
