Music Students Cancel Late and Don't Pay? The Guitar Shop

2026-10-16

You are standing at the till of a music shop, holding two sets of nylon strings and a tin of rosin you don't really need.

The phone buzzes in your coat pocket. You know before you look.

"So sorry, work ran over. Can we skip today? Pay you double next time!"

The lesson is in forty minutes. You have already turned down an offer of a cover shift to keep this slot free. The kind of free that is now just an empty room with a keyboard in it.

The cashier says, "Anything else?" You say no, a bit too brightly.

The sentence you type and delete

Outside, on the pavement, you start a reply. No problem at all! You delete it. You type, Just a reminder that my policy is... and delete that too, because what is your policy, exactly? You wrote it once, in a message to one student, somewhere in a thread you could never find again.

So you send a thumbs-up. A thumbs-up that costs you the price of a lesson.

Let's call this teacher Mara. She is made up, but you may know her. She teaches guitar and violin to adults: a nurse who practises after night shifts, a retired surveyor, a software tester who wants to play at his sister's wedding. She likes every one of them. That is part of the problem.

"Pay you double next time"

Walking to the tram, she tries to remember who owes her what.

The nurse paid in cash in February, or was that January? The surveyor always transfers, but she hasn't checked the account since the weekend. The tester said he'd pay for the whole block, and then there was the week she was ill, and then the week he was away, and now it's one of those things you can't ask about without a tight feeling in your chest.

She doesn't think of herself as someone who chases. She thinks of herself as someone who teaches. Asking for money feels like turning a warm room cold.

So the numbers live in her head, her notes app, a paper diary with coffee on page 14, and a banking app she opens with one eye half-closed.

What it quietly costs

Nobody warns you that this kind of teaching has two jobs inside it. One is the music. The other is the bookkeeping of small things: who cancelled, who rescheduled, who paid, who "will definitely transfer Friday."

The second job doesn't feel like a job. It feels like a mood. A faint hum under the good lessons. You finish a lovely hour with someone who finally nailed the bridge of a song, and on the way home you're quietly thinking, wait, did she pay for last month?

And the cancellations. Each one is small, and each one is reasonable. People have work, colds, deadlines. But when you add them up in your head, you get a fog, not a number. You suspect it's a lot. You'd rather not know. So you don't raise your rate, because how could you, when you can't even say what the current one is actually earning you?

That's the part that stings. Not the empty hour. The not-knowing.

The Sunday it clicked

The turning point isn't dramatic. It's a Sunday afternoon with the diary open on the kitchen table and a pot of tea going cold.

Mara decides to count, just once, properly. Lessons taught. Lessons paid. Lessons cancelled with less than a day's notice. It takes her far too long, because the information is in five places, and she gets cross with herself for letting it get this way.

But then she sees it on one page. Two students are behind by a few lessons each. A couple of people account for most of the late cancellations. The thing she had been carrying as a vague dread turns into something with edges. And things with edges can be handled.

She realises the awkwardness was never about the money. It was about having nothing in front of her to point to. You can't say "I think you might owe me something" with a straight face. You can say, "According to my lesson log, there are three lessons open from March. Shall I send you the details?"

That is a different sentence. It has no apology in it.

An ordinary Thursday, later

Picture the same Thursday, a few months on.

The message comes in: "So sorry, can we skip today?" Mara reads it at the till of the same music shop. She feels a flicker of annoyance, and then it passes, because she knows exactly where this lands. She replies kindly, with a clear line about how short-notice changes work. She doesn't rewrite it four times. She doesn't feel like a villain.

That evening she spends about ten minutes marking the week: who came, who paid, who is a lesson behind. Then she closes the laptop. The hum is gone. The tea is still hot.

When a student pays two lessons late, she knows. When someone has been quietly reliable for a year, she knows that too, and she can be generous on purpose rather than by accident. And when the question of her rate comes round, she doesn't flinch. She has a number to look at, and it is hers.

None of this makes her colder. If anything, she teaches warmer, because she is no longer silently keeping score in the corner of her mind.
