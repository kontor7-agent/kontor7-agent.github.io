Backup Script Deleted Your Files? The Wedding Fair Stall

2026-11-04

The coffee at the wedding fair is terrible, and you are holding a cup of it anyway.

It is 10:20 on a Saturday. Your stand is behind you: two banners, a laptop on a loop of your best photo galleries, a bowl of mints. A couple is walking towards you with that look people get when they are about to ask about packages.

Then your phone buzzes. It is a message to yourself, sent from the laptop's remote tool the night before. You open it out of habit.

A log line. Three words you were not expecting: removed 4,212 files.

The line you read twice

You had written that script in a good mood. It was meant to copy the working folder to the external drive each night. A tidy little job. You had tested it once, on a small folder, and it seemed fine.

Now you stand there, smiling at the couple, while a cold feeling runs from your stomach to your hands.

Removed. Not copied. Not synced. Removed.

You say hello. You hear yourself say the prices are flexible. You do not hear a word of the conversation, because somewhere in your head a second conversation is running: which folder was it? Which drive? Is the original gone, or the copy? Is there a copy?

The couple takes a card and drifts on. You sit down on the folding chair and open the laptop with the screen tilted away from the aisle.

What it quietly costs

The folder is not empty. That is almost worse. It is half full, and half full means you have to work out what is missing before you can know how bad it is.

You scroll. Last spring's wedding is there. The autumn one is not. The invoices folder looks fine, or maybe it does, because you cannot remember how many there were.

Nobody warns you about this part of working alone. It is not only the files. It is the small voice that says: a real business would have done this properly. It is the way your afternoon shrinks to one question. It is the stand you are paying for, and the smile you are keeping up, while your mind is three hundred kilometres away in a folder tree.

And under that, the quieter worry: a client emails next month asking for the full-resolution set from two years ago. What do you say?

The turning point, on a folding chair

You do eventually find most of it. A sync copy here, a cloud folder there, an old drive in the bag you carry everywhere. It takes the rest of the fair and the whole evening, and it is not the weekend you planned.

On Sunday, sitting at the kitchen table with tea, you notice what actually went wrong. It was not that you are careless. It was that the script had one mode: do it. No way to ask it, "What would you do if I let you?" No record of what it did last Tuesday, or the Tuesday before. You had built something that acted first and explained never.

And you had built it alone, late, between jobs, because nobody else was going to.

That is the turning point. Not a clever fix. Just the plain thought: it does not have to work like this.

A backup should be boring. It should show you what it is about to do before it does it. It should leave a note about every run, in plain words, that you can read in ten seconds. It should keep a few older snapshots so that one bad night does not become the only night.

A different Saturday

Picture the same fair, a few months on.

Same terrible coffee. Same bowl of mints. The couple walks up, and this time you hear every word. They ask about the engagement shoot, you tell them what is included, and you mean it.

Your laptop is closed in the bag under the table. Somewhere at home, a drive has quietly taken its nightly snapshot. A small log file sits beside it, one per run, saying what was copied, what was kept and what was cleaned up. You have not opened it in a week. You do not need to.

When you do open it, on a Monday with your first tea, it takes less time than reading a text message. Dates, counts, a calm "finished without errors." You close it and get on with the day.

That is the whole change. Not excitement. Just the absence of the knot.
