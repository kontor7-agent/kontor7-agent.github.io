ChatGPT Wrote Your API Code: Is It Secure? The Dual Brake

2026-11-08

The instructor's right foot hovers over a pedal that isn't his.

It's 8:20 on a Tuesday morning, and Jonas, thirty-four, freelance developer, is learning to drive. He put it off for years. Now he's in a small hatchback on a wet street, hands at ten and two, trying to look like someone who does this often.

The red light ahead gives him a few seconds. His phone buzzes in the cup holder. He glances down. It's the owner of the bike-rental company he built a booking API for, three weeks ago.

"Quick question before our partner call at 11. Our new partner's IT person asks if the API is secure. Can you confirm?"

The light turns green. Jonas does not.

The word "confirm"

"Green," says the instructor, gently.

Jonas drives. But his head is somewhere else, back in a flat with a half-eaten sandwich, three weeks ago, a deadline, and a chat window open on the second monitor.

He had asked ChatGPT for the login route. Then the endpoint that lists bookings. Then the one that updates them. The answers were quick, tidy and confident. He pasted, tweaked, tested the happy path, and it worked. The client was delighted.

Now one word is sitting in his stomach. Confirm.

It isn't that he thinks the code is bad. It may be perfectly fine. That's the uncomfortable part. He doesn't know. He remembers what the code did when he tested it. He can't say what it does when someone tests it badly.

At the next junction he checks his mirror twice, which has nothing to do with traffic.

What the doubt costs

Nobody warns you about this kind of quiet cost.

It isn't a catastrophe. It's the low hum under an ordinary day. The reply you can't write because "yes, it's secure" feels like a promise you can't back up. The "we'll look into it" that sounds unprofessional. The small, cold thought that you're running a business on code you half understand, and everyone else can tell.

And the strange loneliness of it. Solo developers don't have a colleague to turn to and say, "Can you look at this with me?" You have the chat window, the one that wrote the code, and asking it "is this secure?" feels a bit like asking the student to mark their own exam.

Jonas parks, badly, in the lesson's one-minute reversing exercise. The instructor says nothing. He just keeps his foot where it is.

The second set of pedals

"You know why I'm not worried?" the instructor says, nodding at the floor. "The dual controls. You can learn properly because someone has a brake too. You don't have to be perfect. You just can't be the only check."

He means it about driving. Jonas hears it about everything else.

He sits there with the engine ticking down, and something loosens. The problem was never that AI helped write the code. People have always used examples, snippets and borrowed answers. The problem was that nothing and nobody ever stood in the passenger seat. The code went from "it runs" to "it's live" with no second look in between.

And a second look doesn't have to mean an expensive audit, or a weekend lost, or a panicked re-read of everything. It can be a calm list. The same questions, asked in the same order, by something that doesn't get tired or embarrassed. It can check the doors, then the windows, then the places you'd never think to look.

He types a reply with his thumbs, still in the car park:

"Good question, and a fair one. I'll run a proper review and send you a short written summary before 11."

He reads it once. It doesn't sound weak. It sounds like a professional.

An ordinary Monday, after

Picture the same job a few weeks later.

A new client sends the same question. There's no jolt this time. You open one folder. The review is already done, item by item, with notes in plain language: what was checked, what passed, what needed a fix and got one. You send a tidy summary, written like someone who runs a real business.

The partner's IT person replies with a thumbs-up and one sensible follow-up question. You answer it. By lunchtime the whole thing is closed.

You drink your tea while it's still hot.

That's the whole difference. The code might be exactly the same. What changes is that you know, and you can say so without crossing your fingers.
