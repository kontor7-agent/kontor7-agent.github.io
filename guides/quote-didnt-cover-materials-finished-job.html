Quote Didn't Cover Materials? The Last Paving Slab

2026-11-05

The last slab goes down at ten past four.

You tap it level with the rubber mallet, sweep the sand into the joints, and stand up slowly. Your knees remind you how long you've been at this. The garden looks like the photo on the customer's phone. Better, actually, because the sun has come round and the stone is warm.

She comes out with two mugs of tea. Her son has already drawn a chalk spaceship across the new patio.

"It's perfect," she says. "Honestly. What do I owe you?"

You smile. You say, "I'll just check the quote."

And the smile stays on your face, but something underneath it goes quiet.

The number you quoted from memory

You open the message you sent three weeks ago. One line for the job. One figure. You priced it standing in her garden with the tape measure, doing sums in your head while she asked about hedge colours.

You remember thinking, the slabs are not that expensive. You remember thinking, two days, maybe two and a half.

It was four days. And the slabs were the easy part. There was the sub-base, the sharp sand, the cement, the edging, the second skip you hadn't planned for. There was the extra run to the builders' merchant on Wednesday, when you came up short. You paid for all of it. Some of it on the card, some of it from the cash in the van door.

You drink your tea and say, "Lovely, thank you," because what else do you say?

What the glove box knows

Later, in the van, you don't start the engine.

The receipts are in the glove box, in the door pocket, and one is folded inside a sandwich bag on the dashboard. You flatten them on your knee, one by one. You add them up on your phone's calculator, twice, because the first total can't be right.

It is right.

The materials took almost everything. After them, four days of your work is worth a little more than a couple of weekend shifts. You did the job properly, and she'll tell her neighbours. You'll probably get three more calls from this one patio.

And still, driving home, you feel that old knot. It isn't anger at her. She paid what you asked. It's closer to embarrassment. You've done this for years. You're good with your hands. Why does the paperwork always feel like a different, smaller person's job?

Nobody tells you this part of being self-employed: the work can be excellent and the week can still be a quiet loss.

The thing nobody says out loud

You don't mention it at home. You say it went well, which is true.

But the thought sits there through dinner. How many other jobs were like this? You don't actually know. Some paid well. Some, you suspect, barely paid at all. You've just never looked, because looking means finding out.

And there's a second worry, the smaller one. The last two customers. Did the second one pay? You think so. There was a bank notification at some point. Or was that the deposit?

So the real cost isn't only the money that slipped out of this one quote. It's that you've been guessing for a long time, and guessing is tiring.

The moment it changes

A few weeks later, you're on your sister's sofa and she's showing you something on her laptop for her own small business. It's nothing fancy. A sheet with a few columns that fill in as she types.

She enters a job. She types in materials as she goes. The margin appears at the bottom, plain as a clock face. It isn't a clever formula she had to build. It's just there, quietly telling her whether the job is worth taking.

You don't say anything. But you think about that patio. What if the number at the bottom had been in front of you in her garden, before you said the price out loud?

Not a lecture from an accountant. Not a software subscription with forty menus. Just a calm page that says: this is what the stone costs, this is your time, this is what's left.

It doesn't have to be like this. You're allowed to see it before the job, not after.

An ordinary Monday, afterwards

Picture a Monday a few months on.

You're at the kitchen table at 7:30 with a coffee. The van is outside, loaded. You open one page and it shows you the week: two quotes out, one job running, one invoice still waiting on a customer who said "end of the month."

A new enquiry came in on Saturday, a bathroom wall. Last year you'd have done the sum in your head on the drive over. Today you'll measure, note the materials on site, and let the page show you the real margin. If it's thin, you'll say so, calmly, with a figure you trust. Or you'll walk away without feeling guilty.

The receipts from last week are already in. You photographed them at the merchant's counter. The glove box is just a glove box again.

Nothing dramatic has happened. You just know where you stand. The knot in your stomach has loosened, and on Friday you'll close the laptop at six.
