Third Reminder for an Unpaid Invoice? The Dentist Forms

2026-10-25

It is 11:20 on a Thursday morning, and you are in the dentist's waiting room with a clipboard on your knee.

The form asks for your address, your allergies, your emergency contact. You fill in none of it. Your phone is in your other hand, open to a draft email, and the draft has three words in it: Hi Markus, just…

Just what?

Just checking in? Just following up? Just wondering if the invoice you sent seven weeks ago, the one for the brand guidelines you delivered on time and politely, has perhaps slipped through a crack?

This is the third reminder. The first one was friendly. The second one was friendly with a small dent in it. Now you are sitting under a poster of a smiling tooth, trying to find a sentence that is firm but not cold, clear but not angry, and above all not needy.

The sentence you keep deleting

You type: I wanted to gently remind you… Delete.

You type: As per my previous two emails… Delete. That sounds like a lawyer, and you are not a lawyer. You are someone who makes logos and likes it when people smile at them.

You type: Sorry to bother you again! And there it is, the apology. You are apologising for asking to be paid for work you finished. You notice it, and your face goes a little warm, even here among the magazines.

A nurse says a name that is not yours. You put the phone face down on your knee.

What the awkwardness is really made of

It is rarely about the money alone. Underneath the draft there is a small crowd of thoughts.

What if they think I'm pushy? What if they never hire me again? What if there's a reason, and I'm being unkind? What if the first two emails just sat unread?

And under those, quieter: a real business wouldn't have to ask three times.

That last thought is the unfair one. Plenty of real businesses ask three times. But when you work alone, with no accounts department to be the bad cop, every reminder feels like a verdict on you personally. So you postpone it. You tell yourself you'll write it properly tonight, or tomorrow, or after the weekend.

Meanwhile the rent doesn't wait, and a small knot sits behind your ribs every time you open your inbox.

The call that went differently

Picture a different version of this same Thursday.

Same waiting room, same tooth poster. But the phone in your hand shows a short list instead of a blank draft. Three invoices are open. Next to Markus's there is a plain note: day 49, third step. Not a feeling, just a step.

You open a message that is already written. It is polite, it names the invoice number and the amount, it says what happens next and by when. It does not apologise. It does not threaten. It sounds like a person who runs a business and expects to be paid like one.

You change the greeting, check the date, and send it. It takes about ninety seconds.

Then the nurse calls your name, and you realise you are walking in with your shoulders down.

Why it feels so different

Nothing about Markus changed. He may pay tomorrow or in three weeks. What changed is that the decision of what to write was already made, calmly, on a day when you weren't staring at an unpaid bill.

That is most of the secret. The awkwardness lives in the improvising. When the words exist before you need them, and when you can see at a glance which invoice is at which stage, a reminder becomes an ordinary admin task, like renewing a domain. Not a confrontation, just a Thursday.

A normal Monday, afterwards

It is Monday morning, 8:40. You make tea. You spend ten minutes looking at what is open and what is due, send whatever is next in line, and close the tab.

There is no dread on the way to the inbox. A client replies, "So sorry, will pay today!" and you feel something you didn't expect: warmth, not triumph. The relationship is intact. You were clear, so they could be clear back.

By ten you are working on the actual work. The knot behind your ribs has gone somewhere else.
