Signed Contract, No Idea What to Ask? The Lobby Coffee

2026-10-25

The signed PDF came in on Thursday. You printed nothing, saved it to a folder, and felt a small, proud flutter.

Now it is Tuesday, 9:42 am. You are standing in the lobby of a client's office with a paper cup of coffee you don't really want. The meeting is at ten. Your laptop bag is heavy on your shoulder.

And you have just realised something quiet and uncomfortable: you have no idea what to ask.

The contract says what you will deliver and when the invoice is due. It does not say who will answer your emails. Or who approves the work. Or where the logo files live. Or what "a quick round of feedback" means to the person who wrote that sentence.

The sentence you rehearse in the lift

You run through it in your head. "So, tell me a bit about the project."

That is all you have. It is a fine opening line. It is a terrible plan.

Because you know how this goes. The client talks for forty minutes, warmly, enthusiastically, about their vision. You nod. You scribble. You leave feeling good.

Then, on Friday, you realise nobody mentioned who signs off on the final version. You don't have the login to the site. And the "small extra page" they described in passing is now, in their memory, part of the deal.

None of it is anyone's fault. You just never asked.

What it quietly costs

It is rarely dramatic. It is a hundred small leaks.

A week of waiting for access nobody thought to send. An email you write twice because you're not sure who should be copied. A second round of feedback from someone you didn't know was involved. A small knot of dread each time a new message arrives, because you never quite learned the shape of the job.

And underneath all of it, a feeling you don't tell anyone about: that real professionals walk into these meetings with a plan, and you are making it up as you go.

You are not unprofessional. You are just alone, and nobody ever handed you the list.

The meeting, as it goes

A door opens. Someone says your name and smiles. You follow them down a corridor that smells of printer toner and somebody's lunch.

You sit. You open the laptop. The first ten minutes are pleasant and vague, exactly as you feared.

Then a silence falls, the kind where everyone waits for the other person to steer. This is the moment. You can feel it.

You say, "Shall I walk you through how I usually start, so we both know what happens next?"

And you realise that sentence only works if you actually have a how.

The turning point

Imagine, instead, that the how already existed.

Not a lecture, not a thirty-page process document. Just a calm, one-page shape of a first meeting: a few things to confirm, a few things to collect, a few things to agree on before anyone starts work. Written once, sitting in a folder, ready to be opened the way you'd open a recipe you've cooked before.

You wouldn't have to be brilliant in the room. You'd only have to be prepared.

That is the whole shift. The client doesn't need you to improvise charm. They need to feel that you've done this before, and that the next six weeks are in steady hands.

A different Tuesday

Same lobby. Same awful coffee.

But this time you glance at your screen and there is a short agenda waiting. You know roughly how the hour will run. You know the handful of things you'll leave with: who decides, who to contact, what's included, what's not, and where the files and logins will come from.

The meeting feels lighter. The client relaxes too, because someone else is holding the thread. Somewhere in the middle, you say, "Just so we're on the same page, here's what we've agreed," and they nod, and nobody feels policed.

Afterwards, a welcome email goes out in two minutes, because it was already written. The first week starts without the usual scavenger hunt.

By Friday, nothing has surprised you. That is not a small thing. It is the difference between a project you manage and a project that happens to you.

Starting well is a kindness

It is easy to think a first meeting is about charm, or about impressing someone. Mostly it is about clarity. A good start is a gift to both sides: fewer assumptions, fewer awkward emails in week three, fewer evenings spent reconstructing what someone meant.

And it lets you do something freelancers rarely get to do: walk out of a kickoff feeling like the owner of a real business. Because you are one.
