Dreading Tax Season, Receipts Everywhere? Where to Start

2026-10-23

Picture Jonas, a freelance illustrator, standing at the counter of a charity shop on a grey Saturday morning. He's holding a tote bag of old jumpers he finally decided to give away.

The volunteer is friendly. She's checking the bag for anything that needs washing. And she pulls out a small, folded slip of paper from the side pocket.

"Is this something you need?"

It's a receipt. A thermal-paper one, already fading to the colour of weak tea. A sketchbook, a set of brush pens, a train ticket for a client meeting in spring. He can't tell which, because the ink has almost gone.

He says, "Oh, thanks, no, that's fine." Then he takes it back and puts it in his coat pocket, because throwing it away feels wrong in a way he can't explain.

On the walk home, the slip sits in his pocket like a small stone.

The pile you know about

Here is the thing about tax season: it doesn't arrive on one day. It leaks in.

It starts in October, when someone mentions a deadline at a party and your stomach tightens for a second. It shows up when you open the drawer for a stapler and see the envelope you stuffed full in March. It's there when your phone buzzes with a bank notification and you think, I should probably keep track of that.

Jonas knows exactly where his receipts are. Some are in the envelope. Some are in a jacket he wore in June. A few are photos on his phone, somewhere between a screenshot of a recipe and forty pictures of a dog he doesn't own. Some are emails he flagged "to deal with later", and later has a very full inbox by now.

He's not careless. He's a person working alone, with no one to hand the shoebox to.

What he doesn't say out loud, even to his partner, is this: every time he thinks about starting, the size of it makes him close the laptop. So he does something else, something useful and defensible, like answering a client email. And the pile stays where it is, quietly getting bigger.

What the dread actually costs

Nobody puts it on an invoice, but dread costs something.

It costs the Sunday afternoon that never quite relaxes, because there's a low hum underneath it. It costs the small, unfair voice that says a real business would have this sorted. It costs the feeling, when a friend asks how work is going, that "fine" is a slightly dishonest answer.

It costs something in the work, too. You sit down to draw and part of your mind is still in the drawer with the envelope.

Jonas gets home and puts the faded receipt on the kitchen table. He looks at it for a while. Then he does something small. He doesn't open a spreadsheet, and he doesn't make a plan or promise himself a whole weekend in November.

He just asks one question: what if I only had to deal with this one piece of paper, today, and the rest could wait its turn?

The turning point is smaller than you think

That's the whole shift, really.

The pile feels impossible because you're looking at it as one thing. But it isn't one thing. It's a lot of small, ordinary things: a receipt, a date, an amount, a reason. Each of them takes less than a minute when it has a place to go.

What makes it unbearable is having nowhere to put them. No single home, no list of what's still missing, no way to see how far you've come. So every receipt feels like a fresh decision, and decisions are tiring.

A calm system doesn't make the work vanish. It makes the work visible and small. One place where each receipt lands. A short list of what a year-end usually needs, so you're not guessing. A way to see the bar creep forward, which turns out to matter more than you'd expect.

And it doesn't replace anyone who knows the rules. Your tax professional still decides what counts. It just means you walk into that conversation with things in order, instead of an apology and a carrier bag.

A different Monday in October

Now imagine the same kitchen table, a few weeks later.

It's Monday morning, just after nine. The kettle has clicked off. Jonas has a mug, a client call at eleven, and ten spare minutes.

He takes the three receipts that collected over the weekend and adds them to one tidy log. Date, amount, what it was for. Done before the tea is cool. A checklist beside it shows which year-end jobs are finished and which are still waiting, with a few dates in sight so nothing sneaks up on him.

There's no drama, no weekend of sorting, and no sinking feeling when the drawer opens.

At eleven, the call goes well. Afterwards a client asks, casually, "Can you send me your details for the paperwork?" And he just does, without the little flinch he used to feel.

It's an ordinary Monday. That's the point. The relief isn't loud. It's the absence of a hum you'd stopped noticing until it was gone.

The faded receipt? He still has it. It's pinned above his desk now, not as a reminder of failure, but as the moment he stopped treating the pile as a verdict on himself.
