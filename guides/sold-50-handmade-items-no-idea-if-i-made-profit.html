Sold 50 Handmade Items, No Idea If You Made Profit?

2026-10-21

The train to the Saturday craft fair is delayed by eleven minutes, and you are standing on the platform with a crate of ceramic spoon rests at your feet.

You open your banking app, then your shop dashboard. Fifty items sold this month. Fifty. A year ago you would have cried happy tears at that number.

You wait for the happy feeling. It doesn't quite arrive.

Because the thought in your head is small and uncomfortable: Did I actually make anything?

The number that looks like good news

The dashboard says sales are up. The bank balance says something vaguer. Between them sit platform fees, payment fees, postage you paid for a customer who got "free shipping", the packaging you reordered twice, and a clay delivery you can't quite place in the month.

You remember the glaze that cost more than you expected. You remember the half day you spent photographing everything. You remember, with a small wince, the market stall fee.

What you can't remember is whether any of it was counted.

So you do what most makers do. You look at the total, you feel proud for about four seconds, and then you put the phone back in your pocket. Not knowing is easier than finding out you might have worked a whole month for almost nothing.

What it quietly costs

Nobody tells you that not knowing has a price of its own.

It's the way you hesitate when someone asks, "So, is the business doing well?" You say "Yes, busy!" and it is true and not true at the same time.

It's the way you hold back from raising a price, because what if people stop buying? And the way you hold back from not raising it, because what if you're losing money on every spoon rest?

It's the Sunday evening when you say you'll sit down with the numbers, and then rearrange the shelf instead.

It's feeling like a hobbyist with a card reader, rather than someone who runs a small business.

The stall, the notebook, the shoebox

The train finally arrives. You find a seat with the crate between your feet.

At the fair, you set out the spoon rests, the small bowls, the little dishes in the colour everyone keeps asking for. A woman picks up a bowl, turns it over, and asks, "Is this your bestseller?"

You open your mouth to say yes. Then you realise you don't actually know.

It's your most asked-about piece. It's the one you sell most often. But is it the one you earn the most from? Or is it the one that takes the longest to make and costs the most to post, quietly eating the profit from everything else?

You have the market cash in a tin. You have the online orders in another place. You have receipts in a folder, an envelope and, if you're honest, the bottom of your bag.

The bowl sells. That much you know. Whether it pays is a question you have never once answered.

The moment it clicks

At the end of the day, while you wrap the unsold pieces in newspaper, the thought arrives quietly, without drama:

It doesn't have to feel like this.

You don't need to become an accountant. You don't need to love numbers. You just need to be able to look at one product and see, plainly, what it cost you, what the fees took, and what was left.

One product at a time. One clear line each.

That's all. It's not a different business. It's the same business with the lights on.

A different Monday

Picture the Monday after that.

You make a cup of tea. You open one file. Your products sit in a calm list, and next to each one is a number you trust: what's left after materials, fees and postage.

The bowl you thought was a hero turns out to be fine, but not wonderful. The humble spoon rest, which you almost stopped making, is quietly doing the heavy lifting.

So you adjust a price by a little. Not with a knot in your stomach, but the way you'd adjust a shelf: because now you can see.

When a friend asks, "Is the business doing well?" you say, "Yes. Here's what I kept last month." And you mean it.

You close the laptop and the evening is yours. The clay can wait until tomorrow.
