Client Ignoring Overdue Reminders? The Ice Rink Bench

2026-10-16

The ice rink smells of rubber mats and hot chocolate from a machine. It is Wednesday, 17:40, and you are on the cold plastic bench with your phone in your lap.

Your daughter is somewhere in the middle of a wobbling line of seven-year-olds. You wave when she looks over. Then you look down again.

The invoice is 34 days overdue. You have sent three reminders. The first was friendly. The second was friendly with a little more punctuation. The third began with "Just a gentle nudge" and you hated yourself for writing it.

No answer to any of them. Not even "we'll look into it."

The silence is the loudest part

You open the thread and scroll. The client was lovely during the project. Quick replies, thumbs-up emojis, "this is exactly what we wanted." Now there is only the grey line under your last message: Delivered.

So you start doing the thing you always do. You build theories.

Maybe the invoice landed in spam. Maybe accounting is on holiday. Maybe they are quietly unhappy and you missed it. Maybe you are being too pushy. Maybe not pushy enough. Maybe this is what happens to people who aren't "a real business."

On the ice, a small boy falls over and gets up again as if nothing happened. You envy him a little.

What it quietly costs

The amount itself would cover next month's rent for your workspace. But that is not what keeps you on the bench with your jaw tight.

It is the low hum under everything. Every time you open your inbox, a small part of you checks for the payment notification. Every time you say yes to new work, you wonder whether this one will also end up in the silence.

It is the way you now rehearse emails in the shower. The way you write something firm, soften it, delete it, and write "Hope you're well!" in front of a sentence that should simply say please pay.

It is the feeling that chasing money makes you someone who needs to be liked, rather than someone who delivered a good job and sent a fair invoice.

The mum with the thermos

Next to you sits a woman you have nodded at for weeks. She runs a small physiotherapy practice, you know that much. She pours something from a thermos, glances at your face and says, "Bad news?"

You laugh, a bit too fast. "A client who won't answer. Overdue invoice."

She nods without any drama, the way people nod when they know the situation. "Oh, that. I used to rewrite those emails for days. Then I stopped writing them from scratch."

"What do you mean?"

"I just had the next message ready. And a rough idea of when the next one goes out. So it wasn't a mood any more, it was just the next step."

She shrugs and turns back to the ice, because her son is attempting a spin.

You sit with that sentence. It wasn't a mood any more. It was just the next step.

The turning point is small

Nothing dramatic happens. No lightning. You just realise that every reminder you have sent so far was decided in the moment, by whatever version of you was tired, hopeful, annoyed or embarrassed at that hour.

The first one was polite because you were hopeful. The second was stiff because you were irritated. The third was apologetic because you were afraid.

None of them was wrong. But none of them was a plan. And a client who doesn't answer a mood will not answer a fourth mood either.

What if the tone were already decided, calm, clear, and a little firmer each time? What if you could see at a glance which invoices are late, how late, and what the next message is? Then the question wouldn't be "Do I dare?" It would be "Is it Thursday yet?"

You don't need to become tougher. You need to stop improvising.

A different Monday

Picture it a few weeks from now. Monday, 9:15, your coffee is still too hot to drink.

You open one overview. Two invoices are marked as due soon. One is a few days late, and beside it a note says which message goes out today. You open it, change the client's name, read it once, and it sounds like you on a good day: warm, direct, no apology for asking to be paid.

You send it. It takes four minutes.

The invoice that has been quiet for weeks sits in a different column, with a clear next step that doesn't involve rehearsing in the shower. You don't have to decide how you feel about it. You just do what is next.

By 9:30 you are working on the project you actually enjoy. The hum is quieter, because nothing is hanging in the air. Everything has a place and a next move.

And when your phone buzzes, you don't hold your breath. You just look.

Back at the rink

The lesson ends. Your daughter skates over, cheeks red, and announces she did "almost a turn." You tell her that sounds like a very good almost.

On the way to the car you put the phone in your pocket. The invoice is still overdue. The client has still not answered. Nothing is solved yet.

But for the first time in weeks, the knot in your stomach has loosened a notch. You know what you will send tomorrow. And it won't be written in a panic.
