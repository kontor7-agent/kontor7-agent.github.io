Small Invoice Unpaid, Worth Chasing? The Bottle Return

2026-11-05

Thursday, 11:20. Mara stands in the supermarket entrance with two bags of empty bottles, third in line at the return machine.

The man in front of her feeds in bottles one by one. The machine whirs, thinks, spits one back. He tries again. Mara has time, so she does what she always does when she has time. She opens her banking app and looks at the account.

No payment from the café owner. The logo she designed in September. 140 euros.

She has looked at this line maybe fifteen times this month. Each time the same thought arrives, polite and tired: It's only 140 euros. Is it really worth chasing?

The small amount that isn't small

Here is what Mara tells herself. Larger invoices deserve a reminder. This one is small. A reminder for something small feels like making a fuss. The café owner was friendly. She probably forgot. If Mara writes now, it might sound petty, and she would rather be easygoing than be the person who sends an email about 140 euros.

So she does nothing. Which is also a decision, just a quiet one.

What she doesn't say out loud is that 140 euros is not nothing. It is the electricity bill. It is most of a week's groceries. It is the hours she spent in September, adjusting the lettering three times because the owner wanted it "a bit warmer." She did that work properly. It is done, delivered and printed on a shop window somewhere across town.

The machine finally accepts the man's last bottle. He collects his slip and leaves. Mara steps up.

Twenty-five cents at a time

She lifts the first bottle into the slot. Whir. A small tick on the display. A few cents.

Nobody has ever stood at one of these machines and thought, this bottle is too small to count. You feed them in, one after another, and the slip at the end is simply what it is. Nobody feels greedy for collecting it. It is just money that was already yours, waiting to be handed back.

Mara watches the number climb and a thought sneaks in sideways. She would never leave these bottles at home out of embarrassment. But she has left a whole invoice sitting in a café's inbox for seven weeks, because asking felt awkward.

The awkwardness isn't about the amount. It never was. If it were 1,400 euros she would be just as stuck, only louder inside. The problem is that every reminder feels like a small confrontation she has to invent from scratch: what to say, how warm to be, whether it's too early, whether it's too late. The size of the invoice is just the excuse that makes avoiding it feel reasonable.

What the silence costs

The last bottle goes in. The slip prints. Mara folds it into her pocket and, for a moment, thinks about the other cost.

Not the 140 euros. The carrying.

Every time she opens the app, there's that small flinch. Every time she sees the café's Instagram, she thinks of the unpaid line before she thinks of the nice logo. A job she was proud of has quietly turned into a low hum of irritation. She resents the owner a little, which is unfair, because the owner has no idea. Probably.

That is what a "small" unpaid invoice does when you don't deal with it. It doesn't stay small. It takes up room in your head that was meant for the next project.

She stands there in the entrance with her empty bags and sees it plainly. The question was never is it worth chasing? The question is why does chasing feel like a favour I'm asking for?

It shouldn't. A payment reminder isn't an accusation. It's housekeeping. A business that sends one calmly, in plain words, with the date and the amount and no apology, is behaving like a business. The café owner, who runs her own till every day, would understand that better than anyone.

The other kind of Thursday

Imagine the same Thursday, a few weeks from now.

Mara is in the same queue with the same bags. But her account has been checked on Monday, ten minutes, over coffee, the way you glance at a weather forecast. She knew there was one open invoice. She knew it was a few days past due. She knew exactly what the next gentle step was, because it had been decided in advance, when she was calm, and not at the last minute while a machine whirred at her.

The reminder went out on Tuesday. Friendly, short, no drama. By Thursday the money may or may not have arrived. Either way, the knot isn't there. Nothing is waiting in the back of her mind. She feeds in bottles, takes her slip and goes to buy bread.

No heroics and no confrontation. Just a business that keeps track of what it is owed, whether the amount is 140 euros or 4,000.

Before the bag is empty

Mara walks home with the slip in her pocket. She doesn't write the email yet. But she stops telling herself the invoice is too small to mention, and that alone feels like putting down something heavy.
