I Keep Overselling and Disappointing Customers: The Candle Fair

2026-10-13

The card reader beeps. Another sale. You smile, wrap the lavender candle in tissue paper, and hand it over.

Then your stomach drops.

That was the last one. Or was it? You promised two to the woman who messaged yesterday. And one is already in a parcel on the kitchen table. Or is it?

It is 11:20 on a Saturday at the autumn makers' fair, and you are standing behind a table of candles, mentally counting stock you can't actually see.

Let's call you Mara. She is made up, but you might know her.

The apology you can write in your sleep

By Sunday evening the online shop has three new orders. Two are for the lavender candle. Mara opens the box under the bed, the one that holds the "back stock", and finds one.

One candle, two customers.

She already knows the message she will write. She has written it so many times that her fingers move on their own:

Hi! So sorry, there's been a mix-up with my stock. I can refund you straight away, or send it in two weeks once I've made more…

She reads it back. It sounds friendly. It also sounds like someone who doesn't quite have a grip on her own business.

That is the part nobody says out loud. It isn't the refund. It's the small, hot embarrassment of being the person who sold something she didn't have. It's the review she imagines. It's the customer who was excited, and now isn't.

What it quietly costs

Nobody sits down and decides to oversell. It happens in the gaps.

The market table, the online shop and the notebook all hold slightly different versions of the truth. A sale at the fair never quite makes it into the notebook. A restock gets counted in your head and forgotten by Thursday. A bundle uses two of this and one of that, and somehow only one of them gets subtracted.

So you carry the numbers around instead. In the shower. In the supermarket queue. At two in the morning, when you suddenly wonder whether the amber jars arrived.

And it costs more than apologies:

  • You get a little cautious about promoting your best sellers, in case you can't deliver.
  • You reorder late, then in a panic, then too much of the wrong thing.
  • You feel like a hobbyist, even though people are paying you real money.

The shop is growing. The knot in your stomach is growing with it.

The sentence that changed the question

On Monday Mara sits at the kitchen table with a coffee that has gone lukewarm and a pile of sticky notes. A friend who runs a small ceramics studio has stopped by to borrow a glaze bucket.

"Honestly," Mara says, "I don't know how you never run out of things."

Her friend laughs. "I do run out. I just know before it happens."

That is all. A throwaway sentence over a cup of coffee.

I just know before it happens.

Mara realises she has been asking the wrong question. She has been asking, How do I become the kind of person who remembers everything? The better question is, What if I didn't have to remember?

The problem was never that she is careless. It's that her stock lives in her head, in three notebooks and on a sticky note under the fruit bowl, and a head is a poor place to keep a number that changes every hour.

A different Saturday

Picture the same fair, a few weeks on.

The same table. The same autumn light through the tent. The same card reader beeping.

But when the woman asks, "Do you have another of the lavender ones for my sister?", you don't freeze. You know. One place holds the count, and it was updated last night. You say, "Yes, I have three left, and I'll set one aside for you."

On Monday morning you don't wonder. You open one sheet, and it tells you what is running low and what is worth reordering this week, before a customer ever finds out the hard way. Your jars arrive while there are still jars on the shelf.

Nothing dramatic happens. That's the beauty of it. No apology messages. No refund emails. No quiet dread when the shop notification pings.

Just a shelf that no longer surprises you.

And something else, harder to name: you start to feel like the owner of a real shop. You say yes with confidence. You promote your best sellers freely, because you know you can deliver them. You can close the laptop on Sunday and actually be somewhere else.

A small system, not a big overhaul

You don't need a warehouse system or a consultant. You don't need to become someone else.

Most small shops need one honest place where stock lives, one gentle nudge when something is getting low, and a clear view of what is sitting on the shelf as money. That's it. It doesn't have to be clever. It has to be simple enough that you still use it on a tired Saturday.
