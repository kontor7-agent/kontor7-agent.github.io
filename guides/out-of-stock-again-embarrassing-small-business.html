Out of Stock Again, So Embarrassing: The Customer at Your Counter

2026-10-13

It's Wednesday, a quarter past ten. The bell over the door has just rung, and a woman is walking straight toward the back wall of your little yarn shop.

She isn't browsing. She knows exactly what she wants. She holds up her phone and shows you the picture: the dusty-green wool, the one from your own website.

"Two skeins of this, please. It's for my sister's birthday. I've already started the pattern."

You don't need to look. You know. But you walk to the shelf anyway, as if the green might have crept back in overnight. There's a gap there, and a faint dust line where the last basket stood.

The warm face

Your face goes hot. It happens fast, before you can decide how to handle it.

"I'm so sorry," you say. "I'm out of that one."

She is kind about it. That almost makes it worse. "Oh, no worries," she says, but her eyes are already drifting to the other colours, and you can see the small deflation. She had a plan for this morning, and you were part of it.

She leaves with a postcard and a polite smile. The bell rings again.

You stand there with the hum of the fridge in the back room and one thought: again.

This is the third time this season. Last month it was the oatmeal. Before that, the packaging for the gift sets. Each time you told yourself you'd keep a closer eye on things.

What it quietly costs

The lost sale is the smaller part, honestly. Two skeins is a modest number.

The bigger cost is how it feels to be the person who can't be counted on. You love this shop. You chose every colour on that wall. And now someone has walked in with their heart set on something, and you've turned out to be unreliable in a way you can't even explain.

The website still shows the green as available. You forgot to change it, so you'll be apologising by email too, to someone who clicked "buy" last night and has no idea yet.

And there's the other side of it, the part nobody sees. In the back room there's a shelf of a colour you over-ordered in spring, sitting there like a held breath. Money you paid, wool that isn't moving. You're out of the one thing everyone wants and drowning in the thing nobody does.

So you've been running the shop by feel. A glance at the shelf. A rough memory of what sold last week. A notebook with a coffee ring on the cover, half of it in handwriting you can't read anymore.

Mostly it works. Until a woman with a phone and a birthday plan stands in front of an empty gap.

The thing you hadn't thought about

After lunch, with the shop quiet, you pick up the notebook and flip back through it. You're not looking for anything specific.

Then you notice something. The dusty green doesn't just run out. It takes weeks to come back from your dyer. By the time you see the shelf getting thin, it's already too late. The empty gap you stared at this morning was decided weeks ago, on a day when the shelf still looked fine.

You put the pen down.

The problem isn't that you're careless or bad at this. You were looking at the shelf, and the shelf only tells you the truth when it's too late. What you needed was a quiet signal earlier, something that says: this one, this week, before it gets thin.

It doesn't have to be a clever system. It doesn't have to be software with a login and a monthly fee. It just has to be one honest place where what you have, what you sell and what takes a while to come back all sit side by side, so you stop carrying it in your head.

You realise the knot you've been feeling for months wasn't about the green wool. It was about carrying all of it alone, all the time.

A different Wednesday

Picture the same morning, a few weeks on.

You unlock the door and start the kettle. Before the first customer, you spend a few minutes at the counter with a single page. It tells you which colours are getting low and which are sitting too long. Nothing dramatic. No red alarms, no panic. Just a short, clear list of what to order this week.

You send the message to your dyer, close the page and sip your tea.

When the bell rings, a woman comes in with her phone held up. She wants the dusty green.

You smile, because you already know where it is. You walk to the shelf and the basket is there, full. You wrap two skeins in tissue and she says, "Oh, perfect, I was worried."

"It's one of our favourites," you say, and you mean it. Nothing in your chest tightens. You aren't bracing for anything.

That's the whole change. Not a bigger shop or a cleverer plan. The shelf stops surprising you, and so you can stop apologising.
