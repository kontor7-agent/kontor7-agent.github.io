Client Micromanaging You? The Trailhead Car Park, 8:15

2026-11-04

The car park at the trailhead is still half empty. Mara has her boots on, one hand on the boot lid, the other holding her phone.

It is Thursday, 8:15. She booked this day off three weeks ago. She told the client in an email. She even got a thumbs-up.

Now the screen says: Quick check-in? Where are we with the landing page? Can you send a screenshot of progress?

She is a freelance UX writer. The landing page is due Monday. It is nearly done. It will be done on Monday.

She reads the message a second time. Her shoulders have already climbed up toward her ears.

The message that arrives before the question

This is the third check-in since Tuesday. There was one at 9:10 asking if she had seen the earlier one. There was one at 14:30 asking whether she could "just hop on a quick call to align." Last week it was a 7:50 message about a comma.

None of it is rude. That is the trouble. Every message is polite, a little hurried, a little just checking. If you complain, you sound unreasonable.

So you do what many of us do. You answer fast. You send the screenshot. You say "great question!" You tell yourself it is part of good service.

The hiking friends are stretching by the signpost. Mara types a reply with her thumb: Sure, one sec! Then she deletes it. Then she types it again.

What it quietly costs

The check-ins are not the real cost. The real cost is the waiting for them.

You cannot go deep into a sentence when you know your phone might buzz in forty minutes. So you work in short bursts. You keep one eye on the inbox. A two-hour task becomes a four-hour task, and the extra two hours are not on any invoice.

Then there is the other cost, the one you rarely say out loud. You start to feel like staff. Not a professional with a process, but someone who is always "on," who earns trust by being instantly reachable.

You also start to dread the client's name on your screen. And you still want to keep them. They pay. They are not a monster. They are simply anxious, and you have become their way of feeling calm.

Mara knows all of this. What she does not know is how to say it without sounding like she is pushing back, complaining, or about to quit.

Three drafts and a trailhead

She tries a first reply: Hi Anna, I understand you'd like updates, but I really can't respond this often. Too sharp.

Then: Of course, happy to send updates whenever! That is the opposite lie.

Then a long one, with three apologies and a paragraph of explanation. She reads it and sees someone asking permission to have a day off.

She puts the phone in her pocket. Her friend calls from the signpost, "Are you coming?" and she says "Two minutes," and the two minutes become eight.

Here is what she realises, standing next to the car. The problem is not that she lacks the right to a boundary. The problem is that every time it comes up, she has to invent the wording from scratch, while anxious, with someone waiting.

Someone who handles this calmly is not braver than she is. They just do not start from a blank box.

The turn

Suppose a polite, warm message already existed for this exact moment. Suppose it said something like: Here is when you will hear from me and how. Here is when the work will be ready. You don't need to chase, because I'll come to you. Suppose you only had to fill in the names, the days and your own voice, then read it once and press send.

The boundary would not feel like a confrontation. It would feel like a small piece of professionalism, the same as sending an invoice with a clear due date.

And you would not need to say it in a rush, between a car park and a hill.

The same Thursday, afterwards

Picture it differently. The message from the client arrives at 8:15. Mara reads it, feels a flicker, and thinks, I know what I do with this.

She does not reply now. The reply she sent on Tuesday, in her own words, already told the client when the next update would come: Friday at noon, with a link, no call needed. So this check-in is simply not urgent. It can wait for the agreed time.

She locks the car. She walks up through the beech trees with her friends. At the first viewpoint she looks at the valley and nothing in her pocket pulls at her.

On Friday at noon she sends the update, as promised. The client replies with a thumbs-up and a small "thank you, that helps." Perhaps they were never trying to control her. They just wanted to feel in the loop, and nobody had given them a better loop.

On Monday morning, her inbox is not a place she approaches with her shoulders up. It is just an inbox.
