Don't Know How to Use ChatGPT in Business? Tyre Stack

2026-11-03

It's Thursday, 12:40. The shop bell has gone quiet, and you are sitting on a stack of 700c tyres in the back room with a sandwich you haven't unwrapped.

Your phone is propped against a box of inner tubes. On the screen is a white page with a blinking cursor and one line of grey text: Message ChatGPT.

You've opened it three times this week. Each time you typed something like "help me with my business", looked at it, and deleted it.

Let's call you Mara. You repair bikes, you sell a few good second-hand ones, and a part-timer called Joel comes in on Saturdays. Everyone says you should be using AI. Your cousin uses it. A supplier mentioned it. A man on a podcast said it would save you ten hours a week.

You have no idea what to ask it.

The list in your head

The funny part is that you know exactly what is eating your time.

There's the supplier who still hasn't confirmed the brake pads. There's the customer whose e-bike has been waiting for a part for nine days and who deserves a kind, honest message. There's the product text for the three refurbished bikes you want to list before the weekend. There's the helper ad you've been meaning to write since September.

None of it is hard. All of it is slow. Every one of those tasks starts with the same small wince: how do I even begin this email?

So you tried. Once, you typed "write a product description for a bike" and got four paragraphs about "unleashing the joy of cycling". It sounded like a brochure for somebody else's shop. You closed the tab and told yourself it wasn't for people like you.

But it wasn't really that. You had asked a vague question, and you had got a vague answer back.

What it quietly costs

Nobody sees this part of running a small shop.

It's the Sunday afternoon you spend with the laptop on your knees, writing replies you could have written on Tuesday. It's the slight dread when the inbox shows a number in bold. It's the feeling that everyone else has a secret shortcut and you are the only one still doing it by hand.

It's also the polite messages that go out late, or sound a bit curt because you wrote them tired. You care about your customers. Your messages should sound like you do at the counter, and at 9 pm they often don't.

And there's a cost you don't write down anywhere: the quiet belief that you are behind. Not on the work, which you're good at, but on the business of it. The part with the emails and the texts and the procedures.

The sentence with gaps in it

Joel comes in early on Saturday because he forgot his keys the night before. He finds you at the workbench, phone in one hand, a half-written note to a supplier in the other.

"You know it works better if you just give it the blanks filled in," he says. He isn't boasting. He's looking for his keys.

You don't answer. But the sentence stays with you through two punctures and a gear adjustment.

That evening you try again, differently. You don't ask it to "help with your business". You tell it who you are writing to, what happened, what you want to say, and how you'd say it across the counter. You hit send half expecting nonsense.

What comes back is a message that sounds like a calm version of you. Not perfect. You change two words and take out one sentence. But it takes four minutes instead of forty, and it doesn't make your stomach tighten.

Something shifts a little. The tool was never the problem. You just hadn't been handed a first line to start from.

An ordinary Monday, afterwards

Picture the same shop on a Monday in a few weeks' time.

The shutter goes up at nine. The kettle is on. The inbox has six messages, and you don't sigh at any of them. Each one is a small, familiar kind of task, and you know the starting line for each. You fill in a few details, read what comes back, make it sound like you, and send it.

The delayed e-bike customer gets a clear, warm update before they have to ask. The supplier gets a short note with the order number in it. The three refurbished bikes get honest descriptions with the right measurements.

By ten you're at the bench with grease on your thumb and your phone face-down. The admin is done, and it didn't eat your lunch.

Nothing dramatic has happened. You haven't been replaced by a robot, and you haven't turned into a "business owner who hustles". You're still the person who fixes the bike. You've just stopped dreading the blank box.
