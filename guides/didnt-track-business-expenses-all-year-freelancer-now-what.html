Didn't Track Business Expenses All Year? The Hill Walk

2026-11-01

The wind on the ridge is cold enough to make your eyes water. You stop at the stone wall to let the others catch up.

You take your phone out to check the time. Instead your thumb opens the banking app, as if it had a mind of its own.

There it is: eight months of card payments in one long scroll. Software, a train to a client, a lens you needed, a coffee with a prospect, something from an online shop whose name tells you nothing.

You never tracked any of it. You meant to, in January. You even made a folder.

The folder is empty.

The thought you carried up the hill

Mira is a freelance illustrator. She is made up, but you may know her. She walks this ridge every November with her sister and her sister's dog. For three years she has spent the second half of the walk somewhere else in her head.

Her sister points out a hawk. Mira says "oh, lovely" and doesn't see it.

She is thinking: I didn't track anything this year. Is it too late? Is everything gone? Am I the only person who does this?

She isn't. But nobody says so out loud. Freelancers talk about rates and difficult clients. Almost nobody says, "I have no idea what I spent on my own business."

So the knot sits in your stomach and gets a little tighter each time the year-end emails start.

What the silence costs

The cost isn't dramatic. It is small and steady.

It is the Sunday afternoon that never fully relaxes. It is the quick lurch when someone says "tax" or "accountant" in the pub. It is the quiet feeling that other people run a real business and you are winging it.

It is also the not-knowing. You can't say whether the year was good, because you can't see what went out against what came in.

And there is the money you suspect you're leaving on the table. Things you paid for that belong to your work, and that you may never find again in time. You don't know. That is the worst part.

The pub, and the question

At the pub afterwards the dog sleeps under the table and the fire is too hot. Mira's sister asks, kindly, "You're quiet. Everything okay?"

Mira nearly says "fine." Instead she says, "I didn't keep track of a single expense this year. Not one receipt. I think I'm just going to avoid it until January and panic."

Her sister laughs, not unkindly. "So it's November. You have two months, not zero."

Mira realises she has been treating behind as if it meant finished. It doesn't. November with a messy year is a very different place from the 29th of January with the same mess.

The turning point is small

It isn't a grand plan. It is one sentence she hadn't allowed herself before: you don't have to fix the whole year at once. You only have to give it a place to land.

A place for the receipts that do exist: the paper ones in the coat pocket, the emails with PDFs, the bank lines you can place. A list of what needs doing before the year closes. A date or two you can see.

That is all. Not perfect books and not a lost spring rebuilt. Just one calm, orderly spot where the pile stops being a pile.

She doesn't need to feel guilty about the empty folder. She needs somewhere to put the first thing.

A different Monday

Picture the Monday after that walk.

Mira makes tea and opens one file. It isn't a mountain. It is a plain sheet, and she adds the first three receipts while the kettle clicks off. A little bar at the top moves a notch.

That's it. Ten minutes. She closes the laptop and goes to her actual work: a commission she's excited about, a colour palette she's been turning over for days.

By Friday the shoebox is a tidy list. By early December she can see roughly what's covered and what's still missing. The missing part doesn't frighten her, because she can name it.

When the year-end deadlines come near, they are dates on a list, not a weather system. And when the question "what did I spend on the business?" comes up, she answers it without a flinch.

The relief isn't that everything is perfect. It's that nothing is hidden anymore.
