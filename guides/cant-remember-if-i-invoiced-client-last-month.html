Did I Invoice Last Month's Work? The Bowling Alley Shoes

2026-10-20

The rented shoes are a size too big, and you are holding a plastic cup of lemonade you didn't want.

It's Saturday, 15:20, at the bowling alley. Your nephew is turning eight. Somewhere behind you a ball thunders down lane six and a dozen children cheer as if it were a goal.

Your phone buzzes in your back pocket. You almost ignore it. Then you see the name: Katrin, your biggest client.

Hi! Quick one: did September's invoice go out? Finance is closing the month on Monday.

Your stomach drops, just a little.

Did it? You remember finishing the work. You remember the last round of changes, a Thursday, rain on the window. You remember thinking I'll send the invoice tomorrow.

You do not remember tomorrow.

The Search Through Your Own Head

You step away from the lanes, past the shoe counter and the arcade machine blinking its tired lights, and you stand by a poster about birthday packages.

You try to reconstruct it like a detective with a bad memory.

There was a week you were ill. There was the trip to your parents'. There was the invoice for the other client, which you definitely sent, because you remember the typo in the subject line. But was that the same day? Or the week after?

You open your email on the small screen. You type her name into the search bar. A dozen threads come up. Some are about the project, some are old quotes, and one is a thank-you note from June that makes you smile for half a second.

No clear sign of an invoice. But that doesn't mean much. Maybe you sent it from another account. Maybe it's in a folder called "Done" or "Admin" or "Misc." Maybe it's a draft, still sitting there, polite and unsent.

Behind you someone shouts, "Strike!" and the whole party erupts.

You stand there with your thumb hovering over the reply field. Saying "Yes, it's on its way!" feels risky. Saying "Let me check" feels like admitting you don't know your own business.

And under that is the quieter thought, the one nobody says out loud: what kind of professional can't remember whether she got paid for a month of work?

What It Quietly Costs

The answer, of course, is almost any of us who work alone. There is no back office. There is you, a laptop, and a memory that is also trying to hold birthdays, deadlines and what's for dinner.

But the cost is real, even if no one invoices you for it.

It's the Saturday afternoon half-spent in your head instead of at the party. It's the small flinch every time a client writes "quick question." It's the way you start to avoid looking at the numbers, because looking might confirm something you'd rather not know.

And sometimes it's money. Not dramatic money, just the slow kind. An invoice that went out two weeks late. A month you never billed at all and only noticed in the spring.

You type: Let me double-check and get right back to you. It's honest. It's also a little embarrassing.

You send it. Then you put the phone away and watch your nephew throw a ball into the gutter with enormous joy, and you cheer along, a beat too late.

The Moment It Clicked

That evening, on the sofa, you do the digging anyway. Sent folder. Bank app. A half-remembered PDF in Downloads called something like invoice_final_v2.

It turns out you did send it. Eleven days ago. You find it at last, and the relief is strange, because it comes with a flash of irritation. Twenty minutes of searching, an afternoon of low-grade dread, all to learn something you should have been able to see in two seconds.

The problem was never that you forgot. The problem was that the answer lived in five places, and one of them was your head.

You sit with that for a moment. What if the answer lived in one place? What if you could open a single sheet and see: this client, this month, these hours, invoice sent, due on this date, not yet paid?

No detective work. No guessing. Just a plain yes or no, quietly waiting for you.

A Different Saturday

Picture the same bowling alley. Same too-big shoes, same lemonade.

The phone buzzes. Katrin's name. Did September's invoice go out?

You don't flinch. You open one file, look for ten seconds, and reply: Yes, sent on the 9th, due on the 23rd. I'll forward it again so it's on top of your inbox.

Then you put the phone back in your pocket and go watch your nephew take his turn.

That's the whole difference. Not a cleverer memory. Not more discipline. Just somewhere to look, so that Monday morning feels like a clear desk and not a small trapdoor.

And a weekly ten-minute glance is usually enough to keep it that way: who's been billed, who hasn't, what's due, what's late. Nothing to dread, because nothing is hiding.
