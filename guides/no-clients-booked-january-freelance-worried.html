No Clients Booked for January? The Clementine Crate

2026-11-03

Saturday, 10:20. The market square smells of roasted chestnuts and wet cardboard.

You're holding a clementine in each hand, weighing them like it matters. The stallholder says something about the cold snap. You nod. You didn't hear a word.

Because in your head, a calendar is open. December is full: two deadlines, one workshop, a client who wants everything "before the break". And then, on the page after that, January.

White. Nothing in it. Not a booking, not a maybe, not even a polite "let's talk in the new year."

Let's call her Ines. She's an illustrator, six years on her own. This is an illustrative scene, but if you work for yourself, you may know the clementines.

The quiet maths at the fruit stall

Ines pays for the fruit and walks on. The worry follows her past the cheese van and the flower buckets.

She knows the thoughts by heart. Was last January empty too? I think so. Did it recover? It did, eventually. How long did it take? Longer than I'd like.

That's the trouble with an empty month. It isn't only the money you're not earning. It's the not knowing. Is this a dip or a drought? Is it the season, or is it you?

She doesn't say this to friends. They'd say "Oh, it'll pick up!" in a kind voice, and she'd say "Yes, probably," and the knot would stay exactly where it is.

The worry nobody mentions is the small, private one: a real business would have this sorted.

What the knot is actually made of

On the bench by the fountain, Ines peels a clementine and tries to be honest with herself.

The knot isn't made of January. January is just a month. It's made of fog.

She doesn't know what she needs to bring in each quarter. She doesn't know how many weeks she can realistically take off without panicking. She doesn't know which enquiries are warm and which went cold in October. All of it lives in her head, in three email threads, and in a notebook with a coffee ring on the cover.

So when January looks empty, her mind fills it with the worst version. There's nothing to hold it against. No picture of the year, so one blank month looks like the whole picture.

She eats a segment. It's sharper than she expected.

The sentence that changed the angle

That afternoon, Ines is wiping down the kitchen counter when a thought turns up, quietly, the way good ones do.

What if I just wrote the year down?

Not a grand strategy. Not a vision board. Just the plain shape of 2027: what she'd like to earn, how many working weeks she actually has, where the time off goes, which conversations are in the pipeline. In one place, where she can look at it.

She doesn't need it to be exciting. She needs it to be visible.

Because an empty January is frightening when it floats alone. Set inside a whole year, with a planned week off, two projects she's already talked about, and a spring that looks reasonably full, it becomes something else. A gap you can see. A gap you can plan around. Maybe even one you can use, for the portfolio update you've been postponing, or for the quiet outreach emails that never fit into busy months.

Nothing about the calendar has changed. But the dread has a size now. And a size is much easier to carry than a fog.

A normal Monday in January

Fast-forward, in the gentle way imagined scenes allow.

It's the second Monday of January, 9:05. The radiator ticks. Ines has a mug of tea and, open beside it, a single page that shows the year so far: what she hoped to bring in by now, what actually arrived, who she's spoken to, and the two weeks in April she already marked as hers.

January is still quiet. She's not going to pretend otherwise.

But she isn't refreshing her inbox every four minutes. She sends three short, warm emails to people she'd genuinely like to work with again. She spends an hour on the portfolio. She goes for a walk at lunch, and it's an actual walk, not a walk where she's rehearsing disaster.

When a friend asks, "How's the new year starting?" she can say, "Slow, but I know where I stand," and mean it.

That's the difference. Not a full diary. A calm one.

Back at the market

Sometimes the most useful thing isn't more clients by next week. It's being able to look at your year and say: this is what I'm aiming for, this is where the quiet months fall, this is when I rest. Seeing it takes a lot of the sting out.
