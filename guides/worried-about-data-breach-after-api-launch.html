Worried About a Data Breach After Launch? The Bike Hut

2026-11-06

The bike hire hut smells of rubber and warm sun cream. The man behind the counter is adjusting a saddle and asking if you want a helmet in medium.

You say yes. You are looking at your phone.

It is Saturday, 10:20 am. This weekend was supposed to be off. A friend booked it months ago: two days along the river, a picnic, a pub with a garden. You said yes in March, back when the API launch was a date on a calendar and not a live thing with real people's names in it.

It went live on Thursday. The client's booking app now talks to it all day. It has been working beautifully, and that is almost the problem. Something that works beautifully for strangers is something strangers can poke at.

The thought that rides along

You have not opened your laptop. You have opened the hosting dashboard twice, though, on the phone, with your thumb covering half the graph. You have watched the request count tick up. You have wondered who is making those requests.

Most of them are surely customers. Surely.

But the thought that rides along on the back of every pleasant moment is the same one. What if someone is reading data they shouldn't, and I find out from the client on Monday?

You did the things you knew to do. You added login. You checked the obvious bits. You even remember a good feeling around Wednesday night, when you pushed the last fix. But "I think I covered it" and "I looked at everything on purpose" are different sentences, and your stomach knows the difference even if your calendar doesn't.

The picnic that tastes of nothing

By noon you are on a quiet path by the water. Your friend is talking about a cheese she found. You are nodding in the right places.

A small, ugly arithmetic runs under the conversation. A client who trusted you with their customers. A name on the invoice that says you. No team to hand this to, no security person down the corridor. If something is wrong, it is wrong on your desk, and only yours.

There is a cost here that nobody puts on a quote. It is the weekend you are physically present for and mentally not. It is the shoulders that don't drop. It is the quiet voice that says real developers would know, which is not true, but which sounds very reasonable at 3 pm on a bench.

Your friend asks, kindly, "Is it the work thing?"

You say yes. It feels strange to say it out loud. She says, "Can you do something about it, or only worry about it?"

The question underneath

You sit with that on the ride back.

Can you do something, or only worry about it?

The honest answer is that you have been doing the second one because the first one has no shape. "Check the security" is not a task. It is a fog. You don't know where it starts, so you don't start, so you worry instead, and worry feels like effort even though nothing gets done.

What you actually want is not reassurance. You don't want someone to say "it's probably fine." You want a list with an end. A way of walking through the thing once, on purpose, and ticking it off, so that on the day somebody asks "did you check this?" you can answer with something better than a hopeful face.

The Monday that feels different

Imagine the same weekend, with one change. Before the launch, you had gone through the API with a proper list. Not a lecture, just a list: what to look at, what to try, what to write down. Some items came back fine. A couple made you wince and then fix something, calmly, on a Tuesday afternoon with a cup of tea.

And you had a page you could send the client, plain and professional, saying what was reviewed and what was found.

The bike hut would feel different. The phone would stay in the pocket. The picnic would taste of cheese.

On Monday you would sit down at nine and open the dashboard like someone looking at their own work, not like someone checking a smoke alarm. You would still care. You would still keep an eye on things. But the knot would be a loose thread, not a rope.

It doesn't make you invincible. No list does, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. It makes you someone who looked, on purpose, and wrote it down. That is a different person to be on a Sunday evening.

Handing the helmet back

Back at the hut, the man takes the bikes and asks if you enjoyed it. You say you did, and you are surprised to find it is half true.

On the train home you don't open the dashboard. You open a note instead and type one line: Monday: go through it properly, with a list.

That is all. It is a small sentence, but it has an end to it, and it loosens something behind your ribs.
